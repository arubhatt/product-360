## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. Open innovation (Henry Chesbrough, 2003)
- Summary: Firms can and should use external ideas as well as internal ones, and internal and external paths to market. Set out in Chesbrough's book Open Innovation: The New Imperative for Creating and Profiting from Technology (Harvard Business School Press, 2003). [S]
- Two directions: inbound (bring in outside knowledge by licensing patents, buying technology, partnering with startups or universities) and outbound (take unused internal inventions out through licensing, joint ventures or spin-outs). A third "coupled" mode (inbound and outbound together, as in alliances and co-development) is usually credited to Oliver Gassmann and Ellen Enkel (2004). [S, coupled mode not verified this session]
- Known risks: revealing information, losing advantage through disclosed IP, weaker control, and the difficulty of absorbing outside ideas. [S]
- Cautionary origin story Chesbrough drew on: Xerox PARC invented much of the modern desktop but Xerox captured little of it; the Star sold about 25,000 units and staff and spin-outs (Metaphor, 1982) took ideas elsewhere. Xerox donated PARC to SRI International in 2023. [S]
- B2B/platform application: Treat the mechanism list in this chapter as two pipes. Inbound: venture client pilots, design partners, university labs, licensing in, acquisitions. Outbound: licensing out, open-sourcing a connector spec, spinning out a non-core product. A platform PM's main outbound lever is often an open interface (for example an MCP server or public API) that lets partners build on the platform.

### 2. Three Horizons and Lean Innovation Management (Baghai, Coley and White; Steve Blank)
- Three Horizons comes from Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley and David White, The Alchemy of Growth (1999, McKinsey authors). [S, the McKinsey "Enduring ideas" page timed out]
- Steve Blank's Lean version (June 2015) [V]: Horizon 1 executes a known business model (KPIs, stage-gate product management). Horizon 2 extends existing businesses with partly known models. Horizon 3 searches for unknown, possibly disruptive models with small teams (under five people) that talk to 100+ customers in about 10 weeks and build iterative MVPs.
- Blank's organizing rules [V]: separate H3 teams with their own plans, incentives and KPIs; embed legal, finance and procurement so teams can get to "yes"; reward H1 managers for supporting H2 and H3; graduate successful projects by absorption, spin-out or standalone unit; keep successful innovators to start the next cycle instead of folding them into operations.
- "Innovation theater" (Blank) [V]: impressive projects and press releases with no change in product direction. Use this term for the chapter's main failure mode.
- B2B/platform application: The mechanism selector should first ask which horizon the bet sits in. Hackathons and design partners mostly feed H1 and H2; incubators, CVC and acquisitions are H2 and H3 tools.

### 3. The ambidextrous organization (Charles O'Reilly and Michael Tushman, HBR, April 2004)
- Article verified by authors and date. [V] The body was paywalled. The widely taught claim (from memory, [S]): breakthrough units do best when structurally separate from the core business but integrated at the senior team level with a shared strategic intent. Blank's 2015 post builds on this idea.
- Application: Decide where an incubated Beacon bet reports, who funds it and how it later merges back into the core roadmap.

### 4. Innovation ambition matrix (Bansi Nagji and Geoff Tuff, HBR, May 2012)
- Authors verified [V]. Body paywalled. Widely taught content [S, unverified]: a matrix of "where to play" (existing to new markets) against "how to win" (existing to new products and assets) giving core, adjacent and transformational innovation. The often-quoted 70/20/10 allocation and the claim that transformational bets return a disproportionate share must NOT be stated as fact without the article. See flags.
- Application: A portfolio lens for the mechanism selector: each mechanism tends to produce one band (hackathons core and adjacent; CVC and acquisitions often adjacent and transformational).

### 5. Build, borrow or buy: the resource pathways framework (Laurence Capron and Will Mitchell)
- Sources: Finding the Right Path (HBR, July to August 2010, summary verified [V]) and the book Build, Borrow, or Buy (Harvard Business Review Press, 2012) [S].
- Verified premise [V]: new strategies need new resources and capabilities; firms cannot build everything; licensing, alliances and M&A are legitimate paths.
- Decision sequence as commonly taught (from memory, [S], verify against the book before quoting): (1) Are our internal resources relevant (close enough in knowledge and organization fit)? If yes, build. (2) If not, are the target resources tradable, that is, can a contract or licence define and protect them? If yes, borrow by contract. (3) If not, how close must we be to the partner (how much joint work, how aligned are goals)? If a bounded collaboration suffices, borrow by alliance. (4) Only if deep integration is needed and feasible, buy. Their warning: firms default to their habitual path (the "implementation trap").
- Application: This is the spine of the chapter's build-partner-buy analysis artifact. In 2026 add two variables: model-provider dependency (inference cost, switching costs) and the talent-only path (licence-and-hire).

### 6. Acquisitions that leverage versus reinvent the business model (Clayton Christensen, Richard Alton, Curtis Rising and Andrew Waldeck, HBR, March 2011)
- Verified [V]: the article states that study after study puts M&A failure "somewhere between 70% and 90%."
- Framework as commonly taught (from memory, [S]): judge a deal by how it affects the acquirer's business model (value proposition, resources, processes, profit formula). Deals that leverage the existing model can be integrated; deals that reinvent it should be kept separate so the acquirer does not destroy what it bought.
- Application: The acquisition opportunity brief asks "does this target extend Beacon's model or change it?" and sets integration depth to match.

### 7. Venture client model (Gregor Gimmy; BMW Group Startup Garage)
- Definition [S]: a company that buys, uses and adopts a startup's solution for strategic benefit, with no equity stake. Contrast with corporate venture capital, which takes equity.
- BMW Startup Garage (founded 2015; calls itself "the world's first and leading venture client unit") [V]. Process: Discover, Assess (use-case relevance, prototype maturity, team, funding, legal readiness, live demos), Purchase (supplier number and first purchase order), Proof of concept, Long-term partnership. Self-reported: 6,000+ startups assessed, 280+ piloted, over half won paid follow-on projects, 38+ became suppliers, 4 to 8 weeks from first contact to evaluation.
- Application: For a B2B PM the venture client move is the fastest "partner" path: buy a pilot, test against a real workflow, decide in weeks. Pairs with procurement and security review (Sam persona).

### 8. Corporate venture capital (CVC)
- Definition [S]: corporate funds taking equity in external startups. Objectives are strategic (technology and market access, acquisition pipeline) or financial (returns); surveys show most CVC units mix both, screening for strategic fit first. Examples: GV, Intel Capital, Salesforce Ventures. US 2024 context: of 1,147 venture exits, 1,083 were acquisitions and 42 were IPOs (PitchBook-NVCA via Wikipedia) [S]. That matters because acquisition is the usual exit, so CVC often doubles as an M&A scouting channel.
- Application: Most PMs do not run a CVC fund. Their job is to give the fund a thesis (which problems matter to the roadmap) and to run the pilot when a portfolio company is a fit.

### 9. Intrapreneurship (Gifford Pinchot III and Elizabeth Pinchot, 1978)
- Coined in a 1978 white paper "Intra-Corporate Entrepreneurship"; Pinchot (1985) described intrapreneurs as "dreamers who do." [S] Classic cases: Lockheed Skunk Works (1943), 3M and Google employee-chosen project time. [S]
- Google's Area 120 (March 2016) is the cautionary modern case: an internal incubator meant to graduate projects back into Google; moved under Google Labs in November 2021; significantly reduced in the January 2023 layoffs. Some graduates moved into products (Aloud into YouTube auto dubbing in 2023; Tables and Chatbase into Google Cloud). [S]
- Application: Intrapreneur programs work when there is a named receiving organization and graduation criteria from day one.

### 10. Hackathon planning kit (Alexander Nolte, James Herbsleb, Kiev Gama, Abasi-amefon Affia-Jomants; arXiv, 2020, revised 2025) [V]
- Exact template: 12 decisions, each with its question.
  1. Goal: what do you want to achieve?
  2. Theme: what is the overall theme?
  3. Competition or cooperation: compete for prizes or work together?
  4. Stakeholder involvement: how do externals take part?
  5. Participant recruitment: who should come?
  6. Specialized preparation: what will teams need to take part?
  7. Duration and breaks: how long and how intense?
  8. Ideation: when and how do teams develop ideas?
  9. Team formation: how do like-minded people find each other?
  10. Agenda: what happens during the event?
  11. Mentoring: how will you support participants?
  12. Continuity planning: what happens after it ends?
- Guidance: set goals about four months ahead; manage tensions (organizational vs personal goals, illustrating a concept vs producing something useful, challenging vs achievable, speed vs quality). Continuity "does not come for free": plan it before the event and embed the event in a wider strategy. Outcomes worth continuing include extending existing products, reusing technology, community and learning, not only startups.
- Evidence of product impact (vendor's own page) [V]: Atlassian's 24-hour ShipIt event; Jira Service Desk began when five employees built a simpler portal for raising Jira issues.
- Application: This is the backbone of the hackathon plan artifact. For AI hackathons add: sandbox data and connectors, an eval set, a security pre-approval, and a judging rule that rewards measured results over demos.

### 11. Building effective agents (Erik Schluntz and Barry Zhang, Anthropic, December 19, 2024) [V]
- Workflows: code fixes the sequence of model and tool calls. Agents: the model directs its own steps and tool use. Start with the simplest solution (often one well-grounded model call); add complexity "only when it demonstrably improves outcomes."
- Application: Agent prototypes from hackathons should state which pattern they use and show an eval that justifies anything beyond a workflow.

### 12. Idea challenges and prize competitions
- Netflix Prize [S]: $1M; launched October 2, 2006; won September 2009 by BellKor's Pragmatic Chaos, 10.06% better than Cinematch. Widely reported that Netflix never deployed the full winning ensemble because of engineering cost; the Netflix tech blog post that says so returned 403 this session. Treat as [S] and flag.
- Application: Idea challenges are cheap to run and good at surfacing problems and talent; they fail when no budget or owner exists for winners (same continuity problem as hackathons).

### 13. Innovation training at scale
- 2026 example [V]: Anthropic's Claude Frontier Academy (October 2, 2026): $100M backing, goal of 10,000 "Frontier Deployed Engineers" by end of 2027; a multi-day program plus a 12-week residency in which engineers lead a real Claude project at their own organization. First cohort partners include Accenture, Bain, Capgemini, Deloitte, McKinsey, Morgan Stanley, Novo Nordisk. Rationale: people who can make AI work in a real business are "the hardest talent to find."
- Application: Training that ends in a shipped project at the learner's own company is a stronger design than classroom-only training. This is a useful pattern, not a claim of outcomes (none published yet).

### 14. Licensing and spin-outs
- Licensing as a business model [S]: Arm designs processor IP and licenses it rather than making chips, earning licence fees plus royalties, with tiers up to an architectural licence.
- Spin-outs: Byteboard spun out of Area 120 in October 2021 [S]; Metaphor out of PARC in 1982 [S]. Blank lists spin-out as one of three graduation routes [V].

## (d) 2025 to 2026 AI-era developments

- Licence-and-hire ("reverse acqui-hire") became the standard AI talent deal. Pattern: hire founders and key staff, pay a licence fee that lets investors exit, leave a shell company running. Cases: Microsoft and Inflection (March 2024, $650M), Google and Character.ai (August 2024), Meta and Scale AI (June 2025, 49% non-voting stake, about $14.8B), Google and Windsurf (July 2025, about $2.4B) followed within days by Cognition acquiring the rest of Windsurf (July 14, 2025). Lesson for PMs: the "buy" path now splits into buy the company, buy the team, or license the tech, and each leaves different customers stranded or served. Windsurf shows the remaining business can still be bought by someone else. [V for Cognition; S for the others]
- Regulators watch these structures. UK CMA treated Microsoft and Inflection as a merger situation (cleared). The FTC studied cloud and model-provider partnerships (2024 orders, January 2025 report) and flagged switching costs, exclusivity and access to sensitive data. For a PM partnering with a model provider, these same issues are commercial risks to put in the build-partner-buy analysis. [V for FTC]
- Partner over build, at least for now: MIT NANDA (via Fortune, August 2025) reports vendor and partnership approaches succeed roughly twice as often as internal builds. Contested methodology; use as a prompt for thought, not a law. [S]
- MCP (November 2024) made "partner" cheaper: connectors to customer systems and partner tools are now a standard interface, which lowers the cost of co-development and of hackathon prototypes that touch real systems. [V]
- Agent prototypes: Anthropic's 2024 guidance (start with workflows, add agency only with evidence) gives hackathon judges a rule for separating demos from real candidates. [V]
- Talent and training: Claude Frontier Academy (October 2026) treats training as residency on a real project, a model for "innovation training" in the AI era. Model providers also run forward-deployed engineering for enterprise customers; the Academy names the role "Frontier Deployed Engineer." [V]
- AI hackathons: no verified 2025 to 2026 study of AI hackathon outcomes was found this session. Teach the format from the Nolte kit plus the agent guidance. [flag]

## (e) Beacon worked-example ideas (consistent with beacon-case.md)

Facts used as given: Northlight Systems (about 2,400 employees, public); Beacon ARR about $48M, 38% growth, about 420 enterprise customers, NRR 118%; gross margin fell from 81% to 72% after agentic features because of inference cost; personas Priya, Dana, Marcus, Sam, Leo; team of 3 PMs, 35 engineers, 2 applied AI engineers; strategic tension of Observe data advantage versus tool-agnostic reach; storylines: Beacon Agents launch at Northlight Live, per-resolved-incident pricing, the hallucinated root cause, Beacon Classic sunset, EU AI Act documentation.

1. Mechanism selector run on four candidate bets from the Beacon backlog:
   - MSP multi-tenant agent console for Leo: design partner program (5 to 8 MSP partners), because the workflow and billing model are unknown and partners own the data.
   - Change-risk scoring before deploys: venture client pilot with a startup, because a startup may already solve it and the question is fit, not invention.
   - Remediation safety evals (after the hallucinated root cause): university partnership plus internal build, because it is a hard research problem and credibility matters to Sam and EU customers.
   - Runbook-to-agent conversion: AI hackathon, because feasibility is the open question and many engineers hold runbook knowledge.
2. Hackathon plan ("Beacon Agent Days", two days) using the 12 decisions: goal = three runbook agents that pass an eval on sandbox incidents; theme = supervised remediation; cooperation over competition; stakeholders = Priya and two customer SREs as judges, Sam pre-approves sandbox access; specialized preparation = sandbox environment with MCP connectors and a replay of anonymized past incidents; judging rule = measured diagnosis acceptance and safe action rate on the replay, not slide quality; continuity = each winner gets a named PM owner and a one-sprint follow-on slot, decided before the event.
3. Design partner program plan for the MSP console: selection criteria (multi-tenant scale, willingness to share incident data, executive sponsor), commitments both ways (weekly feedback, early access, pricing protection), exit rules, and a guard against building one partner's custom features (a feature must serve at least three partners).
4. Build-partner-buy analysis for the diagnosis model layer, the margin story: build (fine-tune smaller models on Northlight Observe data to cut inference cost, uses the data advantage), partner (deeper commercial agreement with a model provider, watch switching costs and exclusivity terms, the issues the FTC flagged), buy (acquire a small team with inference-optimization skills). Walk through Capron and Mitchell's questions. Likely answer: partner now with a portable abstraction layer, build selectively where Observe data gives an edge.
5. Acquisition opportunity brief: a fictional startup (name to be invented by the chapter author, for example "Kestrel", and flagged as fictional) building Kubernetes remediation agents with strong evals. Brief covers strategic fit, leverage vs reinvent the business model (Christensen et al.), options (venture client pilot first, minority investment, licence-and-hire, full acquisition), customer impact of each option (the Windsurf pattern), integration risk, regulatory and diligence questions, and the trigger conditions to proceed. Note: beacon-case.md does not define any acquisition target, so any new entity must be clearly fictional and minor.
6. Tie-ins: Beacon itself is an incubation story (AIOps feature in Observe, 2023; spun out as a standalone product, 2024), which can illustrate an internal incubation that graduated. Leo's MSPs are a natural co-development channel.
