## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. The innovation placemat (one-page innovation plan)
- Origin: the chapter scope maps to a university certificate course that asks learners to build an "innovation placemat". Do not copy that course's template. Describe it generically as a one-page innovation plan that a sponsor can read in five minutes and sign.
- Book's own format (proposed, original to the Handbook). Nine boxes on one landscape page:
  1. Strategic intent: which corporate goal this serves, quoted from the company strategy, and the one-sentence innovation thesis.
  2. Where we will play: the target cell on the ambition matrix (core, adjacent, transformational) and the innovation type(s) from Ten Types.
  3. Customer and job: who, which problem, what evidence we already hold.
  4. Bets and hypotheses: the two to four assumptions that must be true, ranked by risk.
  5. Goals: one or two outcome goals in SMART form plus leading indicators, with an explicit "learning goal" for the early stage.
  6. Resources and horizon: people, money, time box, and the portfolio bucket it draws from.
  7. Executive champion: named person, what they own, decision rights, cadence.
  8. Risks and mitigations: top five from the risk register, each with an owner and a kill or pivot trigger.
  9. Milestones and stage gates: evidence needed to earn the next tranche of funding.
- How a B2B or platform PM uses it: one placemat per bet, reviewed quarterly in the portfolio forum. The AI harness drafts it from the strategy doc, discovery notes and the risk register via connectors; the PM edits and owns it. The placemat is a contract between the team and the sponsor, not a slide.

### 2. Ten Types of Innovation (Doblin; Keeley, Walters, Pikkel and Quinn, 2013)
- Derived from analysis of more than 2,000 successful innovations [V, Wiley page]. Ten types in three categories [S, standard published list; verify names against the book]:
  - Configuration: Profit Model, Network, Structure, Process.
  - Offering: Product Performance, Product System.
  - Experience: Service, Channel, Brand, Customer Engagement.
- Core claim [S]: most firms over-invest in product performance; innovations combining several types are harder to copy and tend to earn more.
- B2B and platform application: platform PMs often find the strongest moves in Profit Model (outcome-based pricing), Network (partner and connector ecosystems) and Product System (integrations, extensibility), not in features. Use the ten types as a prompt checklist: for each bet, ask the harness to propose one move per type and then test which combinations reinforce each other.

### 3. Sustaining versus disruptive innovation (Christensen)
- Origin: Bower and Christensen, HBR 1995; The Innovator's Dilemma, 1997; renamed "disruptive innovation" in The Innovator's Solution, 2003 (with Raynor) [S, Wikipedia].
- Definitions [V, Christensen Institute]: sustaining innovation is incremental or breakthrough improvement aimed at demanding customers, usually at higher price and margin. Disruptive innovation starts with simple, cheap applications at the bottom of a market and moves upmarket until it displaces incumbents. Two footholds: low-end (a low-cost model takes the least profitable customers) and new-market (a simpler, cheaper or more accessible offer creates customers who were not served).
- Key correction from Christensen, Raynor and McDonald (HBR 2015) [S on detail]: disruption is a process, not an event; a product that is better and more expensive (their Uber example) is not disruptive in the theory's sense.
- Limits: Lepore (2014) attacked case selection and over-extension; King and Baatartogtokh (2015) found that few cases fit all four elements of the theory [S on the count]. Teach it as a lens for asking "which customers are we overserving, and who could serve them cheaper", not as a prediction engine.
- B2B application: for an AI ops vendor, the low-end threat is a cheap agent that does 70% of diagnosis for the mid-market that incumbents overserve; the new-market threat is teams that never bought AIOps at all (small platform teams) now getting it bundled in a cloud console.

### 4. Core, adjacent, transformational and the Innovation Ambition Matrix (Nagji and Tuff, HBR May 2012)
- Authors at Monitor Group [V]. Content below from prior knowledge [S]; HBR body is paywalled:
  - Matrix axes: horizontal "where to play" (serve existing customers and markets, adjacent ones, or new ones); vertical "how to win" (use existing products and assets, add incremental ones, or develop new ones).
  - Core: optimize existing products for existing customers. Adjacent: expand from existing business into "new to the company" business. Transformational: develop breakthroughs and invent things for markets that do not yet exist.
  - Reported allocation: high performers put about 70% of innovation resources into core, 20% into adjacent and 10% into transformational, and the return ratio was roughly the inverse (10, 20, 70).
  - Caveat in the article: the right ratio varies by industry and competitive position; the authors cite technology firms putting less in core and more in transformational than consumer goods firms.
- B2B application: score every roadmap bet into one of the nine cells, sum spend by band, compare with the target mix agreed with the sponsor. The interesting conversation is the gap, not the number.
- Flag: treat 70-20-10 as a starting hypothesis from one consulting study, not a law.

### 5. Three horizons (Baghai, Coley and White, The Alchemy of Growth, 1999; popularized by McKinsey)
- [S, primary not reachable this session] Horizon 1: extend and defend the core businesses that deliver most current profit. Horizon 2: build emerging businesses that are gaining momentum and need investment. Horizon 3: create options on future businesses (research, pilots, minority stakes).
- Caveats to teach:
  - Horizons are about business maturity and the kind of management needed, not calendar time. A Horizon 3 option can mature in a year.
  - Steve Blank (HBR, February 2019) argued that disruption now arrives fast enough that companies must run all three horizons at once with different processes [V on author, date and title; argument detail S].
  - Separate horizons need different metrics: H1 on profit and efficiency, H2 on revenue growth and customer adoption, H3 on learning milestones and option value.
- Relationship to the ambition matrix: horizons describe time-to-maturity and management mode; the ambition matrix describes distance from the core. Most H3 work is transformational, but not all.

### 6. Aligning innovation strategy to corporate strategy
- An innovation strategy answers four questions: which corporate goals it serves, where we will innovate (the matrix cells and the types), how much we will spend in each band, and how we will govern and kill bets.
- Govindarajan and Trimble (HBR 2010): dedicated innovation teams inevitably clash with the rest of the organization [V, summary]; their remedy pairs a dedicated team with the "performance engine" in a deliberate partnership [S].
- O'Reilly and Tushman (HBR 2004) ambidextrous organization: separate exploratory units, integrated at the senior team [S on findings].
- Day (HBR 2007): minor innovations average 85% to 90% of development portfolios and the share of major innovations fell from 20.4% (1990) to 11.5% (2004) [V]. Use as historical evidence that portfolios drift to the core unless someone protects the bigger bets.
- B2B application: write the innovation thesis as "Because the company is pursuing [corporate goal], we will innovate in [cells/types] for [customers], measured by [outcome], funded at [mix]." If the sentence cannot cite a corporate goal, the bet needs a sponsor outside the strategy or should stop.

### 7. Choosing where to innovate
- Inputs: ambition matrix gaps, Ten Types scan, disruption lens (where are we overserving), customer jobs evidence, and the company's right to win.
- Day's Real-Win-Worth It screen (HBR 2007) [S on detail]: Is the market real and is the product real? Can we win, and can the product be competitive and can we win as a company? Is it worth doing: will it be profitable at acceptable risk, and does it fit strategy?
- B2B and platform specifics: favor bets where proprietary data, installed base, distribution through partners, or ecosystem position give an advantage that a model vendor cannot copy.

### 8. Executive sponsorship (the executive champion)
- Prosci benchmark [V, vendor research]: active and visible sponsorship ranked as the top contributor to change success in every benchmark since 1998; projects with extremely effective sponsors met or exceeded objectives nearly three times as often as those with very ineffective ones; half of respondents said sponsors did not adequately understand their role; building a coalition was the hardest sponsor task. ABCs: Active and visible participation, Building a coalition, Communicating directly.
- Kotter (HBR 1995) guiding coalition step [S].
- Executive champion brief (Handbook artifact, proposed format, one page):
  - The ask: decision, money, people, air cover, and by when.
  - Why it matters to the sponsor's own goals (tie to the metric they are measured on).
  - What the sponsor will do: three named behaviors (for example, open the quarterly review, remove a named blocker, attend two customer calls).
  - What the sponsor will not do: no solution design, no scope additions without a trade.
  - Decision rights and escalation path.
  - Cadence: monthly 30 minutes, quarterly gate review.
  - Exit and kill criteria the sponsor pre-agrees to.
- Practical points: pick a sponsor whose P&L benefits if the bet wins; avoid sponsors who only lend a name; agree kill criteria while everyone is still optimistic.

### 9. SMART goals and their limits
- Doran, Management Review, November 1981, 70(11), 35 to 36. Original letters: Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic, Time-related [S, Wikipedia citing Doran]. Common later versions: Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound.
- Doran himself noted not every objective can be quantified, especially at middle-management level [S].
- Limits for innovation:
  - Ordóñez, Schweitzer, Galinsky and Bazerman (HBS 2009) [V]: goal setting has systematic side effects: narrow focus that neglects non-goal areas, more unethical behavior, distorted risk preferences, corrosion of culture, reduced intrinsic motivation. They propose treating goals like prescription medication with a warning label.
  - Early-stage bets cannot honestly set "Realistic" revenue targets. Use learning goals and evidence milestones (McGrath and MacMillan's discovery-driven planning, HBR 1995: plan to learn, convert assumptions into knowledge at checkpoints) [V on framing, S on mechanics].
  - Evidence on SMART itself is mixed and context dependent (Bjerke and Renger 2017; Swann et al. 2020 and 2022) [S].
- Handbook template: pair each SMART outcome goal with (1) a leading indicator, (2) a guardrail metric, (3) a learning goal for the current stage.
  - Example format: "By [date], [segment] will [behavior/outcome] from [baseline] to [target], measured by [source]; guardrail: [metric must not fall below X]; this stage's learning goal: [assumption] confirmed or refuted with [evidence]."

### 10. Innovation risk and mitigation
- Risk categories for the register: desirability (customers do not want it), viability (economics fail, including inference cost), feasibility (tech, data, model quality), adoption and change (users, partners, channel), organizational (sponsor loss, cannibalization fights with the core), regulatory and trust (AI regulation, security review, data residency), and ecosystem or platform dependency (model vendor or connector changes).
- Mitigation patterns: staged funding tied to evidence; reversible pilots; design partners under contract; guardrail metrics; pre-agreed kill criteria; parallel bets; for AI, evals, human approval gates, and NIST AI RMF functions Govern, Map, Measure, Manage [V].
- Innovation risk register (Handbook artifact, columns): ID; risk statement ("If [cause], then [effect] on [goal]"); category; likelihood (1 to 5); impact (1 to 5); score; leading signal to watch; mitigation; owner; kill or pivot trigger; status; date last reviewed.
- Discovery-driven planning link: the riskiest assumptions on the placemat are the top rows of the register.

### 11. Innovation ambition matrix (Handbook artifact)
- A three by three grid using Nagji and Tuff's axes. Each bet is plotted as a dot sized by spend. Totals per band shown against the target mix. A column for "horizon" and for "Ten Types used" sits beside the grid in the list view.

## (d) 2025 to 2026 AI-era developments
- Adoption is near universal but value is concentrated. Stanford AI Index 2026 reports 88% organizational adoption; BCG (September 2025) finds only 5% of companies are generating value at scale and 60% little or none. Implication for the chapter: AI is no longer an innovation in itself; the innovation question is which business model, workflow or ecosystem change AI enables.
- Falling inference cost changes what is viable. More than 280-fold fall for GPT-3.5-level capability (Nov 2022 to Oct 2024, AI Index 2025). Ideas that failed the viability test in 2023 deserve a re-screen; but frontier agentic workloads can still compress margins (Beacon's 81% to 72% margin drop).
- Agents move from demo to portfolio line. BCG says agents were about 17% of AI value in 2025, projected 29% by 2028. AI Index 2026 shows agent benchmark success near human level on OSWorld but still failing about one in three structured tasks. Treat agentic bets as adjacent or transformational with explicit reliability risks in the register.
- Gartner (June 2025, [S]) predicts over 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027. Use as a reason for staged funding and kill criteria, not as a fact about any single company.
- Interoperability standards lower the cost of ecosystem (Network and Product System) innovation: MCP (November 2024) and its move to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation (December 2025). For platform PMs, connectors are now a Ten Types "Network" move.
- AI changes the innovation process itself: the harness can draft placemats from strategy docs, run a Ten Types scan, generate risk registers from past post-mortems, synthesize discovery evidence, and red-team assumptions. Rule for the chapter: the AI drafts, the PM and sponsor decide; every AI-drafted claim on a placemat carries its source.
- Governance: NIST AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage) and its Generative AI Profile give a shared vocabulary for the AI rows of an innovation risk register; RMF under revision in 2026.
- Disruption lens on AI: model vendors and cloud consoles bundling "good enough" agents are a classic low-end and new-market threat to specialist B2B tools. Ask which customers you overserve.
