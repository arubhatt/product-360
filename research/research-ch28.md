- [V] Atlassian Team Playbook, "DACI: a decision-making framework" (accessed 2026-10-30). https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci DACI = Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed. Driver gathers stakeholders and information, sets scope and gets a decision by the agreed date. Approver is one person (the page stresses one) who actively decides. Contributors advise: voice, no vote. Informed are told the outcome: no voice, no vote. Play: prep the doc, assign Driver, assign Approver and Contributors, assign Informed, action plan, gather input and decide, organize action items, share outcome; record the decision. Template fields: title "DACI: [question we're trying to answer]?", details (status, impact, people, due date, outcome), background (1 to 2 sentences), relevant data, decision factors (cost, time, scalability), options considered (table with pros, cons, estimated resources and cost), action items, outcome. Timing: 15 min prep, 60 min run, 3 to 6 people. Variations listed: RACI and force-field analysis. The page does not name an originator (see flags).
- [V] Marty Cagan, "Product Ops Overview", SVPG, 20 Dec 2021. https://www.svpg.com/product-ops-overview/ Cagan favors a "force multiplier" model: product ops sits beside product management and design and helps PMs, designers and sometimes product marketers do better work. Three pillars he credits largely to Melissa Perri's Escaping the Build Trap: quantitative insights, qualitative insights, tools and best-practices evangelism. Should: self-service data tools and one source of truth for KPIs without doing the analysis for teams; research coaching and infrastructure for ongoing customer testing without doing the research; onboard and train PMs and designers; share practices with judgment; help coordinate quarterly planning while product leaders stay in charge; be staffed by senior people. Should not: take over PM coaching or strategy; make PM decisions; become a Trojan horse for a command-and-control PMO; split the PM role into an operational job ("two in a box"); be staffed by juniors. Warning: with the wrong people, process grows until "process becomes the thing."

- [V] Bain & Company, "RAPID: A Tool to Clarify Decision Accountability", 13 Oct 2023. https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/ Recommend (one recommender who leads, gathers input, proposes), Agree (sparing veto for feasibility and mandatory requirements like legal; D resolves R vs A deadlock), Perform (executes), Input (expertise; consulted, not deciding), Decide (one decider ideally; commits the organization). - [V] Jeff Bezos, 2015 Letter to Shareholders, Amazon (published April 2016). https://s2.q4cdn.com/299287126/files/doc_financials/annual/2015-Letter-to-Shareholders.PDF Type 1 decisions are consequential and irreversible or nearly so (one-way doors) and need slow, careful deliberation. Most decisions are Type 2: changeable, reversible, two-way doors; they "can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." Warning: as organizations grow they tend to apply the heavyweight Type 1 process to Type 2 decisions, causing slowness, risk aversion, too little experimentation and less invention. Use: product ops calibrates decision process weight to door type.

- [V] Anthropic Engineering (Mikaela Grace, Jeremy Hadfield, Rodrigo Olivares, Jiri De Jonghe), "Demystifying evals for AI agents", 9 Jan 2026. https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents Vocabulary: task, trial, grader, transcript (trace), outcome (end state of environment), eval harness. ## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. Product operations as a function (Melissa Perri and Denise Tilles)
- Source: Melissa Perri and Denise Tilles, Product Operations: How Successful Companies Build Better Products at Scale, 2023 (book site and author page verified; publisher [M]: believed to be Holloway, check). Earlier groundwork: Perri, Escaping the Build Trap (O'Reilly, 2018) [M], and Perri's 2019 blog.
- Summary: product ops is the function that helps product management scale by supplying the inputs PMs and leaders need to set strategy, prioritize and run the work. Perri's shortest framing: it removes obstacles to evidence-based decisions.
- Three pillars (names as on the author's own book page): (1) Data and Insights: one trusted source of business and product metrics, connecting financials (revenue, retention, cost) to what ships. (2) Customer and Market Insights: a standing flow of research, feedback, win-loss and market intelligence routed to the teams that need it. (3) Process and Governance: the operating model, planning calendar, rituals, templates, decision rights and the tools that make them run. Chapter 00 of this book already names pillar 1 "business and data insights"; reconcile wording (see flags).
- Cagan's restatement (SVPG, Dec 2021): "force multiplier" model with quantitative insights, qualitative insights, and tools and best-practices evangelism. Same shape, different labels; both credit Perri.
- B2B application: in an enterprise company, pillar 1 is where product ops earns trust with Finance (ARR, NRR, gross margin by product); pillar 2 aggregates signals from sales, CS, support and partners into one feedback system rather than per-PM spreadsheets; pillar 3 aligns product's calendar to the company's fiscal calendar, board cycle and annual customer event.

### 2. Guardrails on scope (Marty Cagan)
- Product ops enables, it does not decide. It provides self-service data and a KPI source of truth but does not do teams' analysis; builds research infrastructure but does not run the research; coordinates quarterly planning while product leaders own it; onboards and trains PMs.
- Anti-patterns: product ops as a disguised PMO, as process police ("process becomes the thing"), or as a second PM in a "two in a box" split. Staff it with senior people.
- Chapter use: a "should / should not" table in the charter.

### 3. When to create the function (synthesis; no verified threshold)
- Perri: growth stage and beyond; warning signs are unreliable or siloed data, stale research, undefined cross-functional processes, no structured feedback loop, status meetings without decisions, leaders pulled into ad hoc reporting.
- Pendo (vendor, [S]): roughly 5 to 10 PMs or post Series B; triggers include heavy PM admin load and inconsistent process. Treat as vendor heuristic, not research.
- Proposed book heuristic (author synthesis, label as such): create product ops when (a) three or more product teams share one planning calendar, (b) leaders cannot get the same metric twice with the same answer, or (c) PMs spend planning season building decks rather than deciding. Start with one senior person and one pillar, usually the one with the loudest pain.

### 4. Operating cadence (extends Chapter 00)
Chapter 00 table already defines weekly, biweekly, monthly, quarterly, annual rituals with decision outputs and two rules (every ritual produces a decision or is cancelled; decisions are written down). Chapter 28 should go deeper on who runs each ritual, inputs prepared by product ops, and AI harness pre-work. Sources to cite for rhythm choices:
- Google re:Work: annual plus quarterly OKRs, quarterly grading, mid-quarter check-ins, 0.6 to 0.7 sweet spot.
- Ryan Singer, Shape Up (Basecamp): six-week cycles plus two-week cool-down; a betting table of senior people decides the next cycle in an hour or two and the plan is then protected. A useful alternative to quarterly planning for platform teams.
- Bezos 2015 letter: weight the decision process by reversibility. Most roadmap calls are two-way doors; reserve the heavy process for one-way doors (pricing model changes, deprecations, autonomous actions in production).
- Amazon WBR and OP1/OP2 are already cited in Chapter 00 from Bryar and Carr, Working Backwards (not re-fetched here, [M]).
Proposed detailed cadence calendar format (artifact): columns Ritual | Frequency and slot | Owner (runs it) | Product ops prep | Attendees | Inputs (artifacts) | Decision output | Where recorded | Harness pre-work prompt. Rows: weekly metrics review; weekly team sync; biweekly sprint review; monthly product review; monthly AI quality and cost review (new); monthly voice-of-customer readout; quarterly business review; quarterly planning/betting table; quarterly roadmap publication to field; annual strategy refresh; annual planning (aligned to fiscal year); annual customer event launch window.

### 5. Planning calendar (artifact format, synthesis)
A one-page year view aligned to fiscal quarters: week-by-week milestones such as insight pack due (week -6 before quarter), draft bets due (week -4), betting table or portfolio review (week -3), capacity and dependency check with engineering (week -2), roadmap published internally and to the field (week -1), quarter starts, mid-quarter check (week 6), QBR (week 13). Annual overlay: strategy refresh, budget submission to Finance, pricing review, customer event code freeze. Every date has an owner and a required input. (Format is author synthesis; no single source.)

### 6. Decision rights: DACI, RACI, RAPID, door types
- DACI (Atlassian Team Playbook; Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed). Exactly one Approver. Template fields: title phrased as the question; status, impact, people, due date, outcome; 1 to 2 sentence background; relevant data; decision factors; options table with pros, cons, resources and cost; action items; outcome. 60-minute run, 3 to 6 people. Originator not named on the page; commonly attributed to Intuit (unverified, flag). Wikipedia cites Tom Kendrick, Results without Authority (2006) for the expansion.
- RACI: Responsible, Accountable (one per task in many methods), Consulted (two-way), Informed (one-way). Better for recurring tasks and deliverables than for one-off decisions.
- RAPID (Bain): Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide; one D ideally; Agree is a sparing veto for legal or regulatory must-haves; apply first to high-value or high-frequency decisions.
- How a PM chooses: DACI for one-off product decisions (most common in product orgs); RAPID when a function holds a hard veto (Security, Legal for AI Act); RACI for recurring operational work (release process, launch tiers). Product ops owns the decision-rights register: a standing table of recurring decision types (pricing change, deprecation, new autonomous action, roadmap change after commit, AI model swap) with DACI roles and door type pre-assigned.
- Ch00 says Chapter 29 covers decision logs and DACI in detail: coordinate so ch28 teaches the operating mechanism (register, rituals) and ch29 the leadership use (see flags).

### 7. PM tool stack in 2026 (categories)
Categories for the tool stack map, each with a "harness reach" column (can the AI harness read or write it through MCP or a connector, read-only or read-write):
1. Work tracking and roadmapping (Jira, Linear, Productboard, Aha!): Linear's MCP offers read-only scopes; Productboard Spark is an in-product agent.
2. Product analytics and experimentation (Amplitude, Pendo, Mixpanel): Amplitude MCP lets the harness run funnels, retention and experiment readouts in natural language.
3. Feedback and research repository (support, CS, call notes, win-loss): feeds pillar 2.
4. Docs and knowledge (Confluence, Google Docs, Notion): Rovo-style cross-app search.
5. AI harness and agents (Claude or similar, with skills and connectors): the layer that ties the others together; skills hold team templates.
6. AI quality and cost observability (eval harness, usage and cost reports, LLM observability such as Datadog or Grafana integrations): new in the AI era.
Tool map format: Category | System of record | Owner | Harness access (none, read, read-write) | Data it feeds | Ritual that consumes it | Renewal date and cost. Principle (Pendo, Cagan): product ops owns stack hygiene and prevents sprawl; one system of record per data type.

### 8. Product ops for AI products: eval and cost reporting
- Eval reporting (Anthropic, Jan 2026 vocabulary): report capability pass rate and regression pass rate separately; regression should sit near 100%; report pass^k for consistency-critical agent actions (remediation), pass@k for suggestion-style output; read transcripts each review; start from 20 to 50 real failures.
- Cost reporting (FinOps Foundation): unit costs per use case reported as distributions; cost per successful outcome includes the cost of failed runs; cache hit rate; model fit; product sets the quality bar with engineering through evals; finance in early. Data source: provider usage and cost reports grouped by workspace and model.
- Proposed monthly AI quality and cost review agenda (synthesis): eval scorecard by capability; new failures added to the suite; cost per resolved outcome trend and distribution; gross margin impact; model routing and caching changes; decisions logged.

### 9. Measuring product ops (synthesis; no verified benchmark)
Measure the function by what PMs and leaders can do faster or better, not by artifacts produced. Candidate metrics: time from question to trusted answer (insight latency); share of planning decisions made on the published calendar date; ritual decision yield (share of rituals that log at least one decision); PM time spent on discovery and customers versus reporting (self-reported quarterly survey); new PM time to first shipped decision; metric disputes per quarter (same number, different answers); tool stack cost per PM and number of overlapping tools; AI eval coverage (share of agent capabilities with a regression suite); internal NPS of PMs and field teams. Pendo's list is similar but its numeric targets are unsourced.

### 10. PM onboarding kit (artifact)
Cagan lists onboarding and training PMs as a core product ops job. Kit contents (synthesis): 30/60/90 plan; Product 360 links; metrics glossary and North Star tree; cadence calendar with which rituals to attend; decision-rights register; tool stack map with access requests; harness setup (team skills, connectors, read-only defaults); customer call shadowing schedule; the last two QBR decks and decision log; "first week prompts" for the harness (summarize last quarter's decisions, list open risks).

### Product ops charter (artifact format, synthesis)
Sections: mission (one sentence); customers of product ops (PMs, product leaders, GTM); scope by pillar with "we do / we do not" (Cagan guardrails); services and SLAs (for example, metric requests answered within N days); owned artifacts (cadence calendar, planning calendar, decision-rights register, tool stack map, templates as harness skills, onboarding kit); decision rights of product ops itself (owns process and tools; does not own product decisions); measures; review date.
