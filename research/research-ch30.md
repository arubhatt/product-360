## (b) Verified facts (running log)

- [V] Microsoft Fixed Lifecycle Policy: a defined support timeline set at launch; "a minimum of five years Mainstream Support"; an additional Extended Support period for some products. Mainstream includes incident support, security updates, and the ability to request non-security updates and design changes. Extended includes paid support and security updates at no extra cost; no new features, design changes or warranty claims. Beyond end of support: security updates only through the paid Extended Security Update (ESU) program. Self-help online content stays for at least 12 months after end of support. Previous service packs get 12 or 24 months of support after a new one ships, by product family. Policy revision effective April 13, 2010. https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/policies/fixed (page updated 2023-02-21)
- [V] Microsoft Modern Lifecycle Policy: for continuously serviced products. Stay supported only if customers (1) stay current with published servicing and system requirements, (2) are licensed, and (3) Microsoft still offers support. Minimum 30 days' notice when customers must act to avoid significant degradation. Minimum 12 months' notice before ending support when no successor is offered (excludes free services and previews). Select Azure services may give up to three years' notice. https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/lifecycle/policies/modern (page updated 2024-06-18)
- [V] MCP versioning: version IDs are dates in YYYY-MM-DD form marking the last backwards-incompatible change. Revisions are Draft, Current or Final. Current revision on 2026-10-30: 2026-07-28. Clients and servers may support several protocol versions at once; an unsupported version returns an error listing supported versions. https://modelcontextprotocol.io/specification/versioning (fetched 2026-10-30)
- [V] MCP Feature Lifecycle and Deprecation Policy, adopted via SEP-2596. Three feature states: Active, Deprecated, Removed. Deprecation criteria: superseded; security/privacy/interoperability risk that cannot be mitigated in place; negligible adoption relative to maintenance cost; or other reasons Core Maintainers deem appropriate. Deprecation SEP must name the feature, state rationale, document a migration path (replacement must already be Active), and set a minimum deprecation window of at least twelve months, counted from the release of the revision that first marks the feature Deprecated (not from SEP approval). Signals: @deprecated tag in schema.ts, deprecation notice in prose, "Deprecated" changelog heading, entry in a single deprecated-features registry with earliest removal. Tier 1 SDKs must mark the API deprecated with native language mechanisms in their next release and should emit runtime warnings. A deprecated feature may be restored by SEP; features may stay deprecated much longer than the minimum. https://modelcontextprotocol.io/community/feature-lifecycle (fetched 2026-10-30)
- [V] Anthropic model lifecycle terms: Active, Legacy (no more updates, may be deprecated), Deprecated (still works, replacement named, retirement date assigned), Retired (requests fail). Examples: Claude Sonnet 3.7 deprecated 2025-10-28, retired 2026-02-19; Claude Haiku 3 deprecated 2026-02-19, retired 2026-04-20; Claude Sonnet 4 and Opus 4 deprecated 2026-04-14, retired 2026-06-15; Claude Opus 4.1 deprecated 2026-06-05, retired 2026-08-05; Claude Sonnet 4.5 deprecated 2026-09-30, retiring 2026-11-30. Active models carry a "not sooner than" retirement date (for example roughly a year out). API parameters also get deprecated: temperature, top_p, top_k removed in API version 2026-10-21. https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/model-deprecations (fetched 2026-10-30)
- [V] Anthropic "Commitments on model deprecation and preservation", 2025-11-04: preserve weights of all publicly released models for at least the lifetime of the company; write a post-deployment report and interview the model before deprecation, recording its preferences. No notice period stated there. https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments
- [V] OpenAI deprecations: "deprecation" starts at announcement and always carries a shutdown date; "shutdown" and "sunset" used interchangeably for loss of access; "legacy" means no more updates. - [V] Microsoft Foundry Models lifecycle and support policy (updated 2026-07-24): five stages Preview, GA, Legacy (optional), Deprecated (existing customers only; new customers blocked; "existing" judged per subscription), Retired (inference returns 410 Gone). Standard GA lifecycle 18 months: retirement date set programmatically at launch; deprecated for new customers at 12 months; replacement testable in global standard about 90 days before retirement; official replacement declared only 90 to 120 days before retirement, so the recommendation reflects the best model at migration time. Some partner models (Anthropic, DeepSeek, Fireworks, Mistral AI) follow 12-month lifecycles. Retirement notices at least 60 days ahead for GA, 30 days for previews; preview deployments are force-upgraded. Standard deployments auto-upgrade region by region; customers choose upgrade option (when new default is available, only at expiry, or never, in which case the deployment stops working). Provisioned deployments are not auto-upgraded. Retirement dates are not extendable. Emergency retirement with shortened notice for security or compliance issues. Advice: evaluate candidates on your own prompts and data, comparing quality, latency and cost together, not public benchmarks alone. Note the trap: API status "Deprecated" means retired in docs terms. https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/azure/ai-foundry/openai/concepts/model-retirements
- [V] RFC 9745, The Deprecation HTTP Response Header Field, Sanjay Dalal and Erik Wilde, March 2025, Standards Track. Header signals a resource will be or has been deprecated, with a date (Structured Field date, for example @1688169599). Deprecation does not change behavior; a hint. ## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. The product life cycle (PLC): four stages, and why the curve misleads
- Origin: the PLC as a marketing concept was popularized by Theodore Levitt (HBR, 1965), who argued managers knew it but rarely used it as a planning tool. Fox (1975) used a five-stage version adding precommercialization.
- Stages and typical B2B signals:
  - Introduction: slow adoption, high cost to serve, design partners and lighthouse customers, roadmap driven by discovery. Strategy: find product-market fit, narrow ICP, price for learning.
  - Growth: rapid ARR growth, new competitors, scaling go-to-market. Strategy: invest, widen integrations, harden platform, build partner channel.
  - Maturity: growth slows to market rate, pricing pressure, retention and expansion dominate. Strategy: efficiency, packaging, extend the curve (new segments, adjacent use cases), protect margin.
  - Decline: shrinking usage or replaced by a successor. Strategy: harvest, consolidate, migrate, or retire.
- Critique (Dhalla and Yuspeh, HBR 1976, and later writers): the curve is a description after the fact, not a law. Stage depends on marketing and product decisions; calling a plateau "decline" can become self-fulfilling (their floor wax story). Levitt himself said placing a product on the curve is an art rather than a science. Further 2026 critiques for the chapter (author synthesis, not from a source): SaaS products rarely follow one bell curve because continuous delivery resets features; platform products have stacked curves (core, modules, APIs, models) at different stages; AI features can go from introduction to commodity in months as models improve.
- How a PM applies it: assess stage per offering, module, API version and model dependency, not per company. Use evidence (growth rate vs market, share of new bookings, retention, cost to serve, competitive parity, strategic fit). Treat the stage as a hypothesis that sets investment posture, reviewed each planning cycle.

### 2. Lifecycle stage assessment (artifact)
Suggested template for the chapter (author-built, grounded in the PLC and BCG logic):
- Offering, owner, date, evidence window.
- Signals: ARR and growth vs market; share of new bookings; active customers and usage trend; NRR and gross churn for this offering; cost to serve and gross margin; support ticket volume per customer; competitive parity; dependency risk (third-party models, end-of-life platforms); strategic fit with portfolio.
- Assessed stage plus confidence (low, medium, high) and the single signal that would change the call.
- Investment posture: invest, sustain, harvest, retire.
- Decision and next review date.
AI-harness angle: the PM asks the harness, connected to CRM, billing, product analytics and support, for each signal with sources cited, then challenges the outliers. No queries taught.

### 3. Portfolio lifecycle balance
- BCG growth-share matrix (Alan Zakon sketch; Bruce Henderson, "The Product Portfolio," 1970): stars, cash cows, question marks, dogs (originally "pets"). Prescriptions: invest in stars, milk cash cows to fund question marks, divest or reposition dogs. BCG notes every product ends as a cash cow or dog.
- PM application: a healthy portfolio has offerings at several stages so mature cash flows fund the next curve. Warning signs: everything in maturity (no next curve), or everything in introduction (no cash engine), or a decline-stage product absorbing engineering capacity disproportionate to its revenue.
- Pair with the stage assessment to produce a one-page portfolio view: each offering's stage, posture, revenue share and engineering share.

### 4. Stage-Gate (Robert G. Cooper)
- Term first in print in 1988. Discovery, then stages: Scoping/Concept, Build the Business Case, Development, Testing and Validation, Launch; then a post-launch review.
- Gates: deliverables (what the team brings), criteria (readiness and business value; scored with tools such as scorecards and Expected Commercial Value), outputs (Go, Kill, Hold or Recycle, plus approved plan, resources and next gate date).
- Must-meet (knock-out) and should-meet (scored) criteria are a widely taught feature of Cooper's gates; not confirmed on the source fetched (see flags).
- Later "next generation" versions add iterative build-test-feedback loops and agile sprints inside stages (Agile-Stage-Gate hybrids).
- B2B/platform application: use light gates at moments of irreversible commitment: entering a new segment, GA, price change, end-of-sale, end-of-support. A lifecycle gate set mirrors Stage-Gate in reverse for retirement: Gate R1 "consider retirement," Gate R2 "announce end-of-sale," Gate R3 "end of support," Gate R4 "decommission."
- Stage-gate criteria template (author-built): must-meet (strategic fit, legal and security clearance, no unmitigated customer harm, funded owner); should-meet scored 1 to 5 (customer evidence, market attractiveness, competitive advantage, feasibility, financial return, risk). Decision rules written in advance.

### 5. End-of-life milestones as enterprise vendors publish them (generic)
Typical sequence for on-premises and hardware-style products (describe generically in the chapter):
1. End-of-life announcement: public notice with all dates below and the recommended successor.
2. End-of-sale (last order date): no new orders; renewals may continue.
3. Last ship date (hardware) or last new subscription term.
4. End of software maintenance: no new features or bug fixes; security fixes may continue for a period.
5. End of security/vulnerability fixes.
6. Last date of support: support contracts end; documentation stays available for a time.
Public anchors (verified): Microsoft Fixed Lifecycle Policy (minimum five years Mainstream, then Extended; then paid ESU up to three years); Microsoft Modern Lifecycle Policy (continuous servicing, customers must stay current, minimum 12 months' notice when no successor, 30 days when action is required); Red Hat's ten-year three-phase life cycle with paid extensions; Google Cloud's 12-month discontinuation commitment written into its terms. Pattern: dates set at launch (Fixed) vs dates set by notice (Modern).

### 6. Deciding to retire (sunset decision memo)
Author-built memo format:
- Recommendation in one sentence, with dates.
- Why now: usage, revenue, cost to serve, engineering drag, security or dependency risk, strategic fit, successor readiness.
- Who is affected: customers by segment and ARR, partners, internal teams; top accounts named.
- Options considered: keep investing, sustain in maintenance, sell or spin off, open-source, migrate and retire. Include "do nothing" cost.
- Financial impact: ARR at risk, expected migration rate, expected churn, savings, one-time costs (credits, migration services).
- Contract and legal review: committed terms, SLAs, notice clauses, data return and deletion, partner agreements, regulatory obligations.
- Migration path and incentives.
- Communication plan summary.
- Milestones and gates; reversal criteria (what would make us pause).
- Decision owner and approvers.

### 7. Migration plan
Elements: customer segmentation (by complexity and ARR); feature parity map (old capability, new equivalent, gap, workaround); tooling (automated migration, config import); parallel run window (Stripe's two-endpoint pattern is a clean analogy); success criteria per customer; incentives (credits, price lock, free services); support staffing curve; tracking of migration status per account; escalation path for blockers.

### 8. Customer communication and end-of-sale notice
- Principles drawn from verified policies: name dates up front; name the successor; state what changes at each milestone; give programmatic signals as well as email (Deprecation and Sunset HTTP headers, model status fields, @deprecated tags); publish a single canonical registry (MCP's deprecated-features registry; vendor lifecycle pages).
- End-of-sale notice template (author-built, generic): product and SKUs affected; summary; milestone table (announcement, end-of-sale, end of maintenance, end of security fixes, last date of support); successor and migration path; what customers must do and by when; commercial terms (credits, price protection); support during transition; contacts and FAQ link.
- Deprecation communication plan: audience matrix (economic buyer, admin, developer, partner, internal CSM and sales), channel per audience (email, in-product banner, API headers, docs, account team call), sequence (pre-brief top accounts and partners before public notice; public notice; reminders at fixed intervals; final warning; post-retirement confirmation).

### 9. Contract, legal and revenue considerations
- Contracts can fix notice periods (Google Cloud terms: 12 months, with law, security and burden exceptions; pre-GA excluded). Check master agreements for notice, SLA, data portability and deletion, and termination-for-convenience rights.
- Revenue: retirement creates ARR at risk; mitigations include migration credits and paid extended support. Microsoft's ESU at 100% of license price per year (on-premises) and free on Azure shows extended support used as both revenue and migration incentive. Red Hat sells extended life add-ons.
- Revenue recognition and deferred revenue questions belong to finance; the PM flags them, does not decide them.

### 10. API deprecation policies
- Kubernetes: promise length tied to stability (GA not removed within a major version; beta at least 9 months or 3 releases after deprecation; alpha anytime).
- Google AIP-185: channel-based versions; beta deprecation recommended at 180 days.
- GitHub: date-named versions, previous version supported at least 24 months.
- Stripe: per-account pinned versions; customers upgrade on their schedule.
- MCP: feature lifecycle Active, Deprecated, Removed; at least twelve months deprecated before removal; ninety-day floor only for active security risk; migration path mandatory; SDKs must mark and warn.
- HTTP signals: RFC 9745 Deprecation header (2025) and RFC 8594 Sunset header (2019).
- PM application: publish the policy before you need it; tie each surface's promise to its stability label; measure who still calls deprecated endpoints and reach out directly.

### 11. Lifecycle of AI models and features
- Provider vocabulary converges on Active, Legacy, Deprecated, Retired (Anthropic; Microsoft Foundry adds Preview and GA; Bedrock uses Active, Legacy, EOL; OpenAI uses legacy, deprecation, shutdown).
- Notice floors differ: Anthropic at least 60 days for public models; OpenAI at least 6 months for GA, 3 months for specialized variants, weeks for previews; Microsoft Foundry 18-month standard GA lifecycle with 60-day retirement notice, replacement named 90 to 120 days before retirement; Bedrock 6-month or 45-day Legacy periods; Gemini previews 2 weeks.
- Channel dates differ from provider dates (Anthropic and Bedrock both say so). A B2B product built on several channels must track each.
- Aliases ("latest") change under you; pinned snapshots retire. Choose deliberately.
- Model deprecation runbook (author-built): (1) inventory every model dependency by feature, channel, region and customer tier; (2) subscribe to provider notices and poll status fields; (3) on notice, open a migration ticket with the retirement date and internal deadline at least 30 days earlier; (4) shortlist candidates; (5) run the feature's eval suite on representative data comparing quality, latency and cost together; (6) adjust prompts and tools; (7) shadow or canary; (8) customer communication if behavior, data residency or price changes; (9) cut over with rollback; (10) record results and update the model register. Pre-work: keep evals and a provider abstraction so a swap is a routine change.
- Your own AI features also have lifecycles: an AI feature built on a retiring model, or overtaken by a new capability, needs the same end-of-life discipline. Customers with AI risk documentation (for example EU AI Act requests) may need notice when the underlying model changes.

- Dhalla and Yuspeh's full argument not read; only the abstract and title seen. Phrase the critique cautiously.
- Levitt quote is from a secondary source (Wikipedia citing Levitt).
- Atlassian Server end-of-support (2024-02-15) and Twilio Programmable Video end-of-life reversal: not verified (pages failed). Do not use unless verified.
- Oracle Lifetime Support page returned 403; not used.
