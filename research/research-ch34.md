## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. Audience first: the audience is the hero (Nancy Duarte)
- Originator: Nancy Duarte, Resonate (2010); earlier slide:ology (2008) on visual design of slides; HBR Guide to Persuasive Presentations (2012); DataStory (2019).
- Summary: the presenter is the mentor, the audience is the hero who must change. A talk moves the audience between "what is" and "what could be" (the sparkline), alternating the two and ending with a call to action and a picture of the new state. Duarte's Big Idea: one sentence that states your point of view and what is at stake for the audience. [Sparkline and Big Idea definitions are from the book; Duarte web pages fetched confirm only "audience the hero, presenter the mentor" and "conflict and resolution". Treat structure detail as paraphrase of the book.]
- Template (book-derived, paraphrase): Big Idea = "[Audience] should [action/belief] because [point of view], and if they do not, [stake]."
- B2B/platform PM application: the hero is rarely "the product". In a QBR the hero is the operations org; in an exec review the hero is the portfolio leader who must make a call. Write the Big Idea before any slide exists and test it with the AI harness: "Here is my Big Idea and audience; argue as the CFO why it does not matter."

### 2. The Big Idea and the 3-minute story (Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic)
- Originator: Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic, Storytelling with Data (Wiley, 2015); storytelling with you (presentation book, believed 2022, flag year).
- Summary: before building anything, answer who the audience is, what you need them to know or do, and how you will make the point. The Big Idea is "a single sentence that captures your point of view" plus what is at stake (SWD blog, verified). The 3-minute story: if you had only three minutes, what would you say (from the book, [S]). Storyboard low-tech, on sticky notes, before opening software (verified).
- Graph principles (SWD, verified): be clear whether you explore or explain; create an aha moment; do not overcomplicate; remove the non-essential; make it clear where to look; use words (action titles, axis titles, source footnotes); audience trumps everything.
- Book's six lessons (Wiley description, verified): understand context; choose an effective visual; eliminate clutter; focus attention; think like a designer; tell a story.
- B2B PM application: ask the harness to draft three candidate Big Ideas from the evidence pack, then pick one and justify it. Every chart title states the takeaway ("MTTR fell 22% for agent-assisted incidents") rather than the metric name ("MTTR by month").

### 3. Answer first: Pyramid Principle, SCQ and BLUF
- Barbara Minto, The Pyramid Principle (McKinsey origin, book 1987 and later editions): ideas sit in a pyramid under one governing point; the introduction uses Situation, Complication, Question so the reader's question is explicit, and the top of the pyramid answers it (site verifies pyramid and SCQ). Supporting points are grouped and each group summarizes the ideas below it [S, book].
- Template: Situation (what the audience already agrees with). Complication (what changed). Question (what should we do). Answer (the recommendation). Then three supporting arguments, each with evidence.
- BLUF (bottom line up front): US military writing convention, popularized for business by Kabir Sehgal (HBR, 2016) [S].
- Duarte's executive guidance (verified): assume your slot will be cut; lead with findings, recommendation and call to action; say up front that the summary comes first and discussion follows; executive summary up front with the rest as appendix ("10% rule": 5 summary slides for a 50-slide appendix); let the group steer and pull appendix slides on demand; answer the specific request first.
- B2B PM application: first slide or first paragraph is the decision being asked for, the recommendation and the cost of not deciding. Everything else is appendix that the harness can index for Q&A.

### 4. Pre-reads and narrative memos (Jeff Bezos, Amazon)
- Originator: Bezos; 2017 shareholder letter (verified): no slide presentations; narratively structured six-page memos read silently at the start of the meeting ("study hall"); great memos take a week or more of writing, review and rewriting.
- Edward Tufte, The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint: slide templates fragment reasoning and corrupt statistical analysis; Tufte reports the Columbia Accident Investigation Board found PowerPoint inappropriate for engineering reports (verified as Tufte's account). His alternative: a dense written document or summary matrix, slides only for visuals.
- Synthesis for the chapter: decide the mode. Decision meetings with complex trade-offs: pre-read memo (2 to 6 pages) plus a short verbal frame. Alignment or inspiration: talk with visuals. Status: written update, no meeting. The PM should not present a pre-read; open by asking what questions the reading raised.
- Pre-read format suggestion: one-paragraph ask; context; options with trade-offs; recommendation; risks and what would change our mind; appendix and FAQ.

### 5. Handling interruptions (Matt Abrahams and guests, Stanford GSB)
- Verified: set expectations up front ("give me two minutes for the overall picture"); signpost how many points are coming; if interrupted, paraphrase the interrupter's point, comment, then resume.
- Executive interruptions are usually a signal, not rudeness: the exec has the answer they need or a concern blocking them. Rule for the chapter: when interrupted with a question, answer it in one sentence, then either jump to the slide that matters or offer to cover it in the appendix. Do not say "I'll get to that."

### 6. Data storytelling
- Knaflic (above), Duarte DataStory (2019).
- Kent's words of estimative probability (1964, verified): vague phrases like "serious possibility" were read by experts as anything from 20% to 80%. Kent's fix: map words to numeric ranges (almost certain about 93%, probable about 75%, chances about even 50%, probably not about 30%, almost certainly not about 7%).
- Application: in any deck, a word like "likely" must carry a number or range. Put a glossary line in the footnote when using estimative language.
- Mayer, Multimedia Learning (Cambridge, 2001): people learn better from words and pictures together than words alone (verified at the level of the core claim; individual principles such as coherence, signaling, redundancy not verified in this pass, use as [S]).
- Curse of knowledge (Newton 1990; Heath brothers 2006): experts overestimate how clear their message is; rehearse with someone outside the team [S].

### 7. Delivery and nonverbal communication, with evidence
- Mehrabian correction (verified on Mehrabian's own site): the 7% words, 38% tone, 55% face figures come from experiments on communicating like or dislike with single words, and apply only when someone is talking about feelings or attitudes. They do not mean words carry 7% of a presentation's meaning. Correct teaching point: when words and tone conflict on a feeling (for example "I'm fine with this plan" said flatly), people trust tone and face. That matters in exec rooms when a PM says they are confident but sounds otherwise.
- Power poses: Carney (first author of the 2010 study) wrote in 2016 that she does not believe power pose effects are real, citing tiny sample, flexible analysis and failed replications (Ranehill et al. 2015; Simmons and Simonsohn p-curve). Do not teach power poses as a hormone or confidence hack.
- Anxiety reappraisal (verified): Alison Wood Brooks, Journal of Experimental Psychology: General, 2014: reappraising pre-performance anxiety as excitement (for example saying "I am excited") improved performance in public speaking, singing and math tasks compared with trying to calm down. Single-paper evidence; flag that replication status was not checked.
- Practical nonverbal guidance to teach as craft, not science: pause after the key sentence; slow down on numbers; look at the decision maker when stating the ask; keep hands visible and still when listening to pushback. Label as practitioner advice.

### 8. Virtual and recorded presentations
- Bailenson (2021, Technology, Mind, and Behavior) on "nonverbal overload" in video calls [S; four causes from memory: verify]. Practical implications: hide self-view, allow cameras off for listening, shorter segments.
- Recorded format (Loom-style async video, recorded demos): shorter, front-load the ask in the first 30 seconds, chapter markers, captions, a written summary under the video so execs can skim. These are practitioner norms, not studies.
- TED speaker page (verified): strict time limits; many strong talks run 6 to 8 or 10 to 12 minutes; speakers get coaching and rehearse. Use as an analogy for recorded product updates: short, single idea.

### 9. Q&A and pushback
- Prep: list the 10 hardest questions; for each, a one-sentence answer, the evidence, the appendix slide, and who in the room is likely to ask it. AI harness acts as a murder board in personas (CFO, security reviewer, skeptical peer PM).
- Honest "I don't know" (Lloyd Minor with Abrahams, verified): say you do not know, do not fake certainty, follow up with the answer, be transparent when your view changes.
- SWD (verified): separate findings from recommendations; people can accept data and still reject the action; for contentious topics, offer options and ask for input.
- Answer pattern for chapter (synthesis, original): Acknowledge (restate the concern in their terms), Answer (one sentence), Anchor (one piece of evidence), Advance (return to the ask). Mark as the book's own heuristic.

### 10. Executive presence (Sylvia Ann Hewlett, CTI/Coqual)
- Verified: 2012 survey, 268 senior execs and about 4,000 professionals; EP = gravitas (67% called it core), communication (28%), appearance (5%); execs said EP accounts for 26% of what it takes to get the next promotion. Gravitas behaviors: confidence and grace under fire, decisiveness, integrity and speaking truth to power, emotional intelligence, reputation, vision. Communication: speaking skills, commanding a room, reading an audience.
- Caveat to teach: the same research found people of color felt held to a stricter EP code (56% vs 31%) and 81% were unclear how to act on EP feedback. EP is partly a cultural filter; the chapter should teach the substantive parts (clarity, composure, judgment, candor) and name the bias risk.
- PM translation: presence = knowing the number, stating the recommendation, owning the risk, staying calm when challenged, and saying "I don't know, I'll have it by Thursday."

### 11. Presenting AI products and probabilistic results honestly
- Model cards (Mitchell et al., 2019, verified): report intended use, performance across relevant groups and evaluation conditions. Translate into a one-slide "model card for execs": what it is for, what it is not for, how it was tested, how it performs by segment, known failure modes.
- Error bars on evals (Anthropic, November 2024, verified): report standard error and 95% confidence interval; cluster errors; compare with paired differences; size evals by power analysis. Exec translation: never show a single accuracy number without the range and the sample size; never claim an improvement smaller than the noise.
- Kent's estimative language (verified): map words to ranges.
- Honesty rules to teach: show the denominator; show failures alongside wins (cherry-picked demo outputs mislead); separate offline eval from production outcome; state what the agent does when it is wrong (the guardrail story); never say "the AI understands"; demo live only with a fallback recording and state when a demo is scripted.

### 12. AI tools for deck building and rehearsal (2025 to 2026)
- See section (d). Teach workflow, not clicks: (1) harness drafts a presentation plan from the brief and evidence via connectors; (2) human writes the Big Idea and the ask; (3) harness drafts storyline and slide titles (action titles); (4) human cuts; (5) harness builds slides in the corporate template; (6) PM verifies every number against source; (7) rehearsal with AI coach and persona-based Q&A; (8) post-meeting summary and follow-ups.
- Risks: fluent but generic narratives, invented numbers, mis-scaled charts, sending confidential data to unapproved tools. Rule: every number on a slide has a source link in the speaker notes.

## (d) 2025 to 2026 AI-era developments (verified)

- **Workslop (HBR, Sept 2025; BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab).** AI-generated content that looks good but lacks substance, pushing the real thinking onto the receiver. 40% of 1,150 US desk workers received some in the prior month; about two hours lost per instance; about $186 per employee per month. Implication: the PM's reputation now depends on documents being dense with judgment, not long.
- **Cognitive debt (Kosmyna et al., MIT Media Lab, arXiv preprint, June 2025, revised December 2025).** In an essay task, LLM users showed the weakest brain connectivity, the lowest sense of ownership and trouble quoting their own essays. Small sample, preprint. Implication: do the thinking (the pyramid, the ask) yourself; delegate drafting and critique.
- **Hidden AI use and unchecked output (KPMG and University of Melbourne, April 2025).** Globally 66% of employees rely on AI output without evaluating accuracy and 56% have made mistakes due to AI. In the US, 53% avoid disclosing AI use and present AI content as their own. Implication: verification and disclosure are now differentiators of trust.
- **Social penalty for AI use (Reif, Larrick and Soll, PNAS, May 2025).** Four preregistered experiments, 4,439 participants: AI users are rated lower on competence and motivation. Implication: this penalty is a reason many people hide AI use, and a tension the chapter should name honestly. The better answer is visible judgment: show your reasoning, your evidence and your decisions, so the reader sees what the human contributed.
- **AI use for communications is mainstream (Microsoft Work Trend Index, April 2025).** 68% of "Frontier Firm" leaders use AI for internal communications versus 46% of workers globally. Implication: readers increasingly use AI to read, too; write so summaries keep the ask.
- **AI Fluency 4D framework (Anthropic with Feller and Dakan).** Delegation, Description, Discernment, Diligence. Map to writing: delegate drafting not deciding; describe the audience and template well; discern quality and errors; be diligent about verification and disclosure. Definitions not verified on fetched page; describe at the level of the four names only.
- **Disclosure precedent.** ICMJE guidance (current page, undated) requires disclosure of AI use, bars AI authorship and holds humans accountable. Useful analogy for a workplace disclosure norm.
