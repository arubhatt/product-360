## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. Unit economics: CAC, LTV, payback (David Skok, "SaaS Metrics 2.0", For Entrepreneurs, first written 2011)
- Summary: a recurring-revenue business works only if what it earns from a customer over the relationship comfortably exceeds what it spends to win them, and if it earns that spend back fast enough not to run out of capital. Skok's two guidelines: LTV:CAC above 3, and months to recover CAC under 12. Skok himself relaxes the payback guideline for enterprise land-and-expand models (about 20 months).
- Formats (standard formulations in words; Skok's own formulas appear as images on the page and were not read):
  - CAC = total sales and marketing cost for a period / new customers won in that period. State whether it is blended (all channels) or paid-only, and whether it is fully loaded (salaries, tools, partner commissions).
  - LTV = ARPA x gross margin % / churn rate (customer lifetime = 1 / churn). For B2B with expansion, note that NRR above 100% breaks the simple churn formula; cap lifetime (for example 5 years) and say so.
  - Months to recover CAC = CAC / (new MRR per customer x gross margin %).
- B2B/platform application: compute by segment and by motion (direct enterprise, partner, self-serve), never only blended. Gross margin matters for AI products: inference cost lowers margin and lengthens payback even if price and CAC do not move. Partner-sourced deals carry margin share or commission that belongs in CAC.

### 2. B2B pipeline metrics and lead stages
- MQL (marketing qualified lead: intent inferred from marketing signals), SAL (sales accepted), SQL (sales qualified), opportunity, closed-won. Stage conversion rates, stage velocity (days in stage) and win rate. Pipeline coverage = open pipeline / quota for the period. Pipeline velocity is commonly stated as (opportunities x win rate x average deal size) / sales cycle length (common practitioner formula, no single originator verified).
- Origin: the "demand waterfall" (SiriusDecisions, later part of Forrester) formalized MQL to SQL stages. Not verified this session; see flag. Attribute generically as "the demand waterfall popularized by SiriusDecisions" or omit attribution.
- PM application: the PM owns the product-side stages (activation, PQL, expansion signals) and must agree definitions with marketing and sales ops. In B2B the unit is the account and buying group, not the individual lead.

### 3. Product-led growth and PQLs (OpenView, Blake Bartlett 2016; Wes Bush, ProductLed)
- PLG (OpenView definition): a go-to-market strategy in which the product itself is the primary driver of acquisition, conversion and expansion. Bartlett coined the term at OpenView in 2016 (self-reported by OpenView).
- PQL (Wes Bush): a lead who has experienced meaningful value in the product, usually through a free trial or freemium. Contrast: MQL is based on marketing signals (email opens, downloads, page visits); PQL is based on experienced value.
- Bush's method for defining a PQL: (1) find behaviors that correlate with upgrading, usually tied to the aha moment; (2) review signals: product interest, number of users, features used, usage and spend patterns, adoption velocity; (3) start with a "minimum viable PQL" (his example: a user logging in 10 times) and refine toward the behaviors that best predict conversion.
- Core PLG metrics to teach (standard set): sign-ups, activation rate, time to value, free-to-paid or trial-to-paid conversion, PQL rate and PQL-to-paid conversion, expansion (seats, usage), NRR. For B2B add the product qualified account (PQA): several users in one company crossing the threshold (practitioner term; no originator verified).
- B2B application: in enterprise, PLG is usually "product-led sales": the product creates qualified demand that sales then closes. Forrester's 2026 finding that more than 60% of buyers use a trial (78% for purchases of $10M or more) supports making the trial or proof of value a measured funnel stage even for sales-led products.

### 4. Journey design with data: funnel by journey stage
- Lineage to attribute: the AARRR "pirate metrics" (Dave McClure, 2007: acquisition, activation, retention, referral, revenue) [U, not fetched]; McKinsey's consumer decision journey (Court et al., 2009: initial consideration, active evaluation, moment of purchase, post-purchase loyalty loop) [U, not fetched]; Gartner's B2B buying jobs (2018, already cited in chapter 01 refs).
- Funnel-by-stage model (proposed template for the chapter): for each journey stage record: buyer question; persona; touchpoints (owned, paid, earned, partner, product, AI answer engine); entry definition; leading metric; conversion to next stage; median time in stage; owner; data source and its blind spots.
- B2B application: the journey is non-linear and multi-person. Forrester (2026) says buyers start in AI answer engines, then validate with trusted humans; G2 (2025) says half of software buyers now start in an AI chatbot. A funnel model must therefore include stages that the company cannot observe directly and mark them as such.

### 5. Measuring channel and ad effectiveness: incrementality
- Incrementality = the outcomes that would not have happened without the activity. Measured against a counterfactual, not against a baseline of everyone who converted.
- Methods at concept level:
  - User-level randomized lift tests (holdout): treatment sees ads, control does not. Ghost ads (Johnson, Lewis and Nubbemeyer, 2017) log ads the control group would have seen, avoiding PSA placebo costs.
  - Geo experiments (matched markets): turn spend on or off in regions; works without user tracking. Usable in B2B only where account density per region is high enough.
  - Account-level holdouts (B2B adaptation): randomly hold out a share of target accounts from a campaign and compare pipeline creation. Proposed practice, not a cited framework.
- Evidence to teach: Gordon, Zettelmeyer, Bhargava and Chapsky (Marketing Science, 2019) showed observational methods often fail to recover experimental lift across 15 Facebook RCTs. Lewis and Rao (QJE, 2015) showed even large RCTs give imprecise ad-return estimates because outcomes are noisy. Lesson: prefer experiments, size them honestly, and expect wide confidence intervals in B2B where conversions are few.

### 6. MMM versus MTA (concept level)
- Marketing mix modeling (MMM): top-down regression of outcomes on aggregate spend by channel over time, controlling for seasonality and trend; privacy-durable (no user data); slow, needs long history, weak for small B2B volumes. Open-source: Google Meridian (Bayesian, geo-level, calibrates with experiments, budget optimizer; GA January 2025) and Meta Robyn (ridge regression, calibration with lift and geo tests, budget allocator).
- Multi-touch attribution (MTA): bottom-up credit across observed user-level touchpoints; fast and granular; eroded by ATT, browser cookie blocking and consent; sees only tracked touches; correlational.
- Triangulation (teach as the 2026 default): MMM for budget allocation, experiments for causal truth and to calibrate the MMM, attribution for in-flight optimization. Both Meridian and Robyn are built to be calibrated with experiments.

### 7. Attribution models and their biases
| Model | Rule | Bias for a B2B PM |
|---|---|---|
| First touch | 100% to first recorded touch | Over-credits awareness channels; ignores everything that convinced the buying group |
| Last touch | 100% to last touch before conversion | Over-credits demand capture (branded search, retargeting, direct sales email); under-credits what created the demand |
| Linear | Equal credit to every touch | Looks fair, makes no causal claim; rewards channels that generate many low-value touches |
| Time decay | More credit to touches nearer conversion | Recency bias; penalizes early education in 6 to 18 month enterprise cycles |
| Position-based (U-shaped) | Typically 40% first, 40% last, 20% middle | Weights are convention, not evidence (W-shaped adds opportunity creation for B2B) |
| Data-driven | Machine learning on path data with counterfactual comparison | Better, but a black box trained on the platform's own observed data; cannot see off-platform touches; platform grades its own homework |
- Cross-cutting biases: only observed touches count (peer referrals, communities, podcasts, AI answers are invisible "dark" touches); selection bias (ads target people already likely to buy); double counting across platforms that each self-attribute; person-level models in an account-level buying process.
- Market fact: Google retired first click, linear, time decay and position-based from Google Ads and GA4 in 2023, leaving data-driven and last click. Rules-based models now live mostly in CRM and marketing automation tools.
- Position-based 40/20/40 weights are a common convention; no originator verified.

### 8. Privacy changes and measurement (status as of 2026-10-30)
- Chrome: third-party cookies NOT deprecated. Google dropped deprecation (July 2024), dropped the standalone prompt (April 2025), retired most Privacy Sandbox APIs including the Attribution Reporting API (October 2025). Users choose in settings; Incognito blocks by default.
- iOS: ATT since iOS 14.5 (enforced 2021-04-26): apps need permission to track or read the IDFA.
- Teach the effect, not the mechanics: user-level paths are incomplete and getting more so; consent and platform rules decide what can be joined. Response: first-party data (CRM, product telemetry, which B2B companies own), aggregate models, experiments, and self-reported attribution ("how did you hear about us").

### 9. How strategy uses these numbers
- Where to play: segment and motion economics (LTV:CAC and payback by segment and channel) decide which customers and motions to invest in.
- How to win: journey conversion data shows the binding constraint (for example, trials that never activate, or deals stalling at security review), which becomes a product investment, not a marketing one.
- Investment allocation: MMM and experiments inform budget shifts; strategy memo states expected incremental return and the test that would change the decision.
- Long versus short term: Dawes's 95:5 heuristic says most B2B buyers are out of market at any time, so attribution that only credits in-market capture will starve brand and memory building. Teach as a heuristic, per Dawes.
- Guardrail: Goodhart's law (when a measure becomes a target it ceases to be a good measure; Charles Goodhart, 1975, phrasing by Marilyn Strathern) [U, not fetched this session].

### 10. AI in attribution and growth analytics (2026)
- Platform AI: data-driven attribution (Google, counterfactual using holdbacks and RCT-trained gains), Meta incremental attribution (holdout plus ML; optimization and reporting modes), Bayesian MMM (Meridian v2.1.0 in 2026).
- PM practice with an AI harness: connectors to CRM, product analytics, billing and ad platforms; the PM asks questions, the harness pulls data and drafts reports with cited queries. Teach prompts and evidence standards, not tools: ask for denominators, time windows, segment cuts, confidence intervals, and the data source for every number; ask the harness to list what the data cannot see.
- Risks: invented or mis-joined numbers, silent definition drift (MQL defined differently in two systems), and confident narratives over noisy data. Require a reproducible source for every figure that reaches a decision memo.

### 11. AI search and answer engines changing acquisition (2026)
- Facts: Google AI Overviews at 1.5B monthly users (May 2025) and AI Mode rolling out to all US users (May 2025); Pew found clicks on traditional results fall from 15% to 8% of visits when an AI summary appears, with about 1% clicking a cited link; G2 found 50% of B2B software buyers start in an AI chatbot (Aug 2025); Forrester (Jan 2026) says buyers start in answer engines then validate with trusted humans; OpenAI began testing ads in ChatGPT (announced Jan 2026).
- Implications to teach: fewer, later and less attributable clicks; AI answers become an invisible journey stage; "share of answer" (how often and how accurately AI assistants describe and recommend the product for target questions) becomes a tracked metric; content, documentation, review sites and trustworthy third-party evidence feed answers. GEO (Aggarwal et al., KDD 2024) is the academic framing; treat vendor "GEO" playbooks as unproven.

- Derived ARR bridge (from bible: ARR about $48M, growth 38%, NRR 118%): prior-year ARR about $34.8M; existing customers now contribute about $41.0M (118% of $34.8M); so new-logo ARR is about $7.0M and expansion about $6.2M. Lesson: more than half of Beacon's net new ARR comes from the installed base, which matters for where growth money goes.
- Fictional chapter numbers (proposed): about 90 new logos a year at an average first-year ACV of about $78K; new-logo sales and marketing spend about $12M; CAC about $133K. Payback at the old 81% gross margin about 25 months; at today's 72% about 28 months. Both exceed Skok's enterprise ceiling of about 20 months. The agentic margin drop alone added about 3 months of payback: a clean link between the pricing chapter (per-resolved-incident) and growth economics.
- Three motions to compare in a growth metrics scorecard: (1) direct enterprise (Marcus as economic buyer, long cycle, high ACV); (2) MSP partners (Leo; partner margin belongs in CAC; multi-tenant expansion drives NRR); (3) cross-sell into Northlight Observe customers (lowest CAC, but feeds the "build on Observe data versus tool-agnostic" tension).
- Journey funnel model with personas: Priya asks an AI assistant how to cut alert noise (invisible stage) -> visits docs and community -> starts a proof of value on one environment (activation = first correlated incident; PQA = 3 or more responders in an account accepting a diagnosis) -> Sam's security review (where deals stall: least privilege, audit logs, data residency) -> Marcus's ROI case (MTTR, escalations) -> procurement. The binding constraint the data reveals: security review, which makes the "AI risk documentation pack" a product investment.
- Attribution assessment: marketing's last-touch dashboard credits "Northlight Live" registrations and branded search for most pipeline. A first-touch view credits AI-assistant and documentation traffic and Observe in-product prompts. Neither shows Priya's peer recommendations or community posts. Lesson: the model chosen decides which team gets budget.
- Channel experiment plan: an account-level holdout. Randomly hold out 20% of about 600 target accounts from a paid LinkedIn and webinar program for two quarters; compare opportunity creation rate. Pre-register the metric, minimum detectable effect and decision rule; acknowledge the small-sample problem (Lewis and Rao) and plan to combine with self-reported attribution.
- AI answer engine audit: the PM asks the harness to run a fixed set of buyer questions ("best tool to find root cause of production incidents", "AIOps for MSPs") across assistants monthly and score share of answer and accuracy. The hallucinated-root-cause storyline has a twin here: an AI assistant describes Beacon as "autonomous by default", which alarms Sam's peers. Fix: clear public docs on approval gates.
- Data-driven strategy memo: recommend shifting growth investment from net-new paid demand toward (a) Observe cross-sell and MSP expansion, (b) shortening security review with a self-serve trust center, (c) per-resolved-incident pricing to align price with value and margin; state what evidence would reverse the call.
