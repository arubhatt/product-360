1. Fader, P. S. and Hardie, B. G. S. "How to Project Customer Retention." *Journal of Interactive Marketing* 21(1), Winter 2007, pp. 76 onward. DOI 10.1002/dir.20074. PDF: https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2012/04/Fader_hardie_jim_07.pdf **[V]** (read PDF text this session)
   - Contractual setting: "the departure of a customer is observed." Retention rate r_t = proportion of customers active at end of t minus 1 still active at end of t. Survivor function S(t) = r1 x r2 x ... x rt. Expected tenure = area under the survivor curve (sum of S(t)). CLV in a contractual setting is the discounted sum of margin times S(t), which correctly reflects non-constant retention.
   - Argues against "curve-fitting" regression (linear, quadratic, exponential and so on) to project retention; cites Berry and Linoff (2004) that parametric curves do not "work" for projecting survivor functions (some fitted curves go negative or rise over time).
   - Proposes the shifted-beta-geometric (sBG) model: each customer flips a constant-probability "churn coin" each period; churn probabilities differ across customers (beta distribution). Implementable in a spreadsheet; "remarkably accurate forecasts".
   - Key insight: observed aggregate retention rates rise over time even when each customer's churn probability is constant. This is "simply a sorting effect in a heterogeneous population": high-churn customers leave early. They call this the "ruse of heterogeneity" (citing Vaupel and Yashin, 1985).

# ORGANIZED NOTES

## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### A1. Survival analysis for churn (Kaplan and Meier, 1958; Cox, 1972; applied to customers by Fader and Hardie)
- **Origin [U on bibliographic details]:** Edward Kaplan and Paul Meier, "Nonparametric Estimation from Incomplete Observations," *Journal of the American Statistical Association*, 1958 (the Kaplan-Meier survival curve). David Cox, "Regression Models and Life-Tables," *Journal of the Royal Statistical Society B*, 1972 (proportional hazards: how a factor raises or lowers the hazard). Confirm before print.
- **Precise summary [V via lifelines, Fader and Hardie 2007]:** The **survival curve** S(t) shows the share of a starting group still a customer after t periods. The **hazard** h(t) is the chance of leaving in period t given the customer made it to t: the "risk right now." Expected tenure is the area under the survival curve. **Censoring:** customers who have not churned yet are not "lifetime unknown, so drop them"; they are survivors whose story is incomplete. Dropping them or treating today as their end date underestimates lifetime.
- **Questions it answers (plain language):** When in the lifecycle do customers leave (the hazard spike at first renewal, after onboarding, after a champion leaves)? Do segment A customers last longer than segment B? Did a change (new onboarding, a price change) shift the curve for cohorts after it? What tenure should the CLV model assume?
- **B2B contract churn vs usage churn (synthesis, author framing):** Contract churn is observed and lumpy: it happens at renewal dates, so the hazard is near zero mid-term and spikes at each renewal. This is Fader and Hardie's "contractual setting." Usage churn is unobserved: the account still pays but usage decays (seats idle, workloads moved). That behaves like their "noncontractual setting," where the firm must infer whether a customer is still "alive." B2B PMs need both curves: usage survival is the leading indicator; contract survival is the lagging, financial one. For usage-priced products (Snowflake style, Beacon's per-resolved-incident direction), usage decay is revenue churn directly.
- **The "ruse of heterogeneity" [V, Fader and Hardie 2007; Vaupel and Yashin 1985 cited there]:** Aggregate retention rises with tenure even if no individual becomes more loyal, because high-churn customers leave first. PM implication: do not read rising year-3 retention as "our product gets stickier"; segment before concluding. And do not project retention by fitting a trend line; Fader and Hardie show curve-fits break.

### A2. Cohort retention (logo, user, revenue)
- **Summary:** Group customers by start period (or by a change event) and track the share retained over time. Three lenses: logo (accounts), user or seat (active people), revenue (dollars). Retention curves that flatten indicate a durable core; curves decaying toward zero indicate weak fit (Rachitsky and Winters framing **[V]** on the flat-curve quote; the PMF framing is common practitioner wisdom **[S]**).
- **Template (retention triangle):** rows = cohort start month or quarter; columns = periods since start; cells = % retained (logo) and % revenue retained. Read down a column (are newer cohorts better?), along a row (where does decay stop?), and along diagonals (calendar events such as a price change hitting all cohorts at once).
- **B2B application:** Cohort by segment (ACV band, industry, partner vs direct, onboarding path) not just date. SaaS Capital's data **[V]** show ACV is the strongest single comparison axis.

### A3. Net revenue retention and gross revenue retention
- **Definitions [V, SaaS Capital 2025]:** NRR = recurring revenue now from customers who were customers 12 months ago, divided by their recurring revenue 12 months ago (includes expansion, contraction, churn, price increases). GRR = same, but expansion and price increases excluded, capped at 100%. Churn is roughly 1 minus GRR.
- **Variants [V, Snowflake 10-K]:** Consumption businesses use product revenue over trailing periods. Definitions vary by company; NRR is non-GAAP.
- **Benchmarks:** SaaS Capital 2025 private B2B medians 101% NRR, 91% GRR; above $250K ACV, GRR 95% **[V]**. Rachitsky and Winters 2020 enterprise SaaS NRR good 110%, great 130% **[V]**. Use only as orientation.
- **Decision use:** NRR without GRR hides a leaky bucket: 118% NRR can come from a few large expansions masking many small-logo losses. Always show NRR, GRR and logo retention together, by segment. Decompose NRR into: starting revenue, plus expansion, minus contraction, minus churn.

### A4. Customer lifetime value (Gupta, Lehmann and Stuart 2004; Fader and Hardie)
- **Simple model [S for exact form]:** CLV = margin per period x r / (1 + i minus r), with constant retention r and discount rate i. Gupta and Lehmann's "margin multiple" view: CLV is a multiple of annual margin that depends only on retention and discount rate. Customer value is "expected sum of discounted future earnings" [S].
- **Better model [V, Fader and Hardie 2007, 2010]:** CLV = discounted sum of margin x S(t) over the survival curve, using a model that allows heterogeneity (sBG for contracts). Single-rate CLV undervalues the existing base.
- **Pitfalls for B2B and AI products (author synthesis):** (1) Using revenue instead of contribution margin, especially after inference cost (Bessemer 2025 margin data **[V]**). (2) Using one blended retention rate across segments. (3) Projecting expansion forever (NRR above 100% implies infinite CLV in the naive formula). (4) Ignoring cost to serve (CSM time, support, partner margin). (5) Treating CLV as precise; present ranges. (6) Using CLV to justify acquisition spend without checking payback period. (7) For MSP partners, value sits at the partner and its end-customers: model both layers.
- **Predictive CLV:** probability models (sBG, BG/BB, Pareto/NBD lineage from Fader and Hardie) or machine learning per account. Predictive is worth it when decisions differ by account (whom to invest CSM time in), not for a board-level average.

### A5. Churn prediction and health scores
- **DEAR (Gainsight, Capote 2022) [V]:** Deployment, Engagement, Adoption, ROI, each with a guiding question (listed in source log item 10). Validate by checking component scores against actual renewals.
- **Health score definition template (author synthesis from Gainsight sources):** name; purpose (the decision it drives); population and segment variants; components with signal, source system, weight and threshold; overall bands (for example green, yellow, red); refresh cadence; owner; playbook per band; validation method (back-test against the last 4 to 8 quarters of renewals; report how many churned accounts were green 90 days earlier); review date.
- **Key caution, Ascarza 2018 [S paper / V press release]:** risk scores identify who may leave, not whom intervention helps. Run a small randomized retention pilot, measure response, target by expected lift. Targeting the highest-risk accounts can waste effort or even raise churn.
- **Other pitfalls (author synthesis):** label leakage (using signals that only appear after a customer has decided to leave, such as a cancellation ticket); scores that only measure usage (Gainsight warns not to rely on adoption alone **[S]**); score inflation when CSMs set their own sentiment input; no feedback loop.

### A6. Sentiment and loyalty metrics
- **NPS (Reichheld 2003; Net Promoter 3.0, 2021) [V]:** question, bands and formula in item 5.
- **NPS critiques:** Keiningham et al. 2007 found no clear superiority over satisfaction **[V summary]**; Sauro 2018 showed Reichheld's original analysis used historical growth, though NPS still explained a meaningful share of future growth in his re-analysis **[V]**; Reichheld and co-authors (2021) concede gaming, self-reporting and misuse, and propose Earned Growth Rate **[V]**. B2B-specific issues (author synthesis): small samples per account, the respondent is often not the buyer, one detractor at a key account matters more than ten promoters elsewhere; report account-level and role-level, not one company number.
- **CSAT:** transactional satisfaction ("How satisfied were you with ...?", usually 1 to 5), best right after an interaction. [U on any standard; no source fetched.]
- **CES (Dixon, Freeman and Toman 2010) [S for findings]:** effort predicts loyalty better than delight in service interactions.
- **Earned Growth Rate (Reichheld et al. 2021) [V concept, S formula]:** revenue growth from existing customers (NRR) plus new customers won by referral, as an accounting check on survey sentiment.

### A7. Text analytics with LLMs (2026)
- **Evidence [V]:** LLMs handle simple polarity well, struggle more with aspect-level and nuanced sentiment (Zhang et al. 2023/2024); coding quality varies widely by model and improves with fine-tuning or strong codebooks (von der Heyde et al. 2025); a 2025 pilot with a frontier model on a 40-plus-category codebook performed poorly, missing negative valence and sarcasm (Langer Research 2025).
- **Pattern [V, Anthropic Clio 2024]:** facet extraction, clustering, cluster summaries, hierarchy, minimum-size thresholds for privacy.
- **Verification protocol (author synthesis):** (1) Human-label a random sample (for example 100 to 200 items) before trusting any automated coding. (2) Measure agreement and look at disagreements, not just a score. (3) Check theme shares, not only labels (von der Heyde shows distributions can shift). (4) Test directionality and sarcasm explicitly. (5) Ask the harness to quote verbatims for each theme so a human can audit. (6) Keep a frozen codebook for trend lines; let the model propose new themes separately. (7) Strip personal data and respect customer contracts before sending text to a model.

### A8. Voice of customer (VoC) program
- **Components (author synthesis):** listening posts (surveys, tickets, calls, community, sales notes, churn interviews, product telemetry); a shared taxonomy; routing and closing the loop (inner loop: respond to the individual; outer loop: fix the root cause, a Bain Net Promoter System idea **[U]**); governance (who owns which theme); reporting tied to retention outcomes.
- **2026 shift:** VoC platforms now ship LLM topic detection with human approval of rules (Medallia 2025, 2026; Qualtrics 2026) **[S]**. The PM's job moves from reading verbatims to designing the taxonomy, checking the machine and connecting themes to revenue.

### A9. From analytics to strategy (decision mapping)
| Analysis | Strategy question | Typical decision |
|---|---|---|
| Survival by segment | Which customers stay? | Invest in or exit segments; change ICP |
| Cohort retention after a change | Did onboarding or pricing change help? | Keep, roll back or extend |
| NRR and GRR decomposition | Is growth from expansion masking churn? | Retention bet vs expansion bet |
| CLV by segment and channel | Where is a customer worth most after cost to serve? | Channel mix (direct vs MSP), CAC limits |
| Health score plus uplift pilot | Whom do we act on? | CS capacity allocation |
| Sentiment and VoC themes tied to churn | Which problems cost revenue? | Roadmap priority, pricing packaging |

### A10. Asking an AI harness for these analyses (author synthesis; no source)
- Request template: goal and decision; data sources by connector name and time window; population and exclusions; definitions (churn, active, revenue base, cohort start); method in plain words (for example "survival curve by segment, treating current customers as still active"); outputs (chart, table, one-paragraph finding, confidence); checks (row counts, reconcile to finance ARR within a tolerance, list assumptions, show 5 example accounts); what not to do (no fitted trend extrapolation, no dropping current customers).
