## Sources log (as gathered)

### 1. Lean Startup Co. / Eric Ries. "Principles." theleanstartup.com, undated page (accessed 2026-10-30). [V]
URL: https://theleanstartup.com/principles
- Five principles, exact names: (1) Entrepreneurs are everywhere. (2) Entrepreneurship is management. (3) Validated learning. (4) Innovation accounting. (5) Build-Measure-Learn.
- Build-Measure-Learn: turn ideas into products, measure how customers respond, then learn whether to pivot or persevere; all startup processes should accelerate the loop.
- MVP: the version of a product that lets a team start the loop with the least effort and start learning fastest.
- Innovation accounting: measuring progress, setting milestones and prioritizing work with actionable (cause and effect) metrics, not vanity metrics.
- Pivot: a structured course correction that tests a new fundamental hypothesis about product, strategy or engine of growth.
- Validated learning: the unit of progress; learning demonstrated empirically under extreme uncertainty.

### 2. Wikipedia. "Lean startup." (accessed 2026-10-30). [S]
URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_startup
- The Lean Startup (Ries) published September 2011; Ries coined "lean startup" on his blog in September 2008. Built on Steve Blank's customer development plus lean manufacturing and lean software development.
- MVP definition attributed to Ries: the version of a new product that lets a team collect the maximum amount of validated learning about customers with the least effort.
- Zappos origin story (Nick Swinmurn photographed shoe-store inventory and sold online before holding stock) is the standard "concierge/manual-first" MVP example. [S]
- Vanity vs actionable metrics: vanity metrics paint the rosiest picture without reflecting the business's drivers; a metric can be vanity for one firm and actionable for another.
- Criticisms (secondary summaries, useful for a balanced box): Ben Horowitz (2010) on "running fat" when speed to scale matters; Felin, Gambardella, Stern and Zenger argued lean methods bias toward incremental outcomes; Levinthal argued many ideas pre-existed in other literatures; a 2019 The Conversation piece named cases where it fits poorly (high prototyping cost or risk, tests costing more than they return, teams unable to interpret experiments).
- Government adoption mentioned: Presidential Innovation Fellows, Hacking for Defense.

### 3. Steve Blank. "Why the Lean Start-Up Changes Everything." Harvard Business Review, May 2013. [V for metadata and summary only; body paywalled]
URL: https://hbr.org/2013/05/why-the-lean-start-up-changes-everything
- Frames the traditional sequence (business plan, pitch, team, launch, sell) as the failure path; lean is replacing it for startups and for initiatives inside large corporations.
- Cites Shikhar Ghosh (HBS) research that 75% of start-ups fail. Flag: the 75% figure is Ghosh's definition (failing to return investors' capital) and is often misquoted; cite with care or omit.

### 4. Design Council (UK). "The Double Diamond." designcouncil.org.uk (20th anniversary page, 2023). [V]
URL: https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/our-resources/the-double-diamond/
- Codified in 2003 by Design Council staff (page marks the model's 20th anniversary).
- Four phases, exact names: Discover (understand the real problem by spending time with affected people), Define (reframe the challenge using discovery insight), Develop (generate varied solutions, co-design), Deliver (test at small scale, reject what fails, improve what works).
- Not linear: teams often loop back to the start of a diamond; the expanded "Framework for Innovation" adds idea making and testing at the front end.
- Page notes a newer Systemic Design Framework (no detail fetched).

### 5. Design Council (UK). "Framework for Innovation." designcouncil.org.uk. [V]
URL: https://www.designcouncil.org.uk/our-resources/framework-for-innovation/
- Four design principles, exact: Put people first; Communicate visually and inclusively; Collaborate and co-create; Iterate, iterate, iterate.
- Methods bank in three areas: Explore, Shape, Build. Plus Leadership (permits experimentation) and Engagement (involve those delivering and receiving).
- Two diamonds = divergent then convergent thinking, once on the problem and once on the solution.
- PM application: the first diamond is the problem space (discovery for a B2B buying group), the second the solution space. The most common enterprise failure is skipping the first diamond because a large customer "asked for" a feature.

### 6. Stanford d.school. "Design Thinking Bootleg." dschool.stanford.edu, 2018. [V]
URL: https://dschool.stanford.edu/resources/design-thinking-bootleg
- Five modes, exact: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test. The Bootleg is "a set of tools/methods that constantly evolves."
- Related d.school resource: Design Abilities (8 abilities). Do not claim the d.school replaced the modes; the page does not say so.

### 7. Tim Brown. "Design Thinking." Harvard Business Review, June 2008. [V metadata only]
URL: https://hbr.org/2008/06/design-thinking
- IDEO CEO's foundational article: design moving from late-stage aesthetics to the front of product, service, process and strategy work. The "three spaces" (inspiration, ideation, implementation) are widely attributed to this article and Brown's Change by Design (2009) [S, body not readable].

### 8. Rebecca Ackermann. "Design thinking was supposed to fix the world. Where did it go wrong?" MIT Technology Review, 9 Feb 2023. [V]
URL: https://www.technologyreview.com/2023/02/09/1067821/design-thinking-retrospective-what-went-wrong/
- Critiques: short, fresh-eyes engagements produce recommendations ungrounded in reality; "innovation theater" in corporations; social-impact pilots stall before scale.
- Lilly Irani (2018) argued it elevated designers as privileged interpreters and reinforced labor hierarchies. Former IDEO designer George Aye: consultants as "tourists."
- Failure cases: IDEO.org Diva Centres (Lusaka) scaling proved too expensive; SFUSD cafeteria redesign (2013) relied on connected vending machines many sites could not support.
- Responses: d.school's revamped degree materials drop the label "design thinking" and use "make" and "care" in place of "empathy"; Tim Brown says impact needs capability building and longer engagements.
- PM application: the critique maps directly onto B2B. A two-day workshop with no follow-through, no build and no metric is theater. Tie every design activity to an experiment card and a decision.

### 9. Strategyzer. "Testing Business Ideas" book page and "Validate your ideas with the Test Card." strategyzer.com (accessed 2026-10-30). [V for what is stated; template wording S]
URLs: https://www.strategyzer.com/library/testing-business-ideas-book ; https://www.strategyzer.com/library/validate-your-ideas-with-the-test-card
- Authors: David J. Bland, Alexander Osterwalder (with Alan Smith, designer). Book published by Wiley, 2019 [S: year and publisher not on page; widely cited].
- Book page says the library holds 43 experiment types, organized by cost, time and strength of evidence. Flag: many secondary sources say 44. Use "more than 40" in prose.
- Method: break an idea into prioritized, testable assumptions; integrates Assumptions Mapping with the Business Model Canvas and Value Proposition Canvas.
- Test Card makes four things explicit: the hypothesis that must hold; how you will test it; what you will measure; what success looks like including the threshold.
- Commonly reproduced Test Card wording [S]: "We believe that... To verify that, we will... And measure... We are right if..." Learning Card [S]: "We believed that... We observed... From that we learned that... Therefore, we will..." Treat exact field wording as widely reproduced; present as a template, not a quote.
- Book structure (from the course page): design hypotheses, design experiments that produce strong evidence, learn from experiments, make evidence-based decisions, manage and track experiments, share learning with decision makers.
- Three risk lenses (Strategyzer "critical hypotheses" article): Desirability (market risk: do they want it), Feasibility (can we build and deliver it), Viability (can it earn more than it costs). After each round, mark each hypothesis right, wrong or still unclear.
- Library structure in the book [S, from memory of the book; confirm before printing]: Discovery experiments (weaker evidence, cheap: customer interviews, discussion forums, search trend analysis, explainer video, paper prototype, boomerang, etc.) and Validation experiments (stronger evidence: concierge, Wizard of Oz, mock sale, letter of intent, presale, fake door / smoke test, A/B test, single-feature MVP, etc.).
- PM application (B2B): letters of intent, paid pilots, design-partner agreements and procurement-stage behavior are the strong-evidence B2B analogues of a presale. A customer saying "this is interesting" in a QBR is weak evidence.

### 11. IDEO. "Design Thinking." designthinking.ideo.com (accessed 2026-10-30). [V]
URL: https://designthinking.ideo.com/
- Definition: a human-centered approach to solving complex problems (paraphrase is safer than quote).
- Desirability (what people value), feasibility (what technology makes possible), viability (what the organization needs to succeed): the innovation sweet spot at the overlap.

### 12. GV. "The Design Sprint." gv.com/sprint (accessed 2026-10-30). [V]
URL: https://www.gv.com/sprint/
- Jake Knapp began running design sprints at Google in 2010 (Chrome, Search), brought them to GV in 2012. Contributors: Braden Kowitz (story-centered design), Michael Margolis (one-day customer research), John Zeratsky ("start at the end", key metrics), Daniel Burka.
- Five days: Monday map the challenge, set a long-term goal, ask experts, pick a target. Tuesday remix existing ideas, sketch solutions, start recruiting customers. Wednesday critique sketches, choose, storyboard. Thursday build a realistic facade prototype ("fake it"), write interview script. Friday interview customers and watch reactions.
- Book: Sprint (Knapp, Zeratsky, Kowitz), Simon and Schuster, 2016. Character.vc (Knapp and Zeratsky's current firm) lists a newer Foundation Sprint and the book Click. [V via https://www.character.vc/sprint]
- Book details [S, not on fetched pages, widely cited]: team of about seven or fewer, with a Decider and a Facilitator; five customer interviews on Friday ("five is the magic number" drawn from Nielsen's usability research).

### 13. Wikipedia. "Customer development." (accessed 2026-10-30). [S]
URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Customer_development
- Steve Blank developed it in the 1990s from patterns across his own startups; first set out in The Four Steps to the Epiphany (self-published 2005 [S]); operational guide in The Startup Owner's Manual (Blank and Bob Dorf, 2012 [S]).
- Four steps, exact: Customer Discovery, Customer Validation, Customer Creation, Company Building. The first two are "search"; the last two are "execute."
- Core idea: get out of the building; there are no facts inside the building. Treat the business model as a set of hypotheses tested with customers.
- A startup is not a smaller version of a large company: large companies execute a known business model, startups search for one. (Blank's definition of a startup as a temporary organization searching for a repeatable and scalable business model [S, widely attributed].)
- Lean LaunchPad adopted by 75+ universities; NSF adopted it for Innovation Corps (I-Corps) in 2011.
- PM application (B2B): in enterprise, "customer" is a buying group. Discovery must cover the user (Dana), champion (Priya), economic buyer (Marcus) and gatekeeper (Sam) separately; validation means a repeatable sales motion, not one enthusiastic design partner.

### 14. Wikipedia. "Pivot (business)." (accessed 2026-10-30). [S]
URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pivot_(business)
- Ries: a pivot is a structured course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis. Blank: a pivot changes the plan, not the executive.
- Groupon began as The Point (collective action platform) and pivoted to group coupons.

### 15. Eric Ries. "Pivot, don't jump to a new vision." Startup Lessons Learned blog, 22 Jun 2009. [V]
URL: https://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/06/pivot-dont-jump-to-new-vision.html
- Early pivot types: segment pivot (same product, different customers), customer problem pivot (same customers, different problem), feature pivot (the company focuses on one feature of the current product; became "zoom-in").
- Keep "one foot in the past": retain validated learning; jumping to an unrelated vision throws it away.
- Warning sign: chronic frustration of the solution team; experiments and funnel tweaks yield only modest gains. Ask: are we solving an important problem; are early adopters adopting; does the product deliver its promise?
- Recommends a regular strategic review meeting, e.g. once per development cycle, to make pivot calls more objectively. This is the origin of the "pivot or persevere meeting."
- Full catalogue in The Lean Startup (2011), chapter "Pivot (or Persevere)" [S, from the book; confirm wording]: zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture (high margin low volume vs low margin high volume), value capture (monetization), engine of growth (viral, sticky, paid), channel, technology. Ten types.
- Innovation accounting's three learning milestones, from the book [S]: (1) use an MVP to establish real baseline data; (2) tune the engine from the baseline toward the ideal in the business plan; (3) decide to pivot or persevere. Ries's three engines of growth [S]: sticky, viral, paid.

### 16. Eric Ries. "Minimum Viable Product: a guide." Startup Lessons Learned blog, 3 Aug 2009. [V]
URL: https://www.startuplessonslearned.com/2009/08/minimum-viable-product-guide.html
- MVP is not the smallest product you can build; it is the fastest route to validated learning. It adds overhead because the team must actually learn; if the goal is only a quick sale, an MVP is unnecessary.
- An AdWords smoke test would have revealed a bad concept faster than two weeks of building.
- MVP types to teach (common taxonomy; attribute generally, not to one source) [S]: smoke test / fake door (landing page or button that measures intent), explainer video (Dropbox, as told in The Lean Startup), concierge (deliver the service manually to a few customers, visibly human), Wizard of Oz (customer sees a product; humans operate it behind the curtain), single-feature MVP, piecemeal MVP (stitched from existing tools), presale / letter of intent.

## (a) Key concepts and frameworks (synthesis for the writer)

### Lean Startup (Eric Ries, 2008 blog, 2011 book)
- Unit of progress: validated learning. Loop: Build, Measure, Learn, but planned in reverse: decide what you need to learn, what you will measure to learn it, then the least you must build.
- MVP: the least effort that yields validated learning; not a small v1. MVP types (teach as a menu, pick by the riskiest assumption): fake door / smoke test, explainer video, concierge, Wizard of Oz, single feature, piecemeal (assembled from existing tools), presale or letter of intent.
- Innovation accounting (three milestones): baseline, tune the engine, pivot or persevere. Use actionable, accessible, auditable metrics (Ries's "three A's" [S]) and cohort views over cumulative totals.
- Pivot types (ten, from the book [S]): zoom-in, zoom-out, customer segment, customer need, platform, business architecture, value capture, engine of growth, channel, technology.
- Pivot-or-persevere meeting: scheduled, not ad hoc; Ries suggested once per development cycle (2009 blog).
- B2B and platform application: long sales cycles make "measure" slow. Use leading indicators that move within weeks (design-partner weekly active responders, diagnosis acceptance rate in pilots, security-review pass rate, procurement stage reached). Business architecture and value capture pivots are the commonest B2B pivots (for example per-environment to per-resolved-incident pricing).

### Customer development (Steve Blank, 1990s practice, 2005 book)
- Four steps: Customer Discovery, Customer Validation (search), Customer Creation, Company Building (execute). Iterate inside the search steps; a failed validation loops back to discovery (a pivot).
- Get out of the building. Business model as hypotheses (Blank pairs this with Osterwalder's Business Model Canvas [S]).
- B2B application: run discovery per buying-group role; validation means a repeatable, scalable sales process (several unaided wins), not one champion.

### Design thinking (IDEO; Stanford d.school; Design Council)
- IDEO: human-centered approach; sweet spot of desirability, feasibility, viability. Tim Brown, HBR 2008.
- d.school five modes: Empathize, Define, Ideate, Prototype, Test (Bootleg 2018). NN/g adds Implement and groups into Understand, Explore, Materialize.
- Double Diamond (Design Council, 2003): Discover, Define, Develop, Deliver; diverge then converge twice. Framework for Innovation adds four principles and leadership and engagement.
- Critiques: innovation theater, shallow fresh-eyes engagements, pilots that never scale, designers as privileged interpreters (Irani), weak evidence standards. Natasha Jen's 2017 talk "Design Thinking is Bullsh*t" (99U conference) and Lee Vinsel's 2017 essay are the best-known critiques [S: both pages unreachable; cite by name only]. d.school itself dropped the label in revamped degree materials (MIT Technology Review, 2023).
- How the methods fit: design thinking is strongest in the problem space (first diamond); lean startup in the solution and business-model space; customer development in the go-to-market search. Bland's Testing Business Ideas is the connective tissue (assumptions, experiments, evidence).

### Testing Business Ideas (David J. Bland and Alexander Osterwalder, Wiley 2019)
- Process: Design (shape idea, hypothesize, prioritize) then Test (experiment, learn, decide), looped.
- Assumptions mapping: importance vs evidence; test important-and-unproven first. Desirability, feasibility, viability lenses.
- Library of more than 40 experiments graded by cost, time and evidence strength, split into discovery and validation.
- Test Card and Learning Card templates (below).

### Design Sprint (Jake Knapp with John Zeratsky and Braden Kowitz; Google 2010, GV 2012, book 2016)
- Five days: Map, Sketch, Decide, Prototype, Test. Decider and Facilitator roles; about seven people; five customer interviews [S for roles and numbers].
- B2B application: recruit five people per persona that matters (do not mix the SRE lead and the VP in one interview set); a realistic facade of an admin console, approval flow or ROI report is enough.

### Maker culture and rapid experimentation
- Dale Dougherty, Make: (2005), Maker Faire (2006), makerspaces and fab labs. Lesson: make to think; cheap fabrication changes who gets to try ideas.
- 2026 equivalent: PMs and designers prototype working software with AI (Artifacts 2024, Figma Make 2025, Claude Code and agentic harnesses). The bottleneck moves from building to learning: recruiting real customers, defining success thresholds, and interpreting evidence.

## Artifact templates for the chapter (draft formats; author's own, built on the cited methods)

**Experiment card (after Bland's Test Card and Learning Card)**
- Experiment name / ID; owner; date; linked opportunity or bet.
- Riskiest assumption (desirability, feasibility, viability, or B2B adoptability).
- Test card: We believe that [assumption]. To verify that, we will [experiment type and audience]. And measure [metric]. We are right if [threshold decided before the test].
- Cost, time to result, evidence strength (weak, medium, strong).
- Learning card: We believed that... We observed... From that we learned... Therefore we will [persevere, pivot, run next test, stop].
- AI disclosure: what the AI harness generated (prototype, synthetic data, analysis) and what came from real customers.

**MVP plan**
- Hypothesis being tested and the leap-of-faith assumptions (value and growth hypotheses, Ries).
- MVP type chosen and why it is the cheapest route to that evidence.
- Who sees it (segment, persona, number), how recruited, consent and data handling (B2B: customer agreements, security review).
- Success and kill thresholds; time box; what you will deliberately not build.
- Exit: what happens to the MVP after the decision (destroy, harden, fold into roadmap).

**Innovation accounting dashboard**
- Milestone status: baseline set, engine tuning, next pivot-or-persevere date.
- Leading indicators by cohort (weekly), with baseline, current and target.
- Experiments run, assumptions validated or invalidated, cost per validated learning, cycle time from hypothesis to decision.
- Vanity metrics deliberately excluded (list them).

**Design sprint plan**
- Long-term goal; sprint questions; map; target customer and moment; Decider; Facilitator; team; experts for Monday; five recruited customers per key persona; prototype fidelity and tool (AI-built allowed, flag what is faked); interview script; scorecard.

**Pivot-or-persevere review**
- Cadence (every 6 to 8 weeks or per cycle); attendees (team, Decider, finance partner).
- Evidence summary from the dashboard; experiments since last review; what moved and what did not.
- Options: persevere, pivot (name the type), stop. Cost of each.
- Decision, rationale, what we keep (the "one foot in the past"), next milestone.

- Prototype-in-the-chat became normal: Claude Artifacts (21 Jun 2024), Figma Make on Claude 3.7 Sonnet (7 May 2025), Anthropic teams (designers, legal, data science) building working prototypes with Claude Code (24 Jul 2025). [V]

- Speed claims need measurement: METR RCT (10 Jul 2025) found experienced developers 19% slower with early-2025 AI while believing they were faster; METR now labels it out of date [V]. Teach this as a reason to run innovation accounting on the team's own process.
- Synthetic respondents: NN/g's Oct 2026 study shows mode collapse (about a dozen labels vs 50+ from humans) [V]. In experimentation terms, synthetic users can pilot a script or generate hypotheses but cannot count as evidence on a Learning Card.
- Quality and security risks of AI-built prototypes (reported Lovable exposure, Replit database deletion) [S]: a B2B prototype shown to customers must never touch real customer data without security review; label prototypes as facades.
- Implication for the chapter: when building is nearly free, the scarce resources are customer attention, crisp hypotheses and the discipline to kill ideas. Lean's "least you must build" becomes "least customer time you must spend."
