1. Cedric Chin, "The Amazon Weekly Business Review", Commoncog, published 2024-07-02, updated 2026-06-14. https://commoncog.com/the-amazon-weekly-business-review/ [S for Amazon facts; primary for Commoncog's own practice]
   - WBR answers three questions: what customers experienced last week, how the business did, whether it is on track for targets.
   - Framed as a process control tool for learning the causal structure of the business.
   - Metric owners review numbers daily, investigate exceptional variation, explain at department and company WBRs.
   - Controllable input metrics drive output metrics; outputs are shown but discussion centers on inputs. One process's output can be another's input. Some metrics are context only.
   - Exception-driven: routine variation gets a quick "nothing to see here"; for exceptions, the owner gives a root cause or says "we don't know and are still investigating".
   - Cadence described: Monday owners review; Tuesday department WBRs; Wednesday company WBR facilitated by Finance. 60 minutes (90 in holiday season). 400 to 500 metrics, every metric shown. Static deck; strategy taken offline.
   - 6-12 graph: trailing 6 weeks left, trailing 12 months right, prior-year line faded, targets marked, box score of key values. 6-12 table for several metrics. Targets come from OP1/OP2, converted to weekly values by Finance.
   - Metrics added when they reveal a real driver or after a blowup; removed or demoted to department WBR when no longer useful. Example: recruiting metrics added to WBR during a catalog crisis until it passed.
   - Commoncog itself runs 47 metrics in under 15 minutes (their own practice).
   - Caution: chasing noise when teams skip define/measure/analyze steps.

2. Atlassian Team Playbook, "DACI: a decision-making framework". https://www.atlassian.com/team-playbook/plays/daci (accessed 2026-10-30) [V]
   - Driver: coordinates stakeholders, gathers information, scopes, ensures a decision by the agreed date.
   - Approver: one person who makes the decision ("The one person (yes: one!) who makes the decision").
   - Contributors: subject knowledge, recommend; a voice but not a vote.
   - Informed: told the outcome; no voice, no vote.
   - Play steps: prep doc; assign Driver; assign Approver and Contributors; assign Informed; action plan; gather input and decide; organize actions; share outcome. Prep 15 min, run 60 min, 3 to 6 people.
   - Template fields: Details (status, impact, people, due date, outcome), Background (1 to 2 sentences), Relevant data, Decision factors (criteria such as cost, time, scalability), Options considered (table: description, pros, cons, estimated resources and cost), Action items, Outcome.
   - The page cites a McKinsey survey claiming a "25% higher success rate" for DACI projects. NOT VERIFIED; see flags. Page does not state DACI's origin.

3. Jeff Bezos, 2017 Letter to Shareholders, Amazon (published April 2018). https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2017-letter-to-shareholders [V]
   - Amazon does not use slide presentations; uses narratively structured six-page memos read silently at the start of meetings ("study hall").
   - Great memos are written, rewritten, shared for feedback, set aside, edited again; "a great memo probably should take a week or more".
   - Memo quality varies; readers recognize a great one by its effect.
   - (Fetch summary mentioned an "authorless memo" tradition. Not confirmed in letter text; treat as unverified and do not use.)

4. Jeff Bezos, 2016 Letter to Shareholders, Amazon (April 2017). https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/company-news/2016-letter-to-shareholders [V]
   - High-velocity decisions: Day 2 companies make high-quality decisions slowly; need quality and velocity.
   - Most decisions made with "somewhere around 70% of the information you wish you had".
   - "Disagree and commit"; applies to bosses too (his Amazon Studios example).
   - "recognize true misalignment issues early and escalate them immediately"; otherwise decided by exhaustion ("whoever has more stamina carries the decision").

5. Jeff Bezos, 2015 Letter to Shareholders, Amazon (April 2016), SEC exhibit 99.1. https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1018724/000119312516530910/d168744dex991.htm [V]
   - Type 1 decisions: "consequential and irreversible or nearly irreversible"; slow, deliberate, consultative.
   - Type 2: reversible; should be made quickly by individuals or small groups.
   - Risk: large orgs use the heavyweight Type 1 process on most decisions, leading to slowness, risk aversion, too little experimentation, "diminished invention".

6. Working Backwards LLC (Colin Bryar and Bill Carr), "Operating Planning and Cadence" concept page. https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/amazon-operating-cadence/ (no byline or date; site (c) 2021 to 2026; accessed 2026-10-30) [V for authors' own description; Amazon practice is their account]
   - Calendar-year example: June CEO/CFO top-down guidance ("Investment Envelope": targets for metrics, initiatives, resources), about 2 weeks. Jul/Aug optional offsites and vision docs. Sep/Oct teams write OP1 (about 4 weeks); Finance loads plans. Oct/Nov OP1 reviews with division exec then CEO and exec team (1 to 4 weeks). Nov CEO finalizes OP1 P&L, decisions, goals. Nov/Dec board review. January: exec team reviews variance vs Q4 actuals and produces OP2, the plan of record.
   - Top-down targets meet bottom-up plans at the OP1 review, where initiatives are funded, cut or modified.
   - Plan = Metrics, Initiatives, Resources, Org Structure. Template: introduction; metrics table; prior-year review; key initiatives; resources; appendices (P&L, initiative table, headcount, roadmap, FAQs).
   - More than 5 and fewer than 20 key metrics (about 8 to 15 typical). Initiatives SMART with leader, dates, projected metric impact, resources.
   - S-Team goals: roughly 15% of reviewed initiatives and metrics designated; S-Team spent about 75% of meeting time on them; progress reported quarterly. Cites 2009 letter: 452 detailed goals for 2010 (see source 10 for check).
   - Mid-year change control: changing OP goals or adding resources needed S-Team or CEO approval; friction deliberate. Adding initiatives possible with VP/SVP/CXO sign-off.
   - WBR weekly; MBR/QBR review progress vs OP goals and proposed plan changes; PR/FAQ continuous, feeding planning.

7. Working Backwards LLC, "Quarterly and Monthly Business Reviews". https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/quarterly-monthly-business-reviews/ (no byline/date; accessed 2026-10-30) [V]
   - M/QBR: each P&L or operating unit reports prior month/quarter results against the annual operating plan.
   - Narrative, "6 pages or fewer" plus appendix tables; subheadings, page and line numbers; charts in appendix; small metric snapshots under 15% of a page allowed.
   - Held 5 to 10 business days after period end. One hour with CEO, leader's directs, chosen stakeholders. Written by group leader with team and finance partner.
   - Contents: (1) table of key output and input metrics vs plan and prior year with prose commentary, about 80% focus on controllable inputs; (2) 3 to 5 key initiatives with red/yellow/green, linked input metrics, headcount; (3) updates to business assumptions and proposed course changes; (4) resource status: hiring, fixed and variable opex vs budget; (5) appendix.

8. Working Backwards LLC, "Input Metrics". https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/input-metrics/ [V]
   - Controllable inputs: actions the team controls (add selection, lower cost to lower price, position inventory). Outputs: lagging (revenue, gross profit, FCF, churn, CSAT); "generally can't be directly manipulated sustainably over time".
   - Metric owners are the business leaders, not analysts; prepare in advance, know which metrics to skip and which to explain. Department WBR precedes company WBR. Ties to "Dive Deep".
   - The selection-metric evolution story and DMAIC lifecycle are in the book (Bryar and Carr, Working Backwards, St. Martin's Press, 2021, ch. 6) [B], not on this page. Book account as commonly summarized: selection measured first as count of detail pages, then pages with in-stock inventory, then in-stock and ready for two-day shipping ("Fast Track In Stock"). Mark [B]; author must check against the book before using.

9. Working Backwards LLC, "Narratives and Decision Making". https://workingbackwards.com/concepts/narratives-decision-making/ [V]
   - Slide meetings about 75% presenting, 25% discussing; narratives invert: about 25% reading, 75% discussion. Draws on Edward Tufte's critique of slides for small-group decisions.
   - Six pages or fewer for a 60-minute meeting; three or fewer for 30 minutes. Reading time about 15 to 20 min (six pages), 7 to 10 (three).
   - Prose, full sentences; tables and charts allowed; appendix optional and must not hold required reading; FAQs anticipate questions; tenets used sparingly.
   - Narrative types: OP1, MBR/QBR, PR/FAQ, COE (correction of error), BRD, SOP.
   - Meeting flow: inline comments while reading; team answers in order; most senior leader speaks last.
   - Reader lenses: facts and truth, strategic vision, metrics and initiatives, achievability, resource allocation.
   - Narratives suit 5 to 20 people making decisions; slides suit one-to-many broadcast.
   - Usually one primary author working with the team; cannot be written the night before.

Also on site (not fetched in detail): open-source WBR App that builds an HTML 6-12 deck from metric data, https://workingbackwards.com/wbr-app/ and https://github.com/working-backwards/wbr-app [V existence via resources page].

10. BCG, "The Growth-Share Matrix" (history page). https://www.bcg.com/about/overview/our-history/growth-share-matrix (accessed 2026-10-30) [V]
   - First sketched by BCG's Alan Zakon (later CEO), refined with colleagues; popularized by Bruce Henderson's 1970 essay "The Product Portfolio".
   - Cash cows (low growth, high share): milk for cash to reinvest. Stars (high growth, high share): invest significantly. Question marks (high growth, low share): invest or discard depending on odds of becoming stars. Pets (low growth, low share): liquidate, divest or reposition.
   - BCG page itself does not use "invest/maintain/harvest/divest" labels; that four-verb vocabulary is common practitioner usage. Book should present invest/maintain/harvest/divest as a generic portfolio posture set, crediting BCG for the portfolio-as-cash-allocation idea, not claiming BCG coined these four words.

11. Bain and Company, "RAPID: Bain's tool to clarify decision accountability", 2023-10-13. https://www.bain.com/insights/rapid-tool-to-clarify-decision-accountability/ [V]
   - Recommend (one recommender, drives the proposal), Agree (sparing; often legal or regulatory must-haves; decider breaks deadlock), Perform (executes; assign promptly), Input (expertise; not a vote), Decide (one decider ideally; if a group, agree tie-break rule up front).
   - Start with high-value or high-frequency decisions; not every decision needs formal roles.
   - Use as a comparison to DACI: RAPID adds an explicit Agree (veto-like) role and a Perform role.

## (a) Key concepts and frameworks for the chapter

**Governance that helps vs bureaucracy.** Test every forum against Bezos's "process as proxy" warning (src 4/22) and his Type 1/Type 2 split (src 5): heavyweight review for one-way doors only. A forum earns its place if it changes a decision, a resource or a metric owner's next action. Retire any recurring review whose last three sessions changed nothing.

**Weekly business review (Bryar and Carr, src 1, 6 to 8).** Format: metric owners (leaders, not analysts) present; outputs shown, inputs discussed; exception-driven ("nothing to see here" vs root cause vs "still investigating"); 6-12 charts with prior year and plan; static pack; strategy taken offline; department WBR before company WBR. Add metrics when they reveal a driver; demote them when they stop doing so. Pair with Wheeler's routine vs exceptional variation (src 24).
WBR template for the book (PM-adapted):
1. Header: week, pack owner, data freshness timestamp, sources used.
2. Customer section: what customers experienced (top 3 events, incidents, escalations).
3. Output metrics vs plan and prior year (shown, not debated).
4. Controllable input metrics, one row per owner: value, vs plan, vs last 6 weeks, flag (routine / exceptional), owner note in one of three forms: nothing to see; cause found plus action; unknown plus next step and date.
5. Open actions from last week with status.
6. Items taken offline (with owner and forum).

**Monthly product review / MBR and QBR (src 7).** Narrative, six pages or fewer, held 5 to 10 business days after period close. Sections: metrics table vs plan and prior year with commentary (about 80% on controllable inputs); 3 to 5 initiatives with RAG status, input metric and headcount; assumption changes and proposed course corrections; resources vs budget; appendix. For a product org, the monthly review is the PM's; the internal QBR rolls several products up to the portfolio leader.
QBR outline (internal, if a deck is mandated): 1 summary page in prose; 2 outputs vs OP plan; 3 inputs and what moved them; 4 initiatives RAG; 5 changed assumptions; 6 asks and decisions needed; 7 risks top five; 8 next-quarter commitments; appendix. Rule: decisions and asks up front.

**Annual planning, OP1/OP2 (src 6, 21).** Top-down envelope, bottom-up plans, OP1 review to fund/cut, OP2 in January after Q4 actuals. Plan = metrics, initiatives, resources, org. 8 to 15 key metrics; SMART initiatives with owner, date, metric impact, resources. Mid-year changes need senior approval by design. Amazon's 2010 goals: 452, 360 customer-facing, few financial words.
Annual plan narrative template: introduction and one-paragraph thesis; last year in review (goals hit and missed, lessons); metrics table (outputs and inputs with targets by quarter); key initiatives (SMART, owner, metric moved, cost); resources and headcount; what we will not do; risks and dependencies; FAQ; appendix (P&L, initiative table, roadmap).

**Portfolio reviews (BCG growth-share, src 10; three horizons flagged in (f)).** Postures: invest, maintain, harvest, divest. For each product or bet: market growth, position, unit economics (gross margin after inference cost), strategic fit, posture, posture trigger (what evidence would move it). Platform caveat: a low-share component may be a dependency for others; price the cascade before divesting.
Portfolio review template: one row per line or bet with: posture now; proposed posture; evidence; investment this year and next; dependency notes; decision needed; Approver.

**Metric reviews.** Separate from the WBR: quarterly check that each metric still has an owner, a definition, a source, a known normal range, and still predicts the output. Ask: which input moved without the output following? (Bryar and Carr's metric evolution story, [B].)

**Decision logs and DACI (Atlassian, src 2, 12; RAPID comparison, src 11).** One Approver. Record: decision, date, status, Driver, Approver, Contributors, Informed, background, options (at least three) with pros, cons, cost, criteria, outcome, door type (one-way or two-way), revisit date. Add a Bezos 70% check ("what info would change this?").
Decision log row: ID; date; decision in one sentence; door type; DACI names; options considered; evidence links; outcome; reversal trigger; revisit date.

**Risk registers (NIST AI RMF, src 13; Orange Book, src 18).** Row: ID; risk statement (cause, event, consequence); category (incl. AI risk mapped to Govern/Map/Measure/Manage); likelihood; impact; owner; mitigations; trigger/early indicator; status; escalation threshold; last reviewed.

**Escalation paths (Bezos 2016, src 4; Google SRE, src 17).** Escalate misalignment early rather than by exhaustion. Policy: objective triggers (customer-visible, second team needed, unresolved after a set time, plan-of-record change, budget variance threshold, regulatory exposure); levels and named roles; time to respond at each level; required packet (one-page narrative: issue, options, recommendation, ask); explicit handoff; disagree-and-commit once decided; log the result.

**Narratives vs slides (src 3, 9).** Six pages for 60 minutes, three for 30; silent reading; inline comments; senior speaks last; appendix is never required reading. Slides only for one-to-many broadcasts (all-hands, customer QBR).

**AI-generated review packs and verification.** Harness with connectors (MCP, src 14) pulls metrics, tickets, CRM notes; a team skill (src 25) holds the recipe; citations (src 15) tie each number and claim to a source. Verification protocol for the book: (1) every number cites a source system and timestamp; (2) owner reconciles headline numbers against the system of record before circulation; (3) any causal claim ("X drove Y") is labelled hypothesis unless the owner confirms; (4) spot-check a fixed sample of citations; (5) disclose AI drafting and name the human accountable, as Microsoft's report did (src 16); (6) owner, not the model, writes the variance explanation's final sentence. Cautionary cases: Deloitte Australia report (src 19); summarization error rates (src 26).

## (e) Beacon worked example ideas (consistent with beacon-case.md)

- **Beacon WBR**: output metrics shown (ARR growth, NRR 118%, gross margin 72%); controllable inputs discussed: diagnosis acceptance rate, autonomous resolution rate, alert-to-incident compression, weekly active responders, inference cost per resolved incident. North Star: incidents resolved with Beacon's help per week. Example exception: acceptance rate dips after a model change; owner says "unknown, eval run due Thursday". Do not invent actual weekly values beyond illustrative ones clearly labelled.
- **Hallucinated root cause storyline**: enters the risk register, triggers an escalation (customer-visible, second team needed), produces a COE-style narrative and a decision-log entry (door type: one-way for widening the autonomous allowlist; two-way for prompt changes).
- **Pricing move to per-resolved-incident**: Type 1 decision run through DACI; Approver is VP of Product for Operations; Contributors: finance, PMM, sales; Informed: partners (Leo's MSP channel).
