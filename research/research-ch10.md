## Part B. Verified facts (log, appended as found)

### B1. Regulation of dark patterns

- **FTC staff report "Bringing Dark Patterns to Light" (September 2022) [V].** Primary PDF: https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/P214800%20Dark%20Patterns%20Report%209.14.2022%20-%20FINAL.pdf . Grew out of an FTC public workshop on 29 April 2021. Credits Harry Brignull with coining "dark patterns" in 2010. Appendix A definition (paraphrase): design practices that trick or manipulate users into choices they might not otherwise have made. The report organizes its discussion under four headings: (I) design elements that induce false beliefs; (II) design elements that hide or delay disclosure of material information; (III) design elements that lead to unauthorized charges; (IV) design elements that obscure or subvert privacy choices. On subscriptions, it restates the ROSCA expectation that cancellation be at least as easy as sign-up, through the same medium, and warns against forcing save attempts on people trying to cancel. Commission vote to release was 5-0 on 15 September 2022 [S, law-firm summaries].
- **EU Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065), Article 25 "Online interface design and organisation" [V on SpringLex consolidated text, OJ L 277, 27.10.2022].** https://www.springlex.eu/en/packages/dsa/dsa-regulation/article-25/ . 25(1): providers of online platforms must not design, organise or operate interfaces in a way that deceives or manipulates recipients or otherwise materially distorts or impairs their ability to make free and informed decisions. 25(2): does not apply to practices already covered by the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive (2005/29/EC) or the GDPR. 25(3): Commission may issue guidelines, notably on (a) giving more prominence to certain choices; (b) repeatedly asking for a choice already made, especially via pop-ups; (c) making termination harder than subscribing. Recital 67 carries the description of dark patterns [S]. Scope note: Article 25 binds *online platforms* (a DSA category), not every SaaS vendor. A B2B SaaS console is usually not an "online platform" under the DSA; the UCPD and GDPR still apply where consumers or personal data are involved. [U: legal reading, flag for counsel review.]
- **Amazon Prime settlement (25 September 2025) [S, multiple news outlets; ftc.gov release not fetched].** $2.5 billion total: $1 billion civil penalty plus $1.5 billion consumer refunds, resolving the FTC's 2023 complaint under the FTC Act and ROSCA alleging confusing enrollment flows and a hard-to-cancel process. Amazon did not admit wrongdoing. Required changes reported: clear button to decline Prime, clear disclosure of material terms, cancellation via the same method used to sign up. Sources: https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/news/amazon-prime-membership-ftc-2-5-billion-settlement-customer-refunds ; https://www.thewrap.com/amazon-ftc-prime-enrollment-settlement . A September 2026 report of higher per-person payments (afrotech.com) is unconfirmed; do not use.
- **FTC "click-to-cancel" Negative Option Rule vacated [S].** The Eighth Circuit vacated the rule in July 2025 (most sources say 8 July 2025) on procedural grounds: the FTC skipped the preliminary regulatory analysis required when a rule's annual effect exceeds $100 million. The court did not reach the merits. ROSCA and state auto-renewal laws still apply. A petition (Consumer Federation of America and American Economic Liberties Project) asked the FTC to reopen rulemaking in late 2025; a February 2026 law-firm alert reports a draft ANPRM. Sources: https://www.beneschlaw.com/publication/eighth-circuit-cancels-ftcs-click-to-cancel-rule/ ; https://www.kelleydrye.com/viewpoints/blogs/ad-law-access/ftc-quietly-reopens-click-to-cancel-rulemaking-in-response-to-petition . Status as of October 2026 not confirmed.
- **EU Digital Fairness Act (DFA) [S].** Commission consultation ran in 2025; proposal expected Q4 2026 (one firm cites 11 November 2026). Expected scope: dark patterns, addictive design, subscription traps, unfair personalisation, influencer marketing. Reports of internal Commission disagreement on scope. As of 30 October 2026 treat as *not yet proposed*. Sources: https://datamatters.sidley.com/2025/08/01/eu-consults-on-digital-fairness-act-big-changes-ahead-for-consumer-facing-platforms/ ; https://cms.law/en/aut/legal-updates/inside-the-eu-s-planned-digital-fairness-act-digital-design-subscription-traps-and-influencer-marketing .

### B2. Behavioral science evidence (with replication status)

- **Choice overload, original [S].** Iyengar and Lepper, "When Choice Is Demotivating," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79(6), 995 to 1006, 2000. Tasting booth with 24 versus 6 jams: more shoppers stopped at the 24 display (about 60% versus 40%), but about 3% of samplers bought from the 24 display versus about 30% from the 6 display. Single store, two Saturdays.
- **Choice overload, meta-analysis 1 [S, abstract reproduced consistently].** Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd, "Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload," *Journal of Consumer Research* 37(3), 409 to 425, 2010. 63 conditions from 50 experiments (N = 5,036). Mean effect virtually zero with considerable variance; preconditions found but no sufficient conditions. https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/30861/
- **Choice overload, meta-analysis 2 [S].** Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman, "Choice overload: A conceptual review and meta-analysis," *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 25(2), 2015. Effect is real under four moderators: choice set complexity, decision task difficulty, preference uncertainty, decision goal (for example, choosing to buy versus browsing). Reconciles the two camps: overload is conditional, not universal.
- **Loss aversion, contested [S].** Gal and Rucker, "The loss of loss aversion: Will it loom larger than its gain?" *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 28(3), 497 to 516, 2018: argue loss aversion was overgeneralized into a law. Mrkva, Johnson, Gächter and Herrmann, "Moderating loss aversion," *JCP* 30(3), 407 to 428, 2020: loss aversion is real but moderated; more domain knowledge and experience reduce it, older consumers show more. https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/moderating-loss-aversion-loss-aversion-has-moderators-but-reports/ . B2B implication: expert buyers are less loss averse about *product attributes* but organizations are strongly averse to *career and operational risk* (see B2B section).
- **Defaults [S].** Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber and Johnson, "When and why defaults influence decisions: a meta-analysis of default effects," *Behavioural Public Policy* 3(2), 159 to 186, 2019. 58 studies, 73,675 participants; d = 0.68 (95% CI 0.53 to 0.83); preselected option chosen about 27% more often; large heterogeneity; consumer-domain defaults stronger, environmental weaker. https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/bpubpo/v3y2019i02p159-186_00.html
- **Nudges overall, publication bias [V].** Mertens et al. (PNAS 2022) estimated d = 0.43 for choice-architecture interventions. Maier et al., "No evidence for nudging after adjusting for publication bias," *PNAS* 119(31), e2200300119, 2022, corrected the overall estimate to d = 0.04 (0.00 to 0.14); heterogeneity remains, so some nudges may work; "structure" interventions (which include defaults) were undecided at d = 0.12. Authors later said "no overall evidence" would have been a better title. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9351501/ ; https://www.bayesianspectacles.org/rejoinder-no-evidence-for-nudging/
- **Anchoring [S].** Tversky and Kahneman, "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases," *Science* 185(4157), 1124 to 1131, 1974. Wheel of fortune rigged to stop at 10 or 65; median estimates of the share of African countries in the UN were 25 versus 45. Many Labs 1 (Klein et al., 2014, *Social Psychology*) replicated anchoring robustly, one of the strongest effects in the set [S]. Anchoring is among the better-replicated biases; its mechanism (selective accessibility versus insufficient adjustment) is contested.

### B4. Trust in AI and automation

- **Lee and See, "Trust in Automation: Designing for Appropriate Reliance," *Human Factors* 46(1), 50 to 80, 2004 [V2: abstract and course copy].** Trust guides reliance when complexity makes full understanding impractical. Goal is *calibrated* trust (trust matches actual trustworthiness), not more trust. Miscalibration yields misuse (over-reliance) or disuse (under-reliance). Components: calibration, resolution, specificity.
- **Algorithm aversion [S].** Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, "Algorithm Aversion: People Erroneously Avoid Algorithms After Seeing Them Err," *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 144(1), 114 to 126, 2015: people lose confidence in an algorithm faster than in a human after seeing the same error. Follow-up, Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey, *Management Science* 64(3), 1155 to 1170, 2018: people use imperfect algorithms more if they can modify the output, even slightly. https://wpa.wharton.upenn.edu/content/academic-papers/overcoming-algorithm-aversion-people-will-use-imperfect-algorithms-can-even-slightly-modify/
- **Algorithm appreciation [S].** Logg, Minson and Moore, "Algorithm appreciation: People prefer algorithmic to human judgment," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 151, 90 to 103, 2019: across six experiments lay people weighted algorithmic advice more than human advice; experts discounted it. The two literatures coexist: appreciation before error, aversion after visible error.
- **Human-AI combinations [V on abstract; numbers S].** Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone, "When combinations of humans and AI are useful: A systematic review and meta-analysis," *Nature Human Behaviour*, published 28 October 2024. Over 100 experiments, over 300 effect sizes. On average human-AI combinations performed worse than the best of humans or AI alone (reported Hedges' g = -0.23 [S]); losses in decision tasks, gains in content-creation tasks; when the human alone beat the AI, the combination tended to gain; when the AI alone beat the human, the combination tended to lose. https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.06087
- **EU AI Act, Article 14(4)(b) [V on artificialintelligenceact.eu].** Human oversight measures for high-risk systems must enable people to remain aware of the tendency to over-rely on system output, which the Act itself names "automation bias", in particular where the system provides information or recommendations for decisions taken by people. The site lists applicability as 2 December 2027 (Annex III) and 2 August 2028 (Annex I), reflecting the delay agreed in the EU "digital omnibus" package; the original date was 2 August 2026. https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/14/ [Flag: confirm the omnibus amendment is final in the Official Journal.]
- **EU AI Act, Article 5(1)(a) and (b) [V on artificialintelligenceact.eu].** Prohibits AI systems that deploy subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques that materially distort behavior and impair informed decisions in a way likely to cause significant harm, and AI that exploits vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation. Applies from 2 February 2025. https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/5/ . This is the AI-era extension of dark-pattern law.

### B6. Persuasion, decision psychology and AI-assisted research

- **Cialdini's principles [V on Influence at Work].** Reciprocity, scarcity, authority, consistency, liking, social proof (also called consensus), plus unity added later as a seventh. Social proof: under uncertainty people look to what others, especially similar others, do. https://www.influenceatwork.com/7-principles-of-persuasion/ . *Influence* first published 1984; *Influence, New and Expanded* (2021) added unity [U on edition details].
- **Kahneman, System 1 and System 2 [S].** Nobel lecture "Maps of Bounded Rationality," 8 December 2002, Stockholm; revised in *American Economic Review* 93(5), 2003. System 1: fast, automatic, effortless, associative, hard to control. System 2: slower, serial, effortful, deliberately controlled. Labels credited to Stanovich and West (year cited inconsistently across copies, 2000 is the usual citation). System 2 monitors System 1 but the monitoring is normally lax. Popularized in *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011). Lecture: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/economic-sciences/2002/kahneman/lecture/ (403 to fetcher).
- **Johnson and Goldstein, "Do Defaults Save Lives?" *Science* 302(5649), 1338 to 1339, 2003 [S].** Organ-donor consent rates far higher in opt-out countries than opt-in countries. Nuance: consent rates differ much more than actual donation rates; use as an illustration of default power on *stated choice*, not as proof of outcomes.
- **NN/g on AI research tools [V].** Liu and Moran, "AI-Powered Tools for UX Research in 2023: Issues and Limitations," 2 July 2023: tools missed visual context, gave vague and unprioritized findings, could not take study goals, and often lacked citations back to sessions; advice is to check AI output and not use it where checking is impossible. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ai-powered-tools-limitations/ . Moran and Rosala, "Accelerating Research with AI," 27 September 2024, last reviewed 21 January 2026: delegate transcription, first-pass summaries, initial grouping and PII redaction (with review); treat AI coding as an initial pass; keep interpretation and fact-checking with the researcher; spot-check for fabricated data. https://www.nngroup.com/articles/research-with-ai/
- **Requirements traceability [V2: UCL abstract].** Gotel and Finkelstein, "An Analysis of the Requirements Traceability Problem," Proceedings of the First International Conference on Requirements Engineering (ICRE), 94 to 101, 1994. Traceability is the ability to follow a requirement's life forwards and backwards. Pre-RS traceability covers its life before entering the specification (where it came from, who asked, why); post-RS covers what happens after (design, code, tests). Based on work with over 100 practitioners, most traceability problems come from weak *pre-RS* traceability. https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/749/ . This is the academic anchor for "insight to spec" traceability.
- **Point-of-view statement [S, Stanford course materials].** d.school POV combines user, need and insight. Common template: "[User] needs [need, as a verb] because [surprising insight]." The insight should be a synthesized finding to design against, not just a reason. https://hci.stanford.edu/courses/cs147/2024/au/lectures/03-define.pdf
- **Positioning and repositioning, April Dunford [V on aprildunford.com].** Five components in order: competitive alternatives, differentiated capabilities, value, target customers (segmentation), market category. Reposition when unexpected competitors show up in deals, when value resonates with a different segment than planned, or when entering new segments. https://www.aprildunford.com/post/a-quickstart-guide-to-positioning . Book: *Obviously Awesome* (2019); *Sales Pitch* (2023) [U on years].

## Part A. Key concepts and frameworks

### A1. Decision psychology for product work

**Dual-process thinking (Kahneman, building on Stanovich and West).** System 1 produces fast, associative judgments; System 2 checks them, slowly and lazily. Product implication: most in-product choices (clicking a default, accepting a suggestion, skimming a pricing page) are System 1 decisions; most enterprise purchase decisions are System 2 rituals wrapped around System 1 impressions. Teach the two systems as a *model*, not as brain anatomy; Kahneman presented them as useful characters, not physical systems [U: wording in *Thinking, Fast and Slow*].
- *B2B application:* Design the 3 a.m. responder flow for System 1 (one clear next action, evidence visible without effort); design the buyer's evaluation materials for System 2 (comparable claims, proof, risk controls).

**Loss aversion (Kahneman and Tversky, prospect theory, 1979).** Losses loom larger than equivalent gains. *Nuance for the book:* Gal and Rucker (2018) argue it was overgeneralized; Mrkva et al. (2020) show it is real but moderated, smaller for experienced people, larger for older people. Teach it as conditional.
- *B2B application:* Framing a migration as "keep what you have" versus "lose what you have" matters less for expert evaluators than for end users. The stronger B2B force is *risk aversion to blame*: the buyer fears a visible failure more than they value an invisible gain [U: book's synthesis; no single study cited].

**Anchoring (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974).** An initial number biases later estimates, even when it is arbitrary. One of the more robust effects in replication projects.
- *B2B application:* The first price, the first ROI number or the first "time saved" figure in a deal sets the reference point for the whole negotiation. Anchor value on the customer's own cost of the problem, verified with their data, never on an invented figure.

**Choice overload (Iyengar and Lepper, 2000; contested).** Famous jam study, then two meta-analyses: Scheibehenne et al. (2010) found an average effect near zero; Chernev, Böckenholt and Goodman (2015) found the effect appears when choice sets are complex, tasks are hard, preferences are uncertain and the goal is to choose rather than browse. Teach: "more options hurt *only under conditions you can diagnose*."
- *B2B application:* Pricing pages with many tiers and add-ons, integration catalogs and configuration screens meet all four conditions for a new buyer. Experts with clear preferences do not suffer; give them full control and give novices a recommended path.

**Defaults (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003; Jachimowicz et al., 2019).** Defaults are among the strongest choice-architecture tools (meta-analytic d = 0.68, with wide spread). Mechanisms the meta-analysis discusses: endorsement (the default signals a recommendation), endowment (it feels like the status quo), effort (changing costs work) [S: mechanism list from the paper's framing]. Overall nudge effects shrink sharply after publication-bias correction (Maier et al., 2022), but structural nudges like defaults stay undecided rather than refuted.
- *B2B application:* In enterprise software, defaults are policy. A default autonomy level, a default data-retention setting or a default alert threshold is a decision the vendor makes for thousands of customers. Treat defaults as product requirements with an owner and a rationale.

**Social proof (Cialdini).** Under uncertainty people copy similar others.
- *B2B application:* Peer references, customer advisory boards and "teams like yours" benchmarks are the legitimate forms. Fake activity counters and fabricated reviews are named deceptive patterns (Brignull) and enforcement targets (FTC).

### A2. B2B buyer decision-making

- **Consensus buying.** Gartner research describes buying groups of roughly 6 to 10 decision makers for complex purchases, with only 17% of buying time spent with suppliers [S]. Buyers increasingly prefer to self-serve: 61% (2025 release) then 67% (2026 release) prefer a rep-free experience; 45% used AI tools in a recent purchase [S].
- **Implications to teach:** (1) The insight unit is the *buying group*, not the persona: map who decides, who vetoes (security, procurement, legal) and who uses. (2) Risk reduction often beats value addition: reversibility, pilots, audit logs, exit clauses. (3) Buyers now research through AI assistants, so product claims must survive being summarized by a model: specific, evidenced, consistent across documents [U: book's inference from the 45% figure]. (4) Self-serve buying may raise regret (Gartner abstract, unverified), so design for post-purchase confidence.
- **Framework for the chapter (book's own, label it):** For each buying-group role, capture *job*, *fear*, *proof they need*, *veto power*. This turns persona work from Chapter 1 into decision insight.

### A3. Ethics of behavioral design and dark patterns

- **Definitions:** Brignull (2010) coined dark patterns; FTC staff report (2022) defines them as design practices that trick or manipulate users into choices they might not otherwise make; DSA Recital 67 frames them as materially distorting or impairing autonomous, informed choice.
- **Regulatory positions (state precisely):**
  - *US FTC:* No dedicated dark-patterns statute. Enforcement through Section 5 of the FTC Act (deception, unfairness) and ROSCA for subscriptions. The 2024 click-to-cancel rule was vacated in July 2025 on procedure; rulemaking may restart. Amazon Prime settlement (September 2025, $2.5 billion) shows the cost of enrollment and cancellation patterns.
  - *EU:* DSA Article 25 bans deceptive or manipulative interface design by online platforms, with guideline areas on prominence, nagging and termination asymmetry; UCPD and GDPR cover the rest; AI Act Article 5 bans manipulative AI from February 2025; a Digital Fairness Act proposal is expected late 2026.
- **The line between persuasion and manipulation (book's framing, drawing on the sources):** A design choice is acceptable when it (1) helps the user reach *their own* goal, (2) would survive the user seeing the rationale, (3) is symmetric (easy in and easy out), and (4) uses true information. Thaler and Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008) invokes a publicity principle in this spirit, credited to Rawls [U: confirm wording and attribution in *Nudge*].
- **B2B relevance:** B2B teams think dark patterns are a consumer problem. They are not: auto-renewal terms, seat auto-expansion, hard-to-find data export, preselected telemetry sharing and nagging upgrade prompts all appear in SaaS. End users in B2B are also consumers of the interface, and admins act for many people.

### A4. From insight to specification

**Insight statement.** Base form from the d.school POV: "[User] needs [need] because [insight]." Book's B2B extension (label as the book's own):
> **Insight ID:** INS-xx
> **Who:** [role, context, buying-group position]
> **Observed:** [behavior seen, with evidence: n interviews, usage data, tickets]
> **Because:** [motivation or constraint that explains the behavior]
> **Tension:** [what they want versus what stops them]
> **So we believe:** [implication for the product]
> **Confidence:** [high, medium, low] and [what would change our mind]

Quality test: an insight is non-obvious, explains behavior (why, not what), is grounded in evidence, and implies action. A "finding" says what happened; an insight says why it matters.

**Requirement traceability (Gotel and Finkelstein, 1994).** Most traceability failures are pre-specification: teams lose *why* a requirement exists. Book's matrix (label as the book's own):

| Insight ID | Opportunity or job | Requirement ID | Acceptance criteria | Success metric | Verification (test or eval) | Status |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Rules: every requirement traces back to at least one insight or an explicit strategic or compliance driver; every insight with high confidence traces forward to a decision (build, defer, reject). AI harness use: ask the assistant to flag requirements with no upstream insight and insights with no downstream decision.

**Product specification (book's template, label as own):** 1. Context and linked insights. 2. Users and buying-group roles affected. 3. Goals, non-goals, success metrics. 4. Functional requirements with acceptance criteria (Given, When, Then form from behavior-driven development; attribute to Dan North [U]). 5. Behavioral design decisions (defaults, framing, choice set) with rationale. 6. AI behavior specification: expected outputs, refusal and escalation rules, evals and thresholds. 7. Non-functional requirements (security, audit, latency, residency). 8. Risks, dark-pattern review result, open questions. 9. Traceability table.

### A5. Communicating insights: the insight brief

Book's one-page format (label as own): headline insight in one sentence; so-what for the decision at hand; evidence (sources, sample, strength, contrary evidence); who it affects (roles, segments, revenue at stake); options and recommendation; confidence and what would change it; ask (decision, owner, date). Principles: lead with the decision, not the method (pyramid style, attributed to Barbara Minto [U]); show evidence strength honestly; separate observation from interpretation; tailor by stakeholder (executives want implication and risk, engineering wants the behavior and edge cases, sales wants the buyer language).

### A6. Planning a relaunch or repositioning

- **When to relaunch (Dunford triggers plus book's additions):** competitors you did not expect appear in deals; value resonates with a different segment; you are entering new segments; the product's capability changed enough that the old category undersells it (Beacon moving from correlation to agents); a trust event requires a reset [book's addition].
- **When not to:** when the problem is product quality or onboarding, not perception; when the change is incremental. A relaunch spends attention; spend it only on a real change.
- **Relaunch plan (book's template):** 1. Trigger and evidence. 2. Positioning delta using Dunford's five components (old versus new). 3. Target segments and buying-group messages. 4. Product changes shipped with the relaunch. 5. Existing-customer plan (migration, communication, no forced or deceptive migration). 6. Channel and enablement plan (sales, partners). 7. Metrics and baseline. 8. Risks and rollback.
- **Measuring it:** set baseline before launch; leading indicators (qualified pipeline in the new category, win rate against the new competitor set, sales-cycle length, message recall in win/loss interviews, activation of new capability); lagging indicators (NRR, expansion, churn in migrated cohort). Compare cohorts, not totals, and set the review date up front.

## Part D. 2025 to 2026 AI-era developments

1. **Buyers use AI to decide.** Gartner (March 2026): 45% of B2B buyers used AI tools in a recent purchase; 67% prefer rep-free buying [S]. Insight work must include how AI assistants describe your product.
2. **AI-assisted synthesis matured but still needs human interpretation.** NN/g guidance last reviewed January 2026: delegate transcription, summarization, first-pass coding and redaction; keep interpretation and fact-checking human [V]. In a harness with connectors (call recordings, CRM notes, support tickets, product analytics), the PM's job shifts to framing questions, checking citations back to source and judging confidence.
3. **Human-AI teaming evidence.** Vaccaro et al. (Nature Human Behaviour, October 2024): human-AI combinations underperform the best single agent on average, especially in decision tasks [V abstract]. For PMs: an "AI suggests, human approves" design does not automatically beat either alone; design for calibrated reliance.
4. **Trust as a behavioral design problem.** Lee and See (calibration), algorithm aversion after visible errors, algorithm appreciation before them, and the fix that people accept imperfect algorithms they can adjust. The EU AI Act names automation bias explicitly (Article 14(4)(b)), with high-risk obligations now dated December 2027 or August 2028 [V on site; confirm omnibus].
5. **Manipulative AI is regulated.** AI Act Article 5 prohibitions on manipulative or deceptive AI techniques apply from 2 February 2025 [V]. Emerging concern areas: sycophantic agents, anthropomorphic persuasion, agent-to-agent commerce where the "user" is an agent [U: flagged as emerging; a 2026 arXiv review "Deceptive Patterns as a Sociotechnical Phenomenon" (arXiv 2608.27684) appeared in search but was not read].
