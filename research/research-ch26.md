## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. The event portfolio in B2B go-to-market
- Four event types, each with a different job. Flagship user conference (the company's own annual event: customers, partners, press and analysts in one place; the tier 1 launch moment). Field events (regional roadshows, executive dinners, partner days: pipeline acceleration with named accounts). Analyst days and investor days (an explanation of strategy for the people who score you). Webinars and virtual events (scalable education and demand capture, often the follow-on to a keynote announcement).
- PM application: the PM does not own events (marketing does) but owns what is true on stage: the announcement content, the demo, the readiness claim, and the customer proof. Treat each event as a launch channel that maps to the launch tier system taught earlier in the book: only tier 1 launches earn keynote time; tier 2 gets a breakout and a blog; tier 3 gets release notes.
- Analyst relations practice (Wikipedia definition only, see flags): briefings (vendor tells the analyst), inquiries (client asks the analyst), evaluation submissions (Magic Quadrant, Wave), analyst days, site visits. Analysts are a second audience for every keynote.

### 2. Announcement sequencing and embargoes
- Embargo: the source shares news ahead of time on condition it is not published until a set time. It is a trust agreement; breaches cost the outlet future access (News embargo, Wikipedia).
- Typical sequence a PM should be able to draft (synthesised practice, not a cited standard): internal enablement (sales and support briefed, FAQ and pricing ready) then analyst pre-briefs under NDA (often 1 to 3 weeks before) then press pre-briefs under embargo (days before) then press release at embargo lift (often timed with or just before the keynote) then keynote then breakouts, hands-on labs and webinars then customer and partner follow-up then GA. The Salesforce Agentforce example shows a press release several days before the flagship event, with GA six weeks after.
- Rule for the PM: every claim in the announcement must carry a readiness label: Generally available now, Public preview, Private preview or Roadmap (with no date, or a quarter). The label is printed on the slide, in the press release and in the briefing book. The Apple personalized Siri case shows the cost of a roadmap feature presented with launch-level production.

### 3. Keynote narrative: Nancy Duarte, Resonate (Wiley, 2010)
- Core idea [V]: the audience is the hero, the presenter is the mentor; great talks move between "what is" and "what could be".
- The sparkline [M, from the book and the 2011 TEDxEast talk; re-verify wording]: Beginning sets up "what is" (the familiar world). A first turning point, the call to adventure, shows the gap. The middle alternates between what is and what could be, building contrast. A second turning point, the call to action, says what the audience must do. The end describes the "new bliss", the world once the audience acts.
- S.T.A.R. moment [M]: "Something They'll Always Remember", a planned, dramatic beat (a demo reveal, a striking statistic, a customer on stage) that becomes the thing people repeat.
- Template for a 25-minute B2B product keynote (built on Duarte; author synthesis):
  1. What is (3 min): the customer's world today, in the customer's words and numbers.
  2. Call to adventure (1 min): the gap, stated as a tension the room feels.
  3. Contrast cycles (12 min): three beats, each "today it looks like this; with the product it looks like this", each anchored by a short demo.
  4. S.T.A.R. moment (2 min): one beat designed to be retold.
  5. Customer proof (4 min): a customer on stage with one before/after metric.
  6. Call to action (2 min): what to do this week (join preview, attend lab, talk to account team), with the readiness label visible.
  7. New bliss (1 min): the picture of the audience's world when they adopt.
- B2B adaptation: B2B keynotes serve three audiences at once (users want the demo, buyers want the outcome and price story, analysts and press want the strategy and the claim boundaries). Give each a beat.

### 4. Demo-led keynotes: Apple and others as public examples
- Apple's 2007 iPhone keynote [V on Apple Newsroom]: three-products-in-one reveal, ship date stated on stage, about five and a half months between announcement and release. [M] The "golden path" (fixed demo sequence around fragile prototype) per Vogelstein (NYT Magazine, 2013).
- Since 2020 Apple has moved to produced, pre-recorded keynotes (WWDC 2020 online announcement [V]). The industry split: live demo (credible, risky) versus produced video (polished, safe, lower credibility unless labelled).
- Engelbart 1968: the archetypal live demo with a technical director (Bill English) and a remote environment; the staging was as much the work as the software.

### 5. Demo design and scripting
- Peter Cohan, Great Demo! [M: book first published early 2000s; edition and year not verified]: "Do the Last Thing First": show the end result (the report, the resolved incident) before the steps; start with a situation summary of the customer's problem; then show the shortest path to the result; then peel back detail only on request. greatdemo.com still teaches the method (now attributed to Paul Pearce trainings) and calls it reverse chronology storytelling [V].
- Live versus recorded decision rule (author synthesis): go live when the claim is about speed or interaction and the environment is controlled; use recorded when dependencies are outside your control or the output is nondeterministic, and label it ("recorded", "sped up 2x", "edited for length"). Hybrid: live first beat, recorded backup cued at every beat.
- Demo environment: dedicated, isolated, seeded with synthetic data that looks real, frozen versions of every dependency, reset script between rehearsals, no shared tenancy with the audience's devices (Meta 2025 and Apple 2010 failures both came from audience load).
- Failure plan: for each beat, the trigger to abandon (for example, no response in 8 seconds), the fallback (pre-recorded clip of the same beat), the line the presenter says, and who in the booth switches. Cybertruck 2019 shows state carry-over between beats; reset state before each beat.
- Happy path honesty: every demo is a happy path; honesty is about not implying the happy path is the typical path. Show one realistic imperfection (an agent that asks for approval or says it is unsure) and say what is scripted.
- Pre-mortem [M: Gary Klein, "Performing a Project Premortem", Harvard Business Review, 2007]: before the event, assume the demo failed and list why. The demo risk plan is a pre-mortem turned into a table.

### 6. Demoing AI products
- Nondeterminism: the same prompt can give different output on stage. Controls: pin model version and parameters; constrain inputs to a rehearsed set; cache or record the canonical run as the fallback; run evals on the demo script itself (for example 50 runs, record pass rate); decide the minimum pass rate to go live.
- Disclosure: label edited, sped-up, staged or human-assisted segments on screen, not below a "more" fold (Gemini 2023 lesson); disclose humans in the loop (Tesla We, Robot 2024 lesson); tell third parties they are talking to an AI (Duplex 2018 lesson; EU AI Act Article 50 from 2 Aug 2026 [V]); do not present concept videos as product (Apple Siri 2024 to 2025 lesson); claims are advertising (FTC Operation AI Comply, 2024 [V]).
- Guardrails visible on stage: show the approval gate, the audit log, the confidence or citation. For enterprise buyers, the guardrail is part of the value, not a disclaimer.

### 7. Customer stories on stage
- Practice (author synthesis): select customers for proof of the claim being made, not logo size; obtain written approval for every number shown; rehearse with the customer's communications team; give the customer a story with a before, a turning point and an after (a mini sparkline); never script words the customer would not say; have legal and the customer approve the slide; prepare the customer for press questions after.

### 8. Measuring event impact
- Phillips ROI Methodology [V]: reaction, learning, application, business impact, ROI, plus intangibles and isolating the program's effect. Mapped to an event: reaction (session ratings, NPS of the event), learning (lab completions, certification), application (preview sign-ups that activate within 30 days), business impact (pipeline created and accelerated in attending accounts versus a matched non-attending set), ROI (attributable margin versus event cost), intangibles (analyst and press sentiment, share of voice).
- PM-specific metrics: preview activation, time-to-first-value for event sign-ups, demo-claim support tickets ("it doesn't do what the keynote showed"), analyst note tone, win-rate change where the announcement was cited.
- AI-first method: the PM asks the harness, through the CRM, product analytics and support connectors, questions such as "of accounts that attended Northlight Live, how many enabled the Agents preview within 30 days compared with matched non-attendees" and gets a report with evidence; no formulas.

## (d) 2025 to 2026 AI-era developments

- Live AI demos fail in new ways (Meta Connect, Sep 2025 [V]): the wake phrase triggered every device in the room; routing to an isolated dev server concentrated the load. Agentic and voice demos make the audience part of the system.
- Concept videos for AI features now draw legal and reputational cost (Apple personalized Siri: delay statement and ad pulled Mar 2025 [V]; Landsheft class action filed Mar 2025; proposed $250 million settlement May 2026 [S, needs primary check]).
- Regulatory disclosure duties arrive: EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations apply from 2 Aug 2026 [V on artificialintelligenceact.eu]. Flag: the European Commission's late-2025 "digital omnibus" proposals discussed adjusting some AI Act timelines; check whether Article 50(2) marking deadlines changed before print.
- US enforcement posture: FTC Operation AI Comply (Sep 2024 [V]) established that AI claims are ordinary advertising claims. Flag: check for 2025 to 2026 FTC changes in posture before print; do not state any.
- Agentic launches anchor flagship events (Salesforce Agentforce, announced 12 Sep 2024, GA 25 Oct 2024 [V]). Agent demos shift the honesty question from "is the output real" to "who approved the action and what would it have done without the approval".
- AI in event operations (author synthesis, no stat): PMs now use the AI harness to draft briefing books from PRDs and FAQs, generate likely analyst and press questions, run the demo script many times against the demo environment as an eval, and draft the event impact report from CRM and product analytics connectors. ## (e) Beacon worked example ideas (consistent with beacon-case.md)

Storyline: launching Beacon Agents (supervised remediation) at Northlight Live. All numbers fictional.

- Event plan: Northlight Live is the tier 1 moment. Beacon gets a 20-minute segment in the main keynote (Northlight's CEO opens, the VP of Product for Operations introduces, the Principal PM co-presents the demo), a breakout ("Inside Beacon Agents: how the approval gate works"), a hands-on lab with a seeded sandbox, an MSP partner session for Leo's persona (multi-tenant view), an analyst roundtable and a security deep-dive for Sam's persona (audit logs, least privilege, AI Act documentation for EU customers).
- Readiness labels: Correlate and Diagnose: generally available. Act with human approval gate: public preview at Northlight Live, GA next quarter. Autonomous low-risk actions on customer allowlist: private preview. Per-resolved-incident pricing: announced as "coming", with no price on stage (pricing chapter storyline). The label is on every slide.
- Announcement sequencing: T minus 4 weeks sales and support enablement, partner briefing for MSPs; T minus 2 weeks analyst pre-briefs under NDA (AIOps and ITSM analysts); T minus 3 days press pre-briefs under embargo; T zero press release at embargo lift 30 minutes before keynote; T zero keynote; T plus 1 day webinar for customers not attending; T plus 2 weeks customer advisory board follow-ups; GA date confirmed only when the eval and approval-gate readiness criteria are met.
- Keynote outline on the sparkline: What is: Dana's 3 a.m. page, thousands of alerts, a chat thread, a runbook. Call to adventure: "What if the first responder already knew what broke?" Contrast 1: correlate (thousands of alerts to a handful). Contrast 2: diagnose with cited evidence (logs, traces, recent deploy). Contrast 3: act, with the approval gate on screen. S.T.A.R. moment: Priya (customer champion on stage, persona as a customer SRE lead) approves a rollback from her phone and the incident resolves live; then shows the audit entry Sam would read. Customer proof: one before/after MTTR number approved by the customer. Call to action: join the public preview and the lab today. New bliss: on-call where people sleep.
- Demo script: beats, words, clicks, expected screen, timing, fallback cue per beat. Seeded incident in a demo environment modelled on a fictional retailer. Agent output pinned (model version, temperature, retrieval index frozen). Fifty rehearsal runs as an eval; go-live threshold agreed (for example 48 of 50 identical root-cause outputs); otherwise switch to the labelled recording.
- Demo risk plan (pre-mortem): conference Wi-Fi; audience devices; MCP connector to a third-party observability tool times out; agent proposes a different root cause on stage; approval push notification delayed; state left from rehearsal (Cybertruck lesson); presenter improvises a claim beyond the readiness label. Each has likelihood, impact, mitigation, trigger, fallback, owner.
- Honesty moments: say aloud "this is a seeded incident in our demo environment"; show the agent saying it is not confident in one hypothesis; show that rollback required a human approval. Tie to the hallucinated root cause storyline: the team rehearses a beat where the agent's evidence is weak and the responder rejects it, showing the product working as designed.
- Briefing book for analysts and press: one-page fact sheet, readiness table, architecture diagram (connectors and MCP servers), guardrails and AI risk documentation summary, customer reference list with approvals, pricing posture (no numbers until GA), competitive positioning (against AIOps features in observability suites, incident tools adding AI, and humans plus runbooks), likely tough questions with approved answers (margin drop from 81 to 72 percent is not for external use; flag that the PM must know which numbers are internal), embargo terms.
- Event impact report (Phillips levels): reaction (session ratings), learning (lab completions), application (preview activations within 30 days by attending accounts), business impact (pipeline created and accelerated in attending accounts versus matched non-attendees; diagnosis acceptance rate among preview customers), ROI (attributable gross margin versus Beacon's share of event cost), intangibles (analyst note tone, press accuracy on readiness labels). Plus a "claim drift" check: support tickets and sales calls where customers expected autonomous actions that are only in private preview.
