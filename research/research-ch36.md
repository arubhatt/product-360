## (a) Key concepts and frameworks

### 1. Stakeholder mapping: the power/interest grid (Mendelow)
- Originator: Aubrey L. Mendelow, "Environmental Scanning: The Impact of the Stakeholder Concept," ICIS 1981 [V]. The 1981 paper links how an organization scans its environment to each stakeholder's relative power and the environment's dynamism. The familiar 2x2 with action labels is a later practitioner adaptation [S]. Many textbooks cite "Mendelow 1991" (a later conference paper); not verified this session, so the chapter should cite 1981 and say "attributed to Mendelow."
- Format (two axes, four quadrants):
  - High power, high interest: Manage closely (engage fully, co-create, involve in decisions).
  - High power, low interest: Keep satisfied (brief, concise, no overload; escalate only what touches their goals).
  - Low power, high interest: Keep informed (regular updates, early warning channel, use them as signal).
  - Low power, low interest: Monitor (light touch, watch for movement).
- Recommended extensions for B2B/platform PMs (author's synthesis, label as such): add a third attribute, stance (champion, supporter, neutral, skeptic, blocker), and an arrow showing where you need each person to move by a date. Power in enterprise software often sits with gatekeepers who are low interest until late (security, legal, procurement); the classic failure is leaving them in "Monitor" until they veto at the end. Map internal and external stakeholders separately: internal (sales, marketing, support, finance, legal, engineering, peers, exec) and external (customer economic buyer, champion, security reviewer, partners).
- Stakeholder map template fields (artifact): Name and role; Team; Power (H/M/L, with reason); Interest (H/M/L); Current stance; Desired stance and by when; What they care about (their currency); What they fear; Owner of relationship; Engagement mode and cadence; Last touch; Next action.
- Stakeholder engagement plan template (artifact, synthesis of PMI-style communications planning and the grid): Stakeholder or group; Quadrant; Objective of engagement (inform, consult, involve, collaborate, decide); Message or ask; Channel (1:1, written update, review forum, demo); Cadence; Owner; Evidence they are engaged (signal); Risk if neglected. AI harness use: the PM asks the assistant, through connectors to CRM, ticketing and chat, "for each stakeholder on this map, what have they said or asked about Beacon Agents in the last 30 days, with links," then refreshes the stance column from evidence instead of memory.

### 2. Influence without authority (Cohen and Bradford)
- Originators: Allan R. Cohen and David L. Bradford, Influence Without Authority, 3rd ed., Wiley, 2017 [V]; first developed in a 1989 Organizational Dynamics article [S, not fetched]. Core: the Exchange Model; influence is reciprocal trade in things the other party values, their "currencies" [V on Wiley page].
- Model steps as commonly taught (from author knowledge; verify against the book before quoting as a list): (1) assume everyone is a potential ally; (2) clarify your own goals and priorities; (3) diagnose the other party's world (pressures, goals, how they are measured); (4) identify relevant currencies, theirs and yours; (5) deal with the relationship (is there trust, how do they like to work); (6) influence through give and take.
- Currency families as commonly taught [unverified this session]: inspiration-related (vision, excellence, moral rightness), task-related (resources, assistance, cooperation, information), position-related (recognition, visibility, reputation, contacts), relationship-related (understanding, acceptance, personal support), personal-related (gratitude, ownership, comfort).
- B2B application: a PM's main currencies are information (customer evidence, early roadmap visibility), visibility (crediting a sales engineer in an exec review), task help (a feature flag that unblocks a deal, a field-ready demo) and inspiration (a credible vision). Diagnose what each function is measured on: sales on bookings this quarter, support on ticket volume and CSAT, finance on margin and forecast accuracy, legal on risk exposure. Trade in their measure.

### 3. Decision rights and alignment: DACI and RAPID
- DACI (Atlassian Team Playbook) [V]: Driver, Approver (exactly one), Contributors (voice, no vote), Informed (told afterward). Template title "DACI: [question]?", then details, background, relevant data, decision factors, options table, action items, outcome. 60 min run, 3 to 6 people. Origin of the acronym before Atlassian is not documented on the page; attribute the format to Atlassian's playbook, not as its inventor.
- RAPID (Bain and Company) [V]: Recommend, Agree, Perform, Input, Decide. Key teaching: ideally one decider; "Agree" is a veto reserved for must-meet requirements like legal or regulatory, used sparingly. Useful when legal or security truly hold a veto (in Beacon: Sam's security review).
- Teaching point: a contentious decision usually fails on unclear rights, not on lack of data. Write down who decides before debating options. Note the overlap: DACI has no explicit veto role; for enterprise products that need security or legal sign-off, either add a "Must agree" row or use RAPID's A.
- Alignment practices to cover (synthesis): pre-wiring (meet key stakeholders 1:1 before the group meeting so no one is surprised); written pre-reads; explicit decision logs; "disagree and commit" (Amazon leadership principle [V]) as a norm once the decider has decided; a standing escalation path (Atlassian Working Agreements play includes an explicit escalation process [V]).

### 4. Managing conflict and saying no
- Frame: Cagan and Jones (SVPG, Dec 2025) [V] ask stakeholders to bring problems, not features, and to ask for "high-integrity commitments" only when they need a firm date. This gives PMs the script for saying no: convert the request into the underlying problem, its owner and success measure; then decide it on the same criteria as everything else.
- Saying no patterns (author synthesis, not attributed): "not now, here's when we'll revisit" (with a date tied to evidence); "yes, if" (state what drops); "no, and here is the alternative" (workaround, partner, configuration); "no, and here is why" (strategy, data). Always say no to the request, not the person; put the decision and its reason in writing.
- Conflict sources in B2B: deal-driven requests (sales), launch timing (marketing), escalations (support), margin (finance), risk (legal, security). Separate the person from the problem; agree on decision criteria before options; escalate jointly ("we disagree on X; we both want Y; please decide by date") rather than unilaterally.
- Kim Scott's Radical Candor [V]: care personally and challenge directly; failure modes are ruinous empathy, obnoxious aggression, manipulative insincerity. Useful for both peer conflict and coaching.

### 5. Managing up
- Gabarro and Kotter, "Managing Your Boss" (HBR 1980, reprinted 2005) [V for authorship and core claim]: the boss relationship is mutual dependence between two fallible people and deserves deliberate investment. Commonly summarized checklist [S]: understand the boss's goals, pressures, strengths, weaknesses and preferred work style; understand your own; build a relationship that fits both, with clear mutual expectations, a flow of information, dependability and honesty, and selective use of the boss's time.
- Larson (2026) [V]: middle management is largely balancing pressure from executives, peers and teams; AI benefits require fast binding executive decisions when teams disagree. Implication for managing up: bring your boss decisions, not status; ask for the decision you need, by when, with your recommendation.
- Practical formats: weekly written update (outcomes moved, decisions needed, risks, asks); "no surprises" rule for bad news; pre-wire before exec reviews.

### 6. Cross-functional leadership
- Sales: share roadmap themes with confidence levels, not dates; run a deal-request intake that captures problem, account, ARR at stake, deadline; join a few calls per month; give sales engineers early access. Cagan and Jones: stakeholders bring go-to-market context; product leaders balance competing asks [V].
- Marketing: co-own positioning and launch tiers; align on what is GA versus preview; give PMM evidence and customer stories.
- Support: treat escalations and ticket themes as discovery input; agree severity definitions; a known-issues channel.
- Finance: pricing, packaging, margin and forecast assumptions; in AI products, inference cost and gross margin.
- Legal and security: involve early on data use, AI risk documentation, contracts; treat their veto as legitimate (RAPID "Agree") and design for it.
- Engineering and design: the core team, not stakeholders; Cagan's empowered team model splits accountability (PM value and viability, design usability, tech lead feasibility) [V].

Suggested competencies for the chapter's own list (synthesis based on Mehta's four areas plus 2026 additions): problem discovery, customer insight, data fluency, product definition for humans and agents, delivery, AI product judgment (evals, cost, risk), strategy and roadmap, business outcome ownership, stakeholder influence, communication, managing up, team leadership.

### 8. Coaching and 1:1s
- GROW (Whitmore, Coaching for Performance) [V]: Goal, Reality, Options, Will.
- First 1:1 questions (Lara Hogan, 2016) [V]: frustrations, feedback and recognition preferences, goals and support, growth balance.
- Cagan, "Product Coaching and AI" (Feb 2026) [V]: AI as a personal coach for product creators; human coaching concentrated on leaders.
- 1:1 coaching template (artifact, synthesis): their agenda first; wins since last time; one current challenge, coached with GROW; feedback both ways (Radical Candor); competency focus this quarter (from self-assessment); commitments and date; what I as manager owe you. Rule: the 1:1 belongs to the report. Andy Grove's High Output Management (1983) is the usual origin for this norm [S, not fetched].

### 9. Team charters
- Purpose: one page that says why the team exists, what it owns, how it works and how it is measured. Google's Project Aristotle found "structure and clarity" (clear roles, plans and goals) among the five team dynamics [V], which a charter directly addresses.
- Team charter template (artifact, synthesis from Atlassian Roles and Responsibilities and Working Agreements plays [V] plus outcome framing from Cagan [V]): Mission (one sentence); Customers and problems owned; Outcomes and metrics (with current baseline); Out of scope; Members and roles (PM, design, tech lead, applied AI, PMM); Decision rights (who decides what; DACI defaults); Key stakeholders and engagement cadence; Working agreements (hours, channels, sync vs async, meeting list); Escalation path; Interfaces and dependencies (APIs, other teams); Review date (every 3 to 6 months).

### 10. Product culture and psychological safety
- Edmondson 1999 [V]: definition; 51 teams in a manufacturing company; psychological safety predicts learning behavior, which mediates team performance; leader coaching shapes it.
- Project Aristotle (Google re:Work) [V]: 180 teams; psychological safety ranked first of five dynamics.
- Cagan, Empowered (with Chris Jones) [V]: empowered teams get problems, not features; strong product leaders coach.
- Teaching point: psychological safety is not niceness or low standards; Edmondson pairs it with accountability (The Fearless Organization, 2018 [V book exists; specific matrix not fetched]). For AI products, safety to report a hallucination or a bad eval result early is the operational version.
