Part VI · Go-to-Market: Offers, Announcements and Events

Events, Keynotes and Demos

Plan the moment, script the demo, and make it land.

42 min read · 7 templates · 7 prompts

An event is the one day of the year when your product claims are made in public, to customers, partners, analysts and press at once, by people who can't take the words back. Marketing runs the event. The product manager owns what is true on stage: what gets announced, what the demo shows, what the readiness label says and which customer proof stands behind it.

Why this matters

In B2B software, a flagship event compresses months of launch work into a few hours. The keynote sets the story the field will tell for the next year. Analysts write their first notes from the briefing and the stage. Press coverage fixes a version of what you announced, and that version is often less careful than yours. Customers who watched the demo arrive at their next account review expecting the product to do what they saw.

That last point is where product managers earn or lose trust. A demo is a promise, and the gap between what was shown and what ships shows up later as support tickets, stalled deals, analyst skepticism and, in the worst cases, legal exposure. The public record has plenty of examples. Steve Jobs had to ask a WWDC audience in 2010 to switch off their Wi-Fi so his iPhone 4 demo could load. Google’s 2023 Gemini “Hands-on” video was later reported to have been built from still frames and text prompts rather than live video. Apple advertised personalized Siri features in 2024 that it said in March 2025 would take longer than expected to deliver. In each case the failure came from staging, disclosure or readiness.

AI products raise the stakes. The output is nondeterministic, so the same demo can give a different answer on stage than it did in rehearsal. Agentic products take actions, so the honest question becomes who approved the action and what the agent would have done without that approval. Regulators have started treating AI claims as ordinary advertising claims. The event is where a launch is most visible and least reversible, so it needs a method as rigorous as the launch itself.

This chapter gives you that method:

  1. An event portfolio that matches each type of event to a job and to the launch tiers from Chapter 25.
  2. An announcement sequence with readiness labels, embargoes and analyst and press briefings in the right order.
  3. A keynote narrative built on Nancy Duarte’s work on persuasive presentations.
  4. Demo design, including the choice between live and recorded, demo environments, failure plans and honest scripting.
  5. AI demo practice: nondeterminism, visible guardrails and disclosure.
  6. Customer stories on stage, and a way to measure event impact that goes beyond badge scans.

How events fit B2B go-to-market

The event portfolio

B2B companies run several kinds of events, and each does a different job. Treating them as interchangeable is how a flagship keynote ends up full of minor features and a regional roadshow ends up announcing strategy.

Event type Main job Primary audience What the PM owns Launch tier fit
Flagship user conference Set the year’s story; land tier 1 launches in front of everyone at once Customers, partners, prospects, analysts, press Announcement content, keynote demo, readiness labels, customer proof, breakout and lab content Tier 1 in the keynote; tier 2 in breakouts
Field events (roadshows, executive dinners, partner days) Accelerate pipeline with named accounts Buying groups in specific accounts and regions A field-ready demo, a short talk track, answers to the hard questions Repeats tier 1 and 2 news for local audiences
Analyst days and investor days Explain strategy to the people who score you Industry analysts, investors The strategy narrative, roadmap boundaries, proof points Context for tier 1; rarely news on its own
Webinars and virtual events Scalable education and demand capture Customers and prospects who didn’t attend; practitioners Deep-dive content, demos, Q&A answers Follow-on for tier 1; the main stage for tier 2

The tier fit is the rule that protects the keynote. Chapter 25 sorts launches into tiers by business impact. Only tier 1 launches earn keynote minutes. Tier 2 gets a breakout session and a blog post. Tier 3 gets release notes. When an executive asks for “just one more thing” in the keynote, the tier is your answer: if it isn’t tier 1, the keynote is the wrong channel, and a crowded keynote weakens the tier 1 story it was built for.

Analysts are a second audience for everything you do at a flagship event. Analyst relations, as a practice, covers briefings (you tell the analyst what you’re doing), inquiries (the analyst’s clients ask the analyst about you), submissions to formal evaluations, analyst days and site visits. An analyst who sat through your keynote and then hears a different story in the briefing room will write about the gap.

On-rampIf you've never worked an event

Find out three things in your first week: which launches are tier 1 for this event, who in marketing owns the run of show, and who signs off on claims (usually product, legal and communications together). Then ask to see last year’s keynote recording and the analyst notes that followed it. The distance between the two tells you how this company’s events usually land.

Readiness labels

Every claim you make at an event needs a readiness label, printed on the slide, in the press release and in the briefing book. Use four, and define them in writing before the event:

Label Meaning What you can show
Generally available Customers can buy and use it today, with support Live demo, pricing if public, customer results
Public preview Any eligible customer can turn it on; not yet fully supported or priced Live or recorded demo, preview sign-up, design-partner stories
Private preview Selected customers only, by invitation Recorded demo, with the label on screen; no broad promises
Roadmap Planned, not available Concept only, labeled as a direction, with no date or a quarter at most

The label does two things. It tells buyers what they can act on this quarter, and it gives your own presenters a boundary they mustn’t cross when they improvise. Several public event failures involve a roadmap item presented with the production values of a launch. Apple’s personalized Siri features, covered in the AI lens below, are a recent example.

Announcement sequencing and embargoes

By the time the keynote starts, most of the announcement work is done. It happens in the weeks before, as each audience learns the news in an order that protects trust.

A news embargo is an arrangement where you share news with a journalist in advance on condition that it isn’t published until a set time. It’s usually informal, a matter of trust rather than contract, and an outlet that breaks one can lose its early access in the future. Embargoes let reporters write a considered story instead of a rushed one, and they let you time coverage to the keynote.

A sequence a PM should be able to draft looks like this. The timings are this book’s recommended practice, not an industry standard; adjust them to your company’s norms.

  1. Internal enablement (about four weeks before). Sales, support and partners are briefed. The FAQ, pricing position and readiness labels are final. Chapter 27 covers the enablement itself.
  2. Analyst pre-briefs under NDA (one to three weeks before). Analysts hear the strategy and the specifics early, so their first notes are informed.
  3. Press pre-briefs under embargo (days before). Reporters get the release, the briefing book and a spokesperson.
  4. Press release at embargo lift (shortly before or at the start of the keynote).
  5. The keynote.
  6. Breakouts, hands-on labs and webinars (the same day and the days after), where the detail lives.
  7. Customer and partner follow-up (the weeks after), including advisory boards and account reviews.
  8. General availability when the readiness criteria are met, which may be weeks or a quarter later.

Salesforce’s Agentforce launch in 2024 is a public example of separating the news from the stage. The press release announcing Agentforce went out on 12 September 2024, ahead of the company’s Dreamforce conference, and set general availability for late October. Because the news was already out, the conference could spend its time on demos and customers instead of the announcement itself.

Analyst and press briefings need a briefing book: a fact sheet, the readiness table, an architecture view, customer references with approvals, the pricing position, competitive framing, the hard questions with approved answers and the embargo terms. The briefing book is also where you decide which numbers are internal. A PM who briefs an analyst without knowing which figures are confidential will eventually say one aloud.

Keynote narrative: what is and what could be

Nancy Duarte’s Resonate (2010) and her 2011 TEDxEast talk on the structure of great talks describe a pattern she found in persuasive speeches: the speaker moves back and forth between what is (the audience’s world as it is today) and what could be (the world the speaker proposes). Duarte casts the audience as the hero and the presenter as the mentor, which is a useful correction for product keynotes that cast the product as the hero.

Duarte draws the pattern as a sparkline. The talk opens in what is. A first turning point, the call to adventure, names the gap between today and what’s possible. The middle alternates between what is and what could be, and the contrast does the persuading. A second turning point, the call to action, tells the audience what to do. The talk ends on the new bliss: the world once the audience has acted. Duarte also recommends a planned S.T.A.R. moment, “Something They’ll Always Remember”: one beat designed to be retold in the hallway.

Here’s how this book adapts the sparkline for a 25-minute B2B product keynote. The timings are a starting point, not a rule.

Beat Minutes What happens
What is 3 The customer’s world today, in the customer’s words and numbers
Call to adventure 1 The gap, stated as a tension the room already feels
Contrast cycles 12 Three beats, each “today it looks like this; with the product it looks like this”, each anchored by a short demo
S.T.A.R. moment 2 One beat designed to be retold
Customer proof 4 A customer on stage with one approved before-and-after result
Call to action 2 What to do this week (join the preview, attend the lab, talk to your account team), with the readiness label visible
New bliss 1 The audience’s world once they’ve adopted

A B2B keynote serves three audiences at the same time. Users want to see the product work. Buyers want the outcome and enough of the commercial story to start a conversation. Analysts and press want the strategy and the claim boundaries. Give each of them at least one beat they can’t miss, and check the outline against all three before rehearsal. Chapter 35 covers the presentation craft itself; this chapter focuses on what the PM must get right in the content.

Demo-led keynotes: what the public record teaches

A few public examples are worth knowing, because they show what a demo needs beyond working software.

Engelbart, 1968. Douglas Engelbart’s demonstration at the Fall Joint Computer Conference in San Francisco, later called the Mother of All Demos, showed windows, hypertext, the mouse, video conferencing and real-time collaborative editing to roughly a thousand people. The software ran on a computer in Menlo Park, reached over a leased line with homemade modems and two microwave video links, and projected on a 22-foot screen. Bill English ran the video switching. The lesson for a PM is that staging was half the work, and the demo had a dedicated technical director.

iPhone, 2007. Apple introduced the iPhone at Macworld in January 2007 as three products in one (a phone, a widescreen iPod and an internet communicator) and said on stage that it would ship in June. It shipped in the US on 29 June, about five and a half months later. Fred Vogelstein’s later reporting described how the prototype could fail if tasks were done out of order, so the team rehearsed a fixed sequence, which they called the “golden path.” The demo was scripted around real fragility, and the shipped product did what the demo showed. That’s the honest version of a happy path.

iPhone 4, 2010. At WWDC in June 2010, the demo stalled on network problems. Jobs asked the audience to get off the Wi-Fi, then to turn off their portable hotspots, and switched to backups. The audience’s own devices were the load. Fifteen years later the same failure class returned in an AI demo, as the next section shows.

Pre-recorded keynotes. Apple moved WWDC to an online format in 2020, replacing the live stage keynote with a produced one. Produced video removes the risk of a live failure and raises production quality. It also raises the bar for disclosure, because the audience can’t tell what is real software and what is edited.

Cybertruck, 2019. At Tesla’s Cybertruck unveiling, two “Armor Glass” windows shattered when a designer threw a metal ball at them. Elon Musk later said an earlier sledgehammer strike on the door had cracked the base of the glass. One beat’s success had set up the next beat’s failure. Demo beats share state, so reset state between beats.

Demo design and scripting

Do the last thing first. Peter Cohan’s Great Demo! method argues that a demo should open with the result (the resolved incident, the finished report, the approved change), then show the shortest path to it, and only peel back detail when the audience asks. Most product demos do the reverse: they start with login and configuration and run out of time before the payoff. Cohan’s method also starts with a short situation summary of the customer’s problem, which fits the “what is” opening of the sparkline.

Live, recorded or hybrid. Use a simple rule. Go live when the claim is about speed or interaction and you control the environment. Use a recording when dependencies sit outside your control or the output is nondeterministic, and label it on screen (“recorded”, “sped up 2x”, “edited for length”). The hybrid is usually right for a keynote: run the beat live, with a recorded version of the same beat cued and ready to switch to.

Situation Live Recorded (labeled) Hybrid
Claim is about speed or interactivity Best Weak Good
Third-party services or audience network in the path Risky Best Good
Output is nondeterministic (AI) Only with pinned settings and a passing rehearsal eval Good, with disclosure Best
Feature is private preview or roadmap No Yes, with the label on screen No
Customer data or production systems involved No Synthetic data only No

The demo environment. Run demos in a dedicated, isolated environment seeded with synthetic data that looks real. Freeze the version of every dependency. Write a reset procedure and run it before every rehearsal and before the show. Keep the demo off any network or service the audience shares. The 2010 iPhone 4 stall came from audience load, and so did the first of the two 2025 Meta Connect failures.

The failure plan. For each beat, write down the trigger to abandon (for example, no response within eight seconds), the fallback (the recorded clip of the same beat), the line the presenter says when switching and the person in the production booth who switches. A presenter who knows exactly what happens at second eight stays calm; one who doesn’t starts improvising.

Happy-path honesty. Every demo is a happy path. The honesty question is whether you imply the happy path is the typical path. Show one realistic imperfection (a product that asks for approval, or says it isn’t sure), say aloud what is seeded or scripted, and keep every capability shown inside its readiness label.

The pre-mortem. Gary Klein’s pre-mortem asks a team to imagine that a project has already failed and list the reasons why. Run one for every keynote demo a few weeks out: “It’s the morning after the event, and the demo was a disaster. What happened?” The demo risk plan in this chapter’s artifacts is a pre-mortem turned into a table with owners.

Customer stories on stage

A customer on stage is the strongest proof you have, and the riskiest to stage badly. Choose the customer for proof of the specific claim you’re making, not for the size of the logo. A small customer with a sharp before-and-after result beats a famous one who can only say they’re “exploring.”

Give the customer a story with its own small sparkline: the situation before, the turning point and the result. Get written approval for every number shown, from the customer and from your legal team. Rehearse with the customer’s communications team, not just the speaker. Never script words the customer wouldn’t say in their own voice, and prepare them for the press and analyst questions that follow. If the customer’s result came from a preview feature, the readiness label goes on their slide too.

Measuring event impact

Event reporting usually stops at attendance, session ratings and leads. Those are inputs. A PM should care whether the event changed what customers did.

Jack and Patti Phillips’s ROI Methodology, developed for training and program evaluation, gives a five-level skeleton that fits events well, plus two important additions: intangibles, and a step to isolate the program’s effect from everything else that happened.

Level Question Event measure
1. Reaction and planned action Did they value it, and do they plan to act? Session ratings; stated intent to join the preview
2. Learning Did they learn what we taught? Lab completions; certification passes
3. Application Did they use it afterward? Preview activations within 30 days by attending accounts
4. Business impact Did it change business results? Pipeline created and accelerated in attending accounts compared with similar accounts that didn’t attend; product outcome metrics among adopters
5. ROI Did benefits exceed costs? Attributable margin compared with the product’s share of event cost
Intangibles What else changed? Analyst note tone; press accuracy on readiness labels; share of voice

The isolation step is where most event reports fall short. Attending accounts are usually already more engaged, so comparing them with everyone else overstates the effect. Compare attendees with a matched set of similar accounts that didn’t attend, and say plainly how confident you are in the difference.

Add one measure that belongs to the PM: claim drift. Count the support tickets, sales calls and account reviews in which a customer expected the product to do something it only does in private preview or on the roadmap. Claim drift shows whether the readiness labels survived contact with the audience.

How to apply it

Here’s a method for a tier 1 announcement at a flagship event, counted back from the keynote. Start at twelve weeks if you can; eight is the practical minimum for anything with a live demo.

Twelve weeks out: decide what’s true.

  1. Confirm with product marketing which launches are tier 1 for this event (Chapter 25), and agree that only those get keynote time.
  2. Write the readiness table: every capability you might mention, with its label (generally available, public preview, private preview or roadmap) and the evidence for that label. Get engineering, legal and communications to sign it. This table governs every later artifact.
  3. Draft the event plan: the keynote segment, breakouts, labs, partner sessions, analyst and press sessions, and which persona each one serves.
  4. Pick the customer story. Choose for proof of the tier 1 claim, start the approval process for any number, and book time with the customer’s communications team.

Eight weeks out: build the story and the demo.

  1. Outline the keynote on the sparkline. Write the what-is opening in the customer’s words, three contrast cycles, one S.T.A.R. moment, the customer proof and a call to action that matches the readiness label.
  2. Write the demo script beat by beat: words, actions, expected screen, timing and the fallback cue. Open with the result, in Cohan’s sense, and keep every beat inside its label.
  3. Stand up the demo environment: isolated, seeded with synthetic data, dependencies frozen, reset procedure written and tested.
  4. Run the pre-mortem with the presenters, the demo engineer and the production lead. Turn it into the demo risk plan, with an owner for every risk.
  5. Draft the announcement sequencing plan, from internal enablement through general availability, with the embargo time and who briefs whom.

Four weeks out: prepare the audiences.

  1. Brief sales, support and partners. Give them the readiness table, the FAQ and the answers to the hard questions (Chapter 27).
  2. Finish the briefing book and run the analyst pre-briefs under NDA. Note every question you couldn’t answer well and fix the answer before the press pre-briefs.
  3. Start the rehearsal eval for any AI beat: run the demo script repeatedly against the demo environment and record the pass rate. Agree the threshold for going live in advance.

Event week: rehearse under show conditions.

  1. Hold a dress rehearsal at the venue, on the show network, with the production booth, and with as many audience devices as you can simulate. Run the reset procedure between every beat.
  2. Make a live-or-recorded call for each beat, based on the rehearsal eval and the dress rehearsal. Write it down, with the name of the person who made it.
  3. Do a final claims review of slides, script and press release against the readiness table. Remove any sentence that outruns its label.

After the event: measure and correct.

  1. At 30 days, report reaction, learning and application. At 90 days, report business impact against matched non-attendees, intangibles and claim drift.
  2. Fix claim drift where you find it: update enablement, correct the website, and brief account teams whose customers expected more than was announced.
For product leaders

Treat the readiness table as a gate. If a capability doesn’t have a signed label by the eight-week mark, it doesn’t go on the keynote slides. Back your PMs when an executive wants to show a roadmap item with launch-level production. The cost of that request shows up later, in claim drift, stalled deals and renewal conversations.

The AI lens

Demoing AI products

AI products bring four demo risks that deterministic software mostly doesn’t.

Nondeterminism. The same input can produce a different output on stage than it did in rehearsal. Controls that work: pin the model version and generation settings; constrain inputs to a rehearsed set; freeze any retrieval index the demo depends on; keep a recording of the canonical run as the fallback; and treat the demo script itself as an eval. Run it many times against the demo environment, record the pass rate and agree the minimum before anyone decides to go live. Chapter 31 covers evals in depth; the demo is just a very small, very public eval set.

The audience becomes part of the system. At Meta Connect in September 2025, two live demos of Meta’s smart glasses failed. Meta’s CTO, Andrew Bosworth, later explained that the cooking demo’s wake phrase activated the AI feature on every pair of glasses in the building, and all of them were routed to the same development server set up for the demo, which overloaded it. The second failure, a call that didn’t appear on the new display glasses, was a race condition the team hadn’t seen before. Bosworth called it “a demo fail and not, like, a product failure.” Both points can be true, and the audience still left with a failed demo. With voice and agentic products, the room is load. Model it in the risk plan.

Disclosure. The public record now has a clear pattern of AI demos that misled through what they left out.

  • Google Gemini “Hands-on” video, December 2023. The video appeared to show Gemini responding live to drawings, gestures and a cup game. TechCrunch reported that Google had prompted the model with still frames and text, not live video or voice; the video description noted that latency had been reduced and outputs shortened. A Google DeepMind leader said the video illustrated what experiences could look like. Alphabet’s share price rose after the launch, so the cost wasn’t financial on day one; it was credibility, and the story that Google’s best demo had been staged.
  • Google Duplex, May 2018. The stage demo of an assistant booking a salon appointment drew criticism because the people on the call weren’t told they were talking to a machine. Google then said it was designing the feature with disclosure built in. What an AI does to third parties during a demo is also a disclosure question.
  • Tesla We, Robot, October 2024. Attendees interacted with Optimus robots that many assumed were autonomous. Reports confirmed they were teleoperated by people. A human in the loop is fine. An undisclosed human in the loop is the failure.
  • Apple personalized Siri, 2024 to 2025. Features shown at WWDC 2024 appeared only in a produced video and were never demoed live to press. In March 2025 Apple said the features would take longer than it had thought to deliver, and it pulled a TV ad that promoted them. A class action alleging false advertising followed. John Gruber, writing at the time, pointed out that the features had never been demoed live, and treated that absence as the warning sign.
  • Google Bard, February 2023. A promotional example for Bard gave an inaccurate answer about the James Webb Space Telescope. Alphabet’s shares fell sharply around the launch event, though coverage linked the drop to the underwhelming launch as a whole, not to the one error alone. Fact-check every AI output that appears in a launch asset.

The rule that follows: label edited, sped-up, staged or human-assisted segments on screen, not in a description below the video. Say aloud what is seeded. Tell people when they’re interacting with an AI. Don’t present a concept video as a product.

Regulation. Two developments make disclosure more than good manners. In September 2024 the US Federal Trade Commission announced Operation AI Comply, a set of enforcement actions resting on the principle that there is no AI exemption from consumer protection law: claims about AI are advertising claims and need substantiation. In the EU, Article 50 of the AI Act sets transparency obligations: people must be told when they’re interacting with an AI system unless it’s obvious, providers of generative systems must mark synthetic content in a machine-readable way, and deployers must disclose deepfakes. The Act’s general application date, 2 August 2026, covers Article 50, but the Act has since been amended, so check the current scope and any transitional provisions with your legal team before any event with an EU audience. A keynote with an AI-generated presenter, synthetic voices or synthetic customer footage needs a label. Chapter 33 covers the regulation.

Guardrails are part of the value. In agentic products, the approval gate, the audit log and the cited evidence are often what the economic buyer and the security reviewer came to see, so put them on screen. A demo where the agent asks for approval, or says it isn’t confident, shows the product working as designed, and it’s more credible than a flawless run.

Doing the work with AI

An AI harness with connectors to your documents, CRM, product analytics and support system changes most of the event workload.

  • Briefing books. Give the harness the PRD, the FAQ, the readiness table and the positioning (Chapter 24), and ask for a first draft of the briefing book. Ask it to mark every claim with its source and to flag any claim that outruns its readiness label.
  • Hard questions. Ask the harness to play an analyst, a skeptical reporter and a security reviewer, and to generate the questions each would ask. Write the answers yourself, and get the sensitive ones approved.
  • Claim checks. Before the final review, have the harness compare the keynote script, slides and press release against the readiness table, line by line. This is tedious for people and quick for a model.
  • Rehearsal evals. For AI beats, the harness can run the demo script many times against the demo environment and summarize where outputs diverged. A person still decides what counts as a pass.
  • Impact reports. Ask questions in plain language through your connectors: “Of accounts that attended, how many enabled the preview within 30 days, compared with similar accounts that didn’t attend?” Ask for the evidence behind each number and for the matching method, and read both before you trust the answer (Chapter 19).

Two cautions. Embargoed news, unannounced pricing and customer data are confidential: use only tools your company has approved for them. And a model drafting a keynote will happily write claims you can’t support. Treat every sentence it drafts as a claim to check against the readiness table.

The AI lensThe one-line rule for AI demos

If you’d be uncomfortable explaining exactly how the demo was made to the reporter in the front row, either change the demo or put the explanation on screen.

The Beacon case: Beacon Agents at Northlight Live

Beacon caseThe situation

Beacon Agents, the supervised remediation capability, is Northlight’s tier 1 launch for Northlight Live, the company’s annual customer event. Beacon has a 20-minute segment in the main keynote. Northlight’s CEO opens the keynote, the VP of Product for the Operations portfolio introduces Beacon, and you, as Beacon’s Principal PM, co-present the demo. The product has a recent scar: a hallucinated root cause that led a customer to roll back the wrong service. The whole event has to show that the agent can be trusted, without claiming more than is ready.

The readiness table. You start with labels, because everything else depends on them. Correlate and Diagnose are generally available. Act, with the human approval gate, goes to public preview at Northlight Live (the opt-in early access program in Chapter 25), with general availability planned for the next quarter. Autonomous actions on a customer’s allowlist of low-risk steps are in private preview. Per-resolved-incident pricing (Chapter 15) is announced as coming, with no price on stage. Engineering, legal and communications sign the table, and every slide carries the label for what it shows.

The event plan. The keynote segment is one piece. Around it you plan a breakout (“Inside Beacon Agents: how the approval gate works”), a hands-on lab in a seeded sandbox, an MSP partner session that shows Leo’s multi-tenant view, an analyst roundtable, and a security deep-dive for Sam’s audience covering audit logs, least-privilege access and the AI Act documentation EU customers have been asking for.

The sequence.

The keynote outline. The segment follows the sparkline. What is: Dana’s 3 a.m. page, thousands of alerts, a chat thread and a runbook. The call to adventure: what if the first responder already knew what broke? Three contrast cycles follow. Correlate turns thousands of alerts into a handful of incidents. Diagnose proposes a root cause with cited evidence from logs, traces and a recent deploy. Act proposes a rollback, with the approval gate on screen. The S.T.A.R. moment belongs to a customer SRE lead in Priya’s role, from a design-partner company: they approve the rollback from their phone, the seeded incident resolves, and they open the audit entry that Sam’s team would review. The customer proof is one before-and-after MTTR result, approved in writing by the customer. The call to action is to join the public preview and the lab that afternoon. The new bliss is an on-call rotation where people sleep.

The demo. The demo runs against a seeded incident in a demo environment modeled on a fictional retailer. The model version, generation settings and retrieval index are frozen. The applied AI engineers run the demo script 50 times as an eval, and the team agrees in advance that the live beat goes ahead only if at least 48 of the 50 runs produce the same root cause with the same evidence; otherwise the labeled recording plays. Early runs miss the threshold because the retrieval index is still updating overnight. Freezing it fixes the problem, and the finding goes into the risk plan.

The script includes three honesty moments. You say aloud that this is a seeded incident in the demo environment. The agent shows one hypothesis it isn’t confident about and explains why. And the rollback visibly waits for a human approval. You also rehearse a beat drawn from the hallucinated-root-cause storyline: the agent’s evidence for one hypothesis is weak, and the responder rejects it. In the breakout, that beat shows the product working as designed, which is the point the security reviewers in the room care about most.

The risk plan. The pre-mortem produces seven risks: conference Wi-Fi; audience devices loading the network; an MCP connector to a third-party observability tool timing out; the agent proposing a different root cause on stage; the approval notification arriving late on the phone; state left over from rehearsal; and a presenter improvising a claim beyond the readiness label. Each gets a likelihood, an impact, a mitigation, a trigger, a fallback and an owner. The last one gets the most attention. Executives are most likely to say “and soon it’ll do this on its own” in the moment, so the script gives the CEO an approved sentence about autonomy that names private preview.

The briefing book. The book for analysts and press has a fact sheet, the readiness table, an architecture view of the connectors and MCP servers, a summary of the guardrails and AI risk documentation, a customer reference list with approvals, the pricing position (per-resolved-incident pricing is coming; no numbers until it’s available), competitive framing against AIOps features in observability suites, incident tools adding AI, and humans with runbooks, and the hard questions with approved answers. One line in the PM’s private notes matters more than most of the book: the drop in gross margin from 81% to 72% after the agentic features launched is internal and isn’t for external use. You write down the approved answer for questions about inference cost, so nobody improvises a number.

The impact report. You set the measures before the event, so nobody picks them afterward to fit the result. Reaction: session ratings for the keynote segment, breakout and lab. Learning: lab completions. Application: preview activations within 30 days by attending accounts. Business impact: pipeline created and accelerated in attending accounts compared with matched accounts that didn’t attend, and the diagnosis acceptance rate among preview customers. ROI: attributable gross margin compared with Beacon’s share of the event cost. Intangibles: the tone of analyst notes and whether press coverage got the readiness labels right. And claim drift: support tickets and sales calls where customers expected autonomous actions that are only in private preview.

Anti-patterns

Anti-patternThe roadmap dressed as a launch

A capability that is months from availability gets the keynote’s best production: a polished video, a confident voiceover, no label. Buyers plan around it. Put the readiness label on every slide, and keep roadmap items to concepts with no date or a quarter at most.

Anti-patternThe undisclosed edit

A demo video is cut, sped up or assembled from prompts that differ from what the viewer sees, and the disclosure sits in a description nobody reads. Label edits, speed-ups, staging and human assistance on screen, in the video itself.

Anti-patternThe hidden human

An “autonomous” system on stage is quietly operated or corrected by a person. Human assistance is often a sensible design. Hiding it turns a reasonable design choice into a credibility problem.

Anti-patternRehearsal in a quiet room

The demo is rehearsed on office Wi-Fi with five people watching, then run on the show network with thousands of devices in the room. Rehearse under show conditions, including audience load, and model the audience as part of the system.

Anti-patternThe overloaded keynote

Every team gets a slide, and the tier 1 launch competes with a dozen minor features for attention. Keynote time goes to tier 1 launches; everything else goes to breakouts, blogs and release notes.

Anti-patternThe improvised promise

An executive, energized by the room, adds “and soon it’ll do all of this on its own.” Press quotes the sentence, not the slide. Give every presenter approved words for the questions they’re most tempted to answer beyond the label.

Anti-patternBadge scans as success

The event report counts attendees and leads and stops. Measure application and business impact against a matched comparison group, and track claim drift.

Checklist

Key takeaways

  • Events are a launch channel. Match each event type to a job, and give keynote time only to tier 1 launches.
  • The readiness label is the PM’s main control. Put it on every slide, in every release and in the briefing book, and sign it before the creative work starts.
  • Sequence the announcement so each audience hears the news in an order that protects trust: internal teams, then analysts, then press under embargo, then the public.
  • Build the keynote on Duarte’s what is and what could be, with the audience as hero, one memorable beat and customer proof that has been approved number by number.
  • Design demos to fail safely: open with the result, run in an isolated and pinned environment, rehearse under show conditions and cue a fallback for every beat.
  • AI demos need a rehearsal eval, visible guardrails and on-screen disclosure. A labeled recording or a visible human in the loop is acceptable; an undisclosed edit or a hidden human becomes the story.
  • Measure what the event changed in customer behavior, against matched non-attendees, and track claim drift for 90 days after the event.
