Part II · Technical Product Management

Consumer Insights and Product Specifications

How customers decide, how to specify for it, and how to relaunch.

41 min read · 6 templates · 7 prompts

Research tells you what customers did. An insight explains why they did it, and a specification commits the team to acting on that explanation. This chapter covers the whole chain: how people and buying groups actually decide, where influencing those decisions turns into manipulation, how to write an insight that survives scrutiny, how to trace it into requirements, how to brief the people who must act on it, and how to plan a relaunch when the insight says the market sees you wrongly.

Why this matters

Most product teams have more customer evidence than they can use. Call recordings, support tickets, win/loss notes, usage data and interview snapshots pile up in connected systems. In 2026 an AI harness can summarize all of it in minutes. The bottleneck has moved from collecting evidence to explaining it: turning evidence into a claim about why customers behave as they do, then making sure that claim shapes what gets built.

Two failures are common, and they sit at opposite ends of the chain.

The first is the orphaned insight. A researcher or PM finds something real (“responders reject the agent’s diagnosis when they can’t see its evidence”) and presents it well. Everyone nods. Six months later the spec for the next release contains nothing that responds to it, because nobody wrote the link from insight to requirement. Orlena Gotel and Anthony Finkelstein, in their 1994 analysis of the requirements traceability problem, found that most traceability trouble sits before the specification: teams lose track of where a requirement came from and why it exists. The finding is more than thirty years old and still fits many backlogs.

The second is the orphaned requirement. A spec contains a default, a prompt, a pricing anchor or a cancellation flow that nobody traced to customer evidence or examined for its effect on behavior. Some of those choices shape what thousands of users do without their noticing. A few cross the line into what regulators call dark patterns, and in the last few years that line has acquired real legal consequences.

Senior PMs need both halves. You need a working model of how people decide, good enough to design for it and honest enough to know where the research is shaky. And you need a disciplined way to carry an insight into a specification, a stakeholder decision and, sometimes, a relaunch.

This chapter adds four artifacts to your Product 360: an insight brief, a behavioral design review with a dark-pattern check, a product specification with insight traceability, and a relaunch plan. Chapter 01 built the personas and hypotheses these artifacts draw on. Chapter 24 takes positioning further, and Chapter 25 covers launch mechanics in full.

How people decide

Two systems, used as a model

Daniel Kahneman’s Nobel lecture of 2002, later popularized in Thinking, Fast and Slow, describes two modes of thinking. He credits the labels System 1 and System 2 to Keith Stanovich and Richard West. System 1 is fast, automatic, effortless and associative, and hard to switch off. System 2 is slow, serial, effortful and deliberate. System 2 is supposed to monitor System 1, but the monitoring is normally lax: most of the time we accept what System 1 hands us.

Treat the two systems as a useful model of behavior, not as a map of the brain. For product work the model earns its place because it separates two kinds of decision your product hosts:

  • In-product decisions are mostly System 1. Accepting a suggested value, clicking the highlighted button, skimming a diagnosis card at 3 a.m. Users aren’t deliberating. Whatever is prominent, preselected or easiest tends to win.
  • Enterprise purchase decisions are System 2 rituals wrapped around System 1 impressions. The RFP, the security questionnaire and the business case are deliberate. The impression formed in the first demo, the first number quoted and the first reference call shapes how all that deliberation goes.

Design accordingly. The responder flow should make the right next action obvious and the supporting evidence visible without effort. The buyer’s evaluation material should give System 2 what it needs: comparable claims, proof and risk controls.

Biases worth knowing, with their evidence status

Some behavioral effects have held up better than others. Know which are robust, which are conditional and which are disputed, because your team will cite them to justify design choices.

Effect What it says Evidence status Product use
Anchoring An initial number pulls later estimates toward it, even when the number is arbitrary Robust. Replicated strongly in the Many Labs 1 project (Klein and colleagues, 2014); the mechanism is still debated First price, first ROI figure, first time-saved claim in a deal
Loss aversion Losses loom larger than equivalent gains Real but conditional. Moderated by experience and domain knowledge Framing migrations, renewals and downgrades
Choice overload Too many options can reduce choosing and satisfaction Contested. Average effect near zero; appears under specific conditions Pricing pages, integration catalogs, configuration
Defaults People tend to stay with the preselected option Among the stronger effects, with wide variation by context Every setting, threshold and opt-in in the product
Social proof Under uncertainty, people copy what similar others do Long-standing principle in persuasion research References, peer benchmarks, adoption indicators

Anchoring. Amos Tversky and Kahneman’s 1974 paper in Science described a wheel of fortune rigged to stop at either 10 or 65. Participants then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations. Those who saw 10 gave a median estimate of 25; those who saw 65 gave 45. The number was obviously random and still moved the answer. In B2B the first figure in a deal (a list price, a projected saving, a “time saved per incident”) becomes the reference point for the rest of the negotiation. The honest use of anchoring is to anchor on the customer’s own measured cost of the problem, from their data. The dishonest use is an invented industry figure that nobody can check.

Loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky’s prospect theory (1979) established that losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains. It became a staple of behavioral economics, and then an overgeneralized one. David Gal and Derek Rucker argued in 2018 that it had been treated as a law when the evidence supported something narrower. Kellen Mrkva, Eric Johnson, Simon Gächter and Andreas Herrmann responded in 2020 with evidence that loss aversion is real but moderated: it’s smaller in people with more domain knowledge and experience, and larger in older consumers. For B2B that matters. Expert evaluators comparing product attributes are less loss averse than end users. But organizations are strongly averse to a different kind of loss: visible failure that someone will be blamed for. This book’s synthesis is that in enterprise buying, fear of blame often outweighs the pull of an invisible gain.

Choice overload. Sheena Iyengar and Mark Lepper’s 2000 jam study is the famous one. A grocery tasting booth displayed either 24 or 6 jams. The large display attracted more shoppers, but a far smaller share of them bought. Then the replication record got complicated. Benjamin Scheibehenne, Rainer Greifeneder and Peter Todd’s 2010 meta-analysis of 50 experiments found an average effect of virtually zero, with large variation between studies. Alexander Chernev, Ulf Böckenholt and Joseph Goodman’s 2015 meta-analysis reconciled the camps: overload shows up when the choice set is complex, the decision task is hard, preferences are uncertain and the person’s goal is to choose rather than browse. So the defensible claim is narrower than the folklore: more options hurt under conditions you can diagnose. A first-time buyer on a pricing page with five tiers and twelve add-ons meets all four conditions. An expert configuring an integration they’ve used for years meets none of them. Give novices a recommended path and give experts full control.

Defaults. Eric Johnson and Daniel Goldstein’s 2003 Science paper “Do Defaults Save Lives?” showed that organ-donor consent rates were far higher in countries with opt-out systems than in countries with opt-in systems. Use it carefully: consent rates differed far more than actual donation rates, so it demonstrates the power of defaults over stated choice, not over outcomes. A 2019 meta-analysis by Jachimowicz, Duncan, Weber and Johnson pooled 58 studies with 73,675 participants and found a medium-to-large average effect (d = 0.68), with wide variation across studies: most found the default pulled choices toward it, a few found no effect and two found the opposite.

Social proof. Robert Cialdini’s work on persuasion lists social proof among his principles: when uncertain, people look to what others, especially similar others, are doing. In B2B its legitimate forms are reference customers, advisory boards and “teams like yours” benchmarks built from real data. Its illegitimate forms, fake activity counters and fabricated reviews, are named deceptive patterns and enforcement targets.

Key ideaHow much of nudging survives?

In 2022 Mertens and colleagues published a meta-analysis in PNAS estimating a meaningful average effect for choice-architecture interventions (d = 0.43). Maier and colleagues reanalyzed it in the same journal the same year and, after correcting for publication bias, estimated the average effect at d = 0.04. Their title said “no evidence for nudging”; the authors later said “no overall evidence” would have been the better phrase, because the effects vary widely and some interventions may still work. Defaults and other structural changes came out undecided rather than refuted. The practical lesson: don’t cite a bias as if it guarantees an outcome. Treat each behavioral design choice as a hypothesis and measure it in your own product.

Defaults in enterprise software are policy

In consumer apps a default nudges one person. In enterprise software a default is a decision the vendor makes for every customer who never opens that settings page, which is most of them. The default autonomy level for an agent, the default data-retention period, the default alert threshold, whether telemetry sharing is on: each is a policy applied to thousands of organizations at once.

That gives defaults three properties worth writing into your specs. They need an owner, because someone must answer for them. They need a rationale grounded in what is good for the customer, not just what’s good for a metric. And they need a measurement, because the default will drive the behavior you later report as customer preference. If 90% of customers run with telemetry on and the default is on, you’ve measured your default, not their wishes.

How B2B buying groups decide

Chapter 01 introduced the buying group: user, champion, economic buyer, technical evaluator and procurement or legal. For insight work the consequence is that the unit of analysis changes. A consumer insight explains one person’s decision. A B2B insight has to explain a group’s decision, where the person who loves the product may not be the one who can stop it.

Three features of group buying shape the insights you need.

Risk reduction often beats value addition. A buying group can say no at many points and yes only at the end, after consensus. Anyone with veto power (security, procurement, legal) gains little from a successful purchase and is blamed for a failed one. Insights that matter most are therefore often about fear: what would make this person look bad, and what evidence would let them approve. Reversibility, pilots, audit logs, scoped access and clear exit terms are product features that answer insights about risk.

Buyers research without you, increasingly through AI. A Gartner survey of 646 B2B buyers, fielded in August and September 2025 and published in March 2026, reported that 67% prefer a buying experience without a sales rep and that 45% had used AI tools during a recent purchase. This book’s inference is that your product claims now need to survive being summarized by a model: specific, evidenced and consistent across your documentation, pricing pages and security materials. An AI assistant asked “which incident tools can act autonomously and how is that controlled?” will answer from whatever you published. Insight work should include how those assistants currently describe your product.

Consensus is the product of artifacts. The champion has to sell internally to people who never saw the demo. The materials they carry (a business case, a security pack, a reference) decide whether consensus forms.

This book recommends a simple buying-group insight map: for each role, capture the job they’re doing in the purchase, the fear that drives them, the proof they need and the veto power they hold. It turns Chapter 01’s personas into decision insight.

Role Job in the purchase Fear Proof they need Veto power
End user Get the work done with less effort More work before less Hands-on trial in real conditions Informal: low adoption kills renewal
Champion Fix a team problem and get credit Visible failure on their watch Peer references, quick early win None, but carries the case
Economic buyer Hit a target within budget ROI story fails with finance Business case built on own data Final
Technical evaluator Keep the environment safe Blamed for a breach or outage Controls, audit, documentation Hard block

Persuasion, manipulation and the law

Where the line is

All product design influences behavior. Order, prominence, wording and defaults are unavoidable; there’s no neutral screen. The useful question is whether the influence serves the user’s goals or works against them.

Harry Brignull coined the term dark patterns in 2010 for interfaces designed to trick people into doing things they didn’t mean to do. He now prefers “deceptive patterns,” and his current catalog lists 18 types, including sneaking, forced action, hard to cancel, preselection, obstruction, hidden subscription, hidden costs, trick wording, visual interference, fake social proof, fake urgency, nagging, fake scarcity, confirmshaming, comparison prevention and addictive design. Mathur and colleagues’ 2019 crawl of about 11,000 shopping websites found 1,818 dark-pattern instances, sorted into seven categories, which established that the problem was systematic rather than anecdotal.

This book offers four tests to separate persuasion from manipulation. A design choice is acceptable when:

  1. It serves the user’s own goal, not only yours.
  2. It would survive disclosure. If you explained the design rationale to the user, they wouldn’t feel tricked.
  3. It’s symmetric. Getting out is as easy as getting in; declining is as easy as accepting.
  4. It uses true information. Urgency, scarcity, social proof and prices are real.

A choice that fails any one of the four needs to change or be justified in writing by someone accountable.

The regulatory position in 2026

Regulators now name dark patterns explicitly. State their positions precisely; they differ between jurisdictions and they’re still moving.

United States. There’s no dedicated federal dark-patterns statute. The Federal Trade Commission enforces through Section 5 of the FTC Act (deceptive or unfair practices) and, for subscriptions, the Restore Online Shoppers’ Confidence Act (ROSCA). The FTC’s September 2022 staff report, Bringing Dark Patterns to Light, which followed a 2021 public workshop, defines dark patterns as design practices that trick or manipulate users into choices they wouldn’t otherwise make. It groups them under four headings: designs that induce false beliefs, designs that hide or delay material information, designs that lead to unauthorized charges, and designs that obscure or subvert privacy choices. On subscriptions it restates that cancelling should be at least as easy as signing up, through the same medium.

In September 2025 Amazon agreed to a $2.5 billion settlement of the FTC’s 2023 complaint about Prime enrollment and cancellation: a $1 billion civil penalty and $1.5 billion in customer refunds, with required changes including a clear way to decline Prime and cancellation through the same method used to sign up. Amazon neither admitted nor denied the allegations. Separately, the FTC’s broader “click-to-cancel” rule was vacated by the Eighth Circuit in July 2025 on procedural grounds, without a ruling on its merits. ROSCA and state auto-renewal laws still apply, and reports in early 2026 indicated the FTC was reopening rulemaking. Check the current status with counsel before relying on any of this.

European Union. Article 25 of the Digital Services Act (Regulation (EU) 2022/2065) bars providers of online platforms from designing interfaces that deceive or manipulate users or otherwise materially distort their ability to make free and informed decisions. It names three areas for Commission guidance: giving certain choices more prominence, repeatedly asking for a choice already made, and making it harder to terminate a service than to subscribe to it. Article 25 doesn’t apply where the Unfair Commercial Practices Directive or the GDPR already covers the practice. Note its scope: it binds online platforms, a defined DSA category, and most B2B SaaS consoles aren’t online platforms. Treat it as a design standard worth adopting, and get legal review before treating it as an obligation for your product.

Two further EU instruments matter for AI-era products. The AI Act’s Article 5, which has applied since 2 February 2025, prohibits AI systems that use subliminal, purposefully manipulative or deceptive techniques to materially distort behavior in ways likely to cause significant harm, and AI that exploits vulnerabilities due to age, disability or social or economic situation. And the Commission has consulted on a Digital Fairness Act expected to address dark patterns, addictive design and subscription traps; as of this writing the proposal hasn’t been published, and law-firm briefings expect it in November 2026.

NoteB2B is not exempt

B2B teams tend to see dark patterns as a consumer problem. They aren’t. Auto-renewal terms buried in an order form, seats that expand silently, data export hidden three menus deep, telemetry sharing preselected on, and upgrade prompts that reappear after every dismissal all appear in enterprise SaaS. Admins also make choices on behalf of hundreds of users, which multiplies the effect of any pattern aimed at them.

From insight to specification

What makes an insight

A finding says what happened: “40% of diagnoses were rejected last month.” An insight says why it happened and what follows: responders reject diagnoses when they can’t verify them quickly, because a past wrong answer taught them to distrust uncited claims.

Stanford’s design-thinking courses teach a compact point-of-view statement: [user] needs [need] because [insight]. It’s a good base, but in B2B it leaves out the evidence, the buying-group context and how sure you are. This book extends it into a structured insight statement:

  • Who: role, context and position in the buying group.
  • Observed: the behavior, with its evidence (interviews, usage data, tickets).
  • Because: the motivation or constraint that explains the behavior.
  • Tension: what they want against what stops them.
  • So we believe: the implication for the product.
  • Confidence: high, medium or low, and what would change your mind.

Four tests separate a real insight from a restated finding. It’s non-obvious (the team didn’t already act as if it were true). It explains behavior (why, not what). It’s grounded in evidence you can point to. And it implies action: someone reading it knows what to do differently. An insight that fails the last test is trivia.

On-rampIf insight writing is new to you

Take one finding from your last research readout and ask “why?” until you reach a reason grounded in what customers said or did. Write that reason as the “because” clause, then ask what the team would do differently if it were true. If the answer is “nothing,” keep digging or drop it.

Traceability from insight to requirement

Gotel and Finkelstein defined requirements traceability as the ability to follow a requirement’s life both forwards and backwards. They split it in two. Pre-specification traceability covers where a requirement came from: the customer, the evidence, the reasoning. Post-specification traceability covers what happened afterward: design, code, tests. Most tools handle the second half well; the first half is usually a link to a slide nobody can find.

This book’s traceability rules are simple enough to check with an AI harness every sprint:

  1. Every requirement traces back to at least one insight, or to an explicitly named strategic, compliance or technical driver. “The VP asked” is a driver; write it down as one.
  2. Every high-confidence insight traces forward to a decision: build, defer or reject, with a reason.
  3. Every requirement carries an acceptance criterion and a success metric. For AI behavior, the metric is an eval with a threshold (Chapter 31).
  4. Results flow back. When the metric comes in, update the insight’s confidence. An insight that drove three releases and moved nothing should be demoted.
For product leaders

Make the insight register a shared portfolio asset with stable IDs, owned like the persona set from Chapter 01. When PRDs across teams cite the same insight IDs, you can see which customer truths are driving investment and which high-confidence insights nobody has acted on. Ask for the orphaned-insight list in quarterly reviews.

The specification as a behavioral document

A product specification (PRD, one-pager or spec, whatever your company calls it) is usually read as a list of features. Read it instead as a set of decisions about how users will behave. Three sections deserve more weight than they usually get.

Behavioral design decisions. Every default, framing, choice set and prompt in the spec is an intervention. Write each down with its rationale and the four-test result from the persuasion section above.

AI behavior. For AI features, specify expected outputs, when the system should decline or escalate, how confidence and evidence are shown, and which evals gate release. Chapter 31 covers evals in depth.

Acceptance criteria in Given, When, Then form. Behavior-driven development’s format (“Given [context], when [event], then [observable outcome]”) forces each requirement into something a test or an eval can check. If you can’t write the “then,” the requirement isn’t finished.

Communicating insights: the insight brief

An insight that only the researcher understands changes nothing. The insight brief is this book’s one-page format for carrying an insight to the people who must act on it.

Its structure follows Barbara Minto’s pyramid principle: lead with the governing point and the decision it informs, then the support. Method goes last, if at all. The brief contains the headline insight in one sentence; the so-what for the decision at hand; the evidence, with its sources, sample size, strength and any contrary evidence; who is affected and what revenue is at stake; options and a recommendation; confidence and what would change it; and the ask, with an owner and a date.

Two habits make briefs credible. Separate observation from interpretation: “eleven of fourteen participants scrolled past the summary to open the raw data” is observation; “they don’t trust it” is interpretation, and readers should see the seam. And show contrary evidence. A brief with no disconfirming data reads as advocacy, and senior readers discount it.

Tailor the brief by audience without changing its substance. Executives want the implication, the risk and the ask. Engineering wants the behavior, the edge cases and the acceptance criteria. Sales and marketing want the buyer’s language, verbatim. Chapter 34 covers persuasive writing in more depth.

Relaunch and repositioning

Sometimes the insight isn’t about a feature. It’s that the market understands your product wrongly: it puts you in the wrong category, compares you to the wrong alternatives or values you for the wrong thing. That calls for repositioning, and when the change is large enough, a relaunch.

April Dunford’s positioning method defines five components, worked in order: competitive alternatives (what customers would use if you didn’t exist), differentiated capabilities, the value those capabilities enable, the target customers who care most, and the market category that frames it all. She names three triggers for repositioning: competitors you didn’t expect start showing up in deals, your value resonates with a different segment than planned, or you’re entering new segments. This book adds two more that matter for AI-era products: the product’s capability changed enough that the old category undersells it, and a trust event requires a visible reset.

Relaunches spend customer and field attention, which is always scarce. Don’t relaunch when the problem is product quality or onboarding rather than perception; a new story on the same experience only brings new customers into the old problems faster. Don’t relaunch an incremental change.

A relaunch plan covers the trigger and its evidence; the positioning delta (old against new, for each of Dunford’s five components); target segments and the message for each buying-group role; the product changes that ship with it; the plan for existing customers; channel and enablement; metrics with baselines; and risks with a rollback. The existing-customer section is where relaunches go wrong ethically. Migrations that happen silently, contracts that change on renewal without clear notice and exports that become harder after the switch are the B2B versions of hard-to-cancel and sneaking.

Measure the relaunch against a baseline set before launch. Leading indicators: qualified pipeline in the new category, win rate against the new competitor set, sales-cycle length, message recall in win/loss interviews and activation of the new capability. Lagging indicators: net revenue retention, expansion and churn in migrated cohorts. Compare cohorts rather than totals, and set the review date in advance so the result can’t be quietly reinterpreted. Chapter 25 covers launch execution and Chapter 30 covers migrating customers off retired products.

How to apply it

This method runs alongside continuous discovery (Chapter 01). It takes a body of evidence to a decided, specified and communicated change in about four weeks.

Week 1: Frame and synthesize.

  1. Write the decision you need to inform, with its owner and date. Insight work without a decision drifts.
  2. Gather the evidence through your harness’s connectors: interview snapshots, call recordings, tickets, post-incident reviews, usage reports. Use approved tools for customer data.
  3. Ask the harness for a first-pass clustering, with every claim cited to a source record. Read the sources behind the top clusters yourself.
  4. Map the buying group’s decision: job, fear, proof and veto for each role.

Week 2: Write insights and brief them.

  1. Write three to five insight statements. Test each against the four criteria (non-obvious, explains behavior, grounded, implies action). Kill the ones that fail.
  2. Write the insight brief for the decision owner. Review it with one skeptic before it goes out.
  3. Get a decision: build, defer or reject, recorded in the decision log.

Week 3: Specify and review.

  1. Draft the specification with linked insight IDs, acceptance criteria and success metrics.
  2. List every behavioral design decision in it. Run the behavioral design review, including the dark-pattern check, with design, engineering and someone from legal or compliance for anything touching billing, consent or data.
  3. Build the traceability table. Have the harness flag requirements with no upstream insight and insights with no downstream decision.

Week 4: Commit and measure.

  1. Agree baselines and the review date for each success metric before work starts.
  2. If the insight concerns market perception rather than a feature, open a relaunch plan and test the new positioning with five to ten customers before committing a launch moment.
  3. After release, update insight confidence with the results and close the loop in the brief.

The AI lens

Building AI-powered products: trust is a behavioral question

For AI products, the most important consumer insight is usually about trust: when people rely on the system, when they don’t, and whether that matches how good it actually is.

John Lee and Katrina See’s 2004 review, “Trust in Automation,” set the goal that still holds: calibrated trust, where trust matches the system’s actual trustworthiness. Too much trust produces misuse (accepting wrong answers); too little produces disuse (ignoring right ones).

Two research findings pull in opposite directions, and both apply. Berkeley Dietvorst, Joseph Simmons and Cade Massey showed in 2015 that people lose confidence in an algorithm faster than in a human after seeing it make the same mistake: algorithm aversion. Jennifer Logg, Julia Minson and Don Moore showed in 2019 that lay people often weight algorithmic advice above human advice, though experts discounted it: algorithm appreciation. Read together, people may over-trust before they see an error and under-trust after. Dietvorst, Simmons and Massey’s 2018 follow-up found a practical fix: people use imperfect algorithms more when they can modify the output, even slightly.

The human-in-the-loop design doesn’t solve this automatically. Michelle Vaccaro, Abdullah Almaatouq and Thomas Malone’s 2024 meta-analysis in Nature Human Behaviour, covering more than 100 experiments, found that human-AI combinations on average performed worse than the better of the human or the AI alone, with losses concentrated in decision tasks and gains in content-creation tasks. An approval gate is only as good as the reviewer’s ability to judge what they’re approving. The EU AI Act recognizes the risk: Article 14 requires that oversight measures for high-risk systems help people stay aware of automation bias, the tendency to over-rely on system output.

Specify trust as behavior. Put evidence where the decision happens. Show confidence honestly. Let users adjust the output rather than only accept or reject. Measure both acceptance and wrong acceptance, because a rising acceptance rate is good news only if the accepted answers are right. And treat manipulation by AI as a dark pattern: an agent that flatters, pressures or exploits a user’s situation can fall under the AI Act’s Article 5 prohibitions, which apply in the EU.

Doing the work with AI

AI-assisted synthesis is now normal. The Nielsen Norman Group’s guidance, by Kate Moran and Maria Rosala and last reviewed in January 2026, is to delegate transcription, first-pass summaries, initial grouping and redaction of personal data, and to keep interpretation and fact-checking with the researcher. Their phrase for AI’s thematic coding: “an initial pass.” An earlier NN/g review by Feifei Liu and Moran found AI research tools missed visual context, produced vague findings and often couldn’t cite back to sessions, and advised against using them where the output can’t be checked.

In a harness with connectors, the PM’s job shifts toward three things: framing the question precisely, checking citations back to source records and judging confidence. Two practices help. Ask for counts and citations, not adjectives: “seven of twelve calls, with IDs” rather than “many customers.” And ask for what doesn’t fit: the unclassifiable records and the contrary cases are where real insight hides and where models smooth things over.

The Beacon case: why responders don’t accept the diagnosis

Beacon caseThe situation

Beacon Agents is heading for launch at Northlight Live. Diagnose is live with design partners, and its key metric, diagnosis acceptance rate, is lower than the business case assumed. Earlier this year a hallucinated root cause led one customer to roll back the wrong service. Pricing is moving from per-environment to per-resolved-incident, Beacon Classic is being sunset, and gross margin has fallen from 81% to 72% under inference cost. You have four weeks before the Agents spec freezes.

Synthesis. You connect the harness to design-partner call recordings, support tickets and post-incident reviews, and ask it to group every rejected diagnosis by the stated reason, citing the record for each. You read the sources behind the largest cluster yourself. The pattern: responders reject diagnoses when the supporting evidence isn’t on the first screen. They scroll past the card to open logs and verify by hand. Several cite the wrong-rollback incident by name.

The insight. “On-call engineers like Dana need to verify a proposed root cause in under a minute, because since the wrong-rollback incident they treat any uncited claim as wrong.” It passes the four tests: the team had assumed accuracy was the constraint (non-obvious), it explains the scrolling (behavior), it’s grounded in calls and session evidence, and it implies a design change. It fits the pattern of algorithm aversion: after one visible error, trust in later answers dropped.

The brief. Your insight brief to the VP of Product and the Beacon engineering leads with one line: trust, more than accuracy, is the adoption constraint for Diagnose and Act. It shows contrary evidence too: two partners accept most diagnoses without opening the evidence, which you flag as a possible over-reliance risk rather than a success.

The buying group. The map sharpens it. Priya fears the agent will make things worse. Dana fears being blamed at 3 a.m. Marcus fears an ROI story that won’t survive his CFO. Sam fears uncontrolled production access and needs AI Act documentation for EU sales. Leo fears margin erosion across 60 customers. None of them names a smarter model as the first need.

The behavioral design review. The Act approval gate fails two of the four tests. “Approve” is visually dominant over “Reject,” which pushes System 1 toward approval and invites automation bias. And the admin console nags admins to expand the autonomous allowlist after every successful action. The fixes: medium-risk actions now require the evidence summary to be shown before approval is possible; the default autonomy level stays conservative; allowlist expansion becomes admin-initiated only; and turning autonomy off is one step, the same as turning it on.

The spec. The “evidence-first diagnosis card” traces INS-07 to REQ-21: show the top three evidence items, each with a source link, above the fold. Acceptance criterion: Given a diagnosis with cited evidence, when the card renders, then the three highest-weighted evidence items and their source links are visible without scrolling. Success metrics: diagnosis acceptance rate and wrong-acceptance rate, measured through post-incident review, with an eval set built from resolved incidents through the Learn capability.

Pricing. For per-resolved-incident pricing, the business-case template anchors on each customer’s own measured cost of an incident hour, from their data, rather than an industry figure. The pricing page keeps the old and new plans side by side so customers can compare (Chapter 15 covers the pricing decision itself).

The relaunch. The evidence supports repositioning Beacon from “AIOps alert correlation,” where it’s compared with features bundled into observability platforms, to “agentic incident resolution,” where the main alternative is humans plus runbooks plus chat. The relaunch plan works through Dunford’s five components, old against new. Classic customers get a published sunset timeline, no silent auto-migration and one-step data export. The scorecard has baselines set before Northlight Live: pipeline in the new category, win rate against startups building ops agents, sales-cycle length, Beacon Agents activation, autonomous resolution rate, NRR in the migrated cohort against today’s 118%, and gross margin against today’s 72%.

Prompts

✦ PromptCluster evidence into cited themes
Goal: Get a first-pass synthesis you can verify, with every claim traceable to a record.
Paste in (or connect the harness to): interview snapshots, call recordings or transcripts, support tickets and review notes for one question. Use only tools approved for customer data, and redact personal identifiers first.

I need to understand [question, for example: why customers reject X / why deals stall at Y]. Using only the material provided:

1. Group the records by the reason or behavior they show. Name each group from the customer's point of view.
2. For each group, give the count of records and list every record ID. Quote one representative line verbatim with its ID.
3. Separate what customers did (observed behavior) from what they said (stated reasons).
4. List the records you could not classify, and the records that contradict the largest group.
5. Note any group that appears in only one customer, segment or buying-group role.

Use counts, not adjectives like "many" or "most." Do not infer anything the records do not say.

Check the output: open the records behind the two largest groups and confirm the quotes and counts. Read every unclassified and contradicting record yourself; that's where the model is most likely to have smoothed something over.
✦ PromptTurn findings into insight statements
Goal: Move from "what happened" to "why it happened and what follows."
Paste in: the cited themes from your synthesis and the decision you need to inform.

For the decision [decision], turn these themes into at most five insight statements. For each, write:
- One sentence: [user] needs [need] because [explanation].
- Who, Observed, Evidence (with record IDs), Because, Tension, So we believe, Confidence (high/medium/low) and what would change our mind.

Anti-patterns

Anti-patternThe insight deck that changes nothing

A well-made research readout gets applause and no decision. Six months later no requirement traces to it. Every insight brief ends with an ask, an owner and a date, and every high-confidence insight traces forward to build, defer or reject.

Anti-patternBias citations as proof

“Loss aversion means customers will upgrade to avoid losing features.” The team cites a bias as if it guarantees an outcome. Several famous effects are conditional or contested. Treat behavioral design as a hypothesis and measure it in your product.

Anti-patternMeasuring your own defaults

Ninety percent of customers keep a setting on, and the team reports it as customer preference. It may only show the power of the default. Before claiming preference, check what customers chose when the default was different or when they were asked explicitly.

Anti-pattern"It's B2B, dark patterns don't apply"

Auto-renewals buried in order forms, silent seat expansion, hard-to-find data export and nagging upgrade prompts all appear in enterprise products. Run the behavioral design review on billing, consent, data and migration flows, whatever the market.

Anti-patternOptimizing for acceptance

An AI feature’s acceptance rate rises, and the team celebrates. Nobody measured whether accepted outputs were right. Rising acceptance with no measure of wrong acceptance can mean the product is training users to over-rely on it.

Anti-patternThe cosmetic relaunch

The product gets a new category name and a launch event while the onboarding problem that caused churn stays unchanged. New customers arrive faster into the same experience. Relaunch only when the change is real and perception is the actual problem.

Checklist

Key takeaways

  • A finding says what happened; an insight explains why and implies action. Write insights in a structured form with evidence and confidence, and test them before briefing them.
  • Use decision psychology with its evidence status attached. Anchoring and defaults are relatively robust; loss aversion and choice overload are conditional; average nudge effects shrink sharply after correcting for publication bias.
  • In B2B the buying group decides, and fear of blame often outweighs the pull of invisible gains. Map each role’s job, fear, proof and veto, and build risk reduction into the product.
  • In enterprise software, defaults are policy. Give each one an owner, a rationale and a measurement, and run a behavioral design review that checks for dark patterns in every market.
  • Traceability starts before the spec. Link every requirement to an insight or named driver, and every high-confidence insight to a decision, then feed results back.
  • For AI products, aim for calibrated trust. Show evidence where the decision happens, let users adjust the output, and measure wrong acceptance alongside acceptance.
  • Relaunch only when the market’s perception is the problem and the product has really changed. Set baselines before launch and measure cohorts, not totals.
