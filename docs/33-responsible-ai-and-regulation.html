Part VIII · AI-Era Product Management

Responsible AI and Regulation for PMs

The EU AI Act, NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 in product terms.

43 min read · 7 templates · 7 prompts

Responsible AI used to live on a values page and in a legal review at the end of the cycle. In 2026 it lives in the product: in the disclosure a user sees, the logs a customer may need to keep, the approval gate on an agent's riskiest action and the answers you give a bank's security reviewer. This chapter turns principles, the EU AI Act, the NIST AI Risk Management Framework and ISO/IEC 42001 into decisions a PM makes and evidence a PM owns. Counsel owns the legal answers; this chapter covers the product half of the work, and none of it is legal advice.

Why this matters

Chapter 31 made the case that regulation is now a product input. This chapter is about what that means on an ordinary Tuesday, when a sales engineer forwards a long AI questionnaire from a renewal that closes in three weeks, or an engineer asks whether a new connector needs “a review.”

Three things changed between 2024 and 2026.

The rules became dated and specific. The EU AI Act’s prohibitions have applied since February 2025, its obligations for general-purpose AI models since August 2025 and its transparency duties since August 2026. The Digital Omnibus on AI, adopted in July 2026, moved the high-risk obligations to December 2027 and August 2028. The delay helps less than it seems: logging, human oversight, accuracy evidence and instructions for use take quarters to build, and you can’t retrofit logs for decisions your product has already made.

Customers turned responsible AI into a buying criterion. An enterprise customer will ask for evidence long before any regulator does. Security reviewers now ask what model you use, whether it trains on their data, how you test for prompt injection, who can switch the agent off and what your EU AI Act role is. Chapter 01 called this adoptability: a product can be desirable, feasible and viable, and still die in a questionnaire.

Agents raised the stakes. A model that summarizes can embarrass you; an agent that acts can change a customer’s production system. In December 2025 the OWASP GenAI Security Project published a separate Top 10 for agentic applications, covering risks such as tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, and one failure cascading into others.

Inside product orgs, responsible AI usually fails the same way. A principles document gets published, a review board gets formed, and neither touches the backlog, so teams learn to describe features in words that avoid the review. The fix is to treat each principle as a product requirement with an owner, a test and an artifact, and to run review in proportion to risk so that low-risk work moves fast and high-risk work gets real scrutiny.

The artifacts in this chapter (a use-case triage, an impact assessment, a system card, a red-team plan, an incident plan, a questionnaire answer bank and a review checklist) sit mostly in the Operate dimension of your Product 360, next to the risk register and the AI risk assessment from Chapter 31.

On-rampIf you've never worked with regulation before

You don’t need to read the law, but you need three facts about each AI feature you own: your role (are you the provider who builds it or the deployer who uses it?), its risk tier (does it fall into a category the law treats as high-risk?) and its transparency duties (must people be told they’re dealing with AI?). Write them in the PRD and ask counsel to confirm them; most of this chapter follows from them.

The frameworks a PM actually needs

Principles become product requirements

Responsible AI frameworks list similar values. The OECD AI Principles, adopted in 2019 and updated in May 2024, name five: inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; and accountability. Microsoft’s six principles are fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency and accountability. NIST’s AI RMF describes the characteristics of trustworthy AI in similar terms.

The lists broadly agree; the hard part is translation. A principle helps a team only when it becomes a question someone must answer, a thing someone must build and evidence someone can show. This book uses six principles and maps each to product practice.

Principle The product question What the PM specifies Evidence
Fairness Who could be treated worse, and how would we know? Evals sliced by segment, language and region; no profiling of people without review Eval report with slices
Transparency Does the user know it’s AI, and why it said this? AI disclosure, cited evidence, confidence cues, labels on generated content UX spec, system card
Accountability Who owns each risk, and who can stop the launch? A named risk owner per use case, a review gate, a decision log Review record
Privacy What personal data enters prompts, logs and training? Data minimization, retention limits, no training on customer data by default, DPIA triggers DPIA, data flow diagram
Safety What’s the worst action the system can take? Autonomy limits, approval gates, a kill switch, action allowlists Red-team plan, autonomy matrix
Security How can it be attacked or misused? Prompt-injection defenses, least-privilege tools, scoped connectors Threat model, questionnaire answers

The table is this book’s synthesis rather than a published standard, and its most useful column is the last one. A principle with no evidence behind it in your product is still only a statement of intent.

Key ideaPrinciples need owners and evidence

For each principle, name the person who answers the product question, the artifact that holds the answer and the gate that checks it. A principle without all three won’t survive a deadline.

The EU AI Act in product terms

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) entered into force in August 2024. In July 2026 the EU amended it with the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744). It reaches AI systems placed on the EU market or used in the EU, wherever the company is based. For a PM, three ideas carry most of the weight: roles, risk tiers and obligations by tier.

Roles. The Act assigns duties by role. The provider develops an AI system (or has one developed) and places it on the market under its own name. The deployer uses an AI system under its own authority. Importers, distributors and authorized representatives have smaller roles; providers of general-purpose AI (GPAI) models have a separate track. In a typical B2B stack, the model lab is the GPAI model provider, the SaaS company that builds a product on the model is the provider of the AI system, and the enterprise customer that uses the product is the deployer. A vendor that uses its own AI internally (say, to screen job applicants) is also a deployer.

Roles can switch. Under Article 25(1), a deployer, distributor or importer becomes the provider of a high-risk system if it puts its own name or trademark on it, substantially modifies it, or repurposes a system (including a general-purpose one) so that it becomes high-risk. Platform PMs feel this most: if customers can configure your agent to screen job candidates or score credit, they may turn themselves into providers, so your terms and documentation must state what the system is for and what it must not be used for.

Risk tiers. The Act sorts AI systems by intended use.

  • Prohibited practices (Article 5) are banned outright. New prohibitions on generating non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material apply from 2 December 2026.
  • High-risk systems come in two kinds. Annex I covers AI in products already governed by the EU product safety laws that annex lists. Annex III lists eight use areas: biometrics; critical infrastructure; education; employment; essential services such as credit scoring and life and health insurance pricing; law enforcement; migration and border control; and justice and democratic processes.
  • Transparency-only systems (Article 50) carry disclosure and marking duties.
  • Everything else is minimal risk, with no specific duties beyond AI literacy.

Annex III has an exit. Under Article 6(3), a system in an Annex III area isn’t high-risk if it poses no significant risk and performs a narrow procedural task, improves the result of a completed human activity, detects patterns or deviations without replacing human assessment, or performs a preparatory task. The exit closes for profiling: an Annex III system that profiles people “shall always be considered to be high-risk” (Article 6(3)). A provider relying on the exit must document the assessment before placing the system on the market and register it (Article 6(4) and Article 49(2)). The Omnibus kept that registration.

Obligations by tier. For high-risk systems, Article 16 lists what providers must do: a risk management system, data governance, technical documentation, automatic logging, transparency and instructions for use for deployers, human oversight, and accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity, plus a quality management system, conformity assessment, an EU declaration of conformity, CE marking, registration and corrective action when something goes wrong.

Deployers have their own list in Article 26: follow the provider’s instructions, assign human oversight to competent people with authority to act, keep input data they control relevant and representative, monitor operation and suspend use if they see a risk, keep the logs under their control for at least six months, inform workers before workplace use and inform people when a high-risk system makes or supports decisions about them. Most of those duties depend on something the provider builds: logs, oversight controls and clear instructions.

Some deployers also owe a fundamental rights impact assessment (FRIA) under Article 27 before first use: public bodies, private entities providing public services, and deployers using AI for credit scoring or life and health insurance pricing. It covers the deployer’s process, frequency of use, affected people, specific risks, human oversight and what happens if harm occurs, and it complements any GDPR data protection impact assessment rather than duplicating it.

Transparency duties reach more systems, and they already apply. Under Article 50, providers must design systems that interact with people so that people know they’re dealing with AI, unless it’s obvious, and must mark synthetic audio, image, video and text in a machine-readable, detectable format where technically feasible. Deployers must inform people exposed to emotion recognition or biometric categorization, disclose deep fakes, and disclose AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest unless a human reviewed it and took editorial responsibility. A voluntary Code of Practice on marking and labelling AI-generated content, finalized in June 2026, gives providers and deployers a recognized way to meet these duties.

Serious incidents. Once the high-risk rules apply, Article 73 requires providers to report serious incidents to the market surveillance authority of the Member State where they occurred: no later than 15 days after becoming aware, no later than 10 days for a death, and no later than 2 days for a widespread infringement or a serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure (Article 3(49)(b)). An incomplete first report is allowed. Deployers report to the provider first. If your product runs inside customers’ infrastructure operations, the two-day clock is the one to design your incident plan around.

Penalties. Under Article 99, prohibited practices can cost up to EUR 35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher. Breaches of other operator obligations can cost up to EUR 15 million or 3%, and supplying incorrect information to authorities up to EUR 7.5 million or 1%. For SMEs and the small mid-caps the Omnibus added, the lower of the two figures applies.

What to build in now. Most of what the high-risk rules require is what enterprise customers already ask for, so build it in even if none of your features is high-risk:

  • An intended-purpose statement and a prohibited-use list in the product terms and documentation.
  • Logging that lets a deployer keep at least six months of records.
  • Human oversight controls: approval gates, overrides and a way to stop the system.
  • Instructions for use written for the deployer, covering capabilities, limits and oversight.
  • Accuracy and robustness metrics that come from your eval suite (Chapter 31).
  • AI disclosure in the interface and machine-readable marking of generated content.
  • A documented Article 6(3) assessment wherever a use case sits in an Annex III area and you believe it isn’t high-risk.

NIST AI RMF and the Generative AI Profile

The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) is voluntary, and US enterprise and public sector buyers often ask vendors to align with it rather than with EU rules. Its core has four functions:

  • Govern: the culture, policies, roles and accountability that run across everything else.
  • Map: the context in which the system will be used, so risks can be framed.
  • Measure: quantitative and qualitative analysis, evaluation and monitoring.
  • Manage: prioritizing risks, allocating resources, responding, recovering and communicating.

For a PM the mapping is direct. Map is your home function: intended use, users, affected people and harms. Measure is your eval suite. Manage is your launch gates and incident response. Govern is your organization’s review process.

The Generative AI Profile (NIST AI 600-1, July 2024) tailors the framework to generative AI. It lists twelve risks that generative AI creates or worsens: CBRN information or capabilities; confabulation; dangerous, violent or hateful content; data privacy; environmental impacts; harmful bias or homogenization; human-AI configuration; information integrity; information security; intellectual property; obscene, degrading or abusive content; and value chain and component integration. It defines confabulation as “confidently stated but erroneous or false content,” a more precise term for what most teams call hallucination. The list works as a checklist for impact assessments and red-team scope.

NIST says the AI RMF is being revised under the July 2025 US AI Action Plan, with no date published, and in April 2026 it issued a concept note for a critical infrastructure profile. Check for a new version before you cite it in a customer document.

ISO/IEC 42001

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 is the first certifiable management system standard for AI. It asks an organization to run AI the way ISO/IEC 27001 asks it to run information security: with a policy, defined roles, risk and impact assessments, controls, internal audit and continual improvement. In enterprise questionnaires it’s becoming the AI counterpart of ISO/IEC 27001.

Certification is a company decision, usually led by security or compliance. Your part is the per-product evidence an auditor will sample: the impact assessment for each AI system, lifecycle records (what changed, when, who approved it) and controls on suppliers such as model providers. Design your artifacts so the management system can use them without rework.

The US: states move, Washington pushes back

The US has no comprehensive federal AI law. The states are legislating, and in 2026 the federal government is pushing back.

  • Colorado enacted SB24-205, a broad law on algorithmic discrimination by high-risk AI systems, in May 2024. A 2025 special session (SB25B-004) delayed its duties to 30 June 2026. That date has passed, so before you plan around the law, confirm with counsel whether those duties now apply as written or were changed again in the 2026 session.
  • California’s SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, signed in September 2025, requires large frontier model developers to publish a safety framework and report critical safety incidents. It targets model developers rather than most B2B application vendors, though your model provider’s disclosures under it can feed your own documentation.
  • Texas enacted HB 149, the Texas Responsible AI Governance Act, in June 2025, effective 1 January 2026. It regulates the use of AI systems in the state and carries civil penalties.

At the federal level, Executive Order 14365, signed on 11 December 2025, aims at a national policy framework and at challenging state AI laws the administration considers burdensome; it names Colorado’s law. It directed a Department of Justice AI Litigation Task Force, a Commerce Department review of state laws, an FTC policy statement on AI and a recommendation for federal legislation that would preempt state laws, with carve-outs such as child safety. Check what those directives have produced by the time you read this.

For a PM selling in the US, customer contracts, procurement questionnaires and the NIST AI RMF set most of the bar today. Keep a short, dated register of the state laws your customers raise, and let counsel own the interpretation.

Other jurisdictions

Two more matter if you sell in Asia. South Korea’s Framework Act on AI took effect on 22 January 2026, with duties for “high-impact” AI (risk management, explanation, user protection, human oversight, records), notice and labelling for generative AI, and a domestic representative for foreign operators above certain thresholds. China’s Measures for Labeling AI-Generated Synthetic Content took effect on 1 September 2025 and require both visible labels and metadata labels. The common thread with EU Article 50 is labelling: design one content-marking capability that can satisfy several regimes.

Data protection: GDPR and the DPIA

AI regulation sits on top of data protection law. Under GDPR Article 35, a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) is required where processing is likely to result in a high risk to people, and always for systematic and extensive evaluation of people (including profiling) with legal or similarly significant effects, large-scale processing of special-category or criminal data, and large-scale systematic monitoring of public areas. A DPIA must describe the processing and its purposes, assess necessity and proportionality, assess the risks to people and set out the measures to address them.

If your company trains or fine-tunes models on personal data, your privacy team will also work from the European Data Protection Board’s Opinion 28/2024 (December 2024) on GDPR and AI models.

In most B2B products the customer is the data controller and you’re the processor. Three commitments then sit at the center of every enterprise deal: whether you train on customer data, where data is processed and stored, and which sub-processors (including model providers) touch it. State them once, consistently, in the data processing agreement and the system card.

The evidence a PM owns

The evidence behind the frameworks is a small set of documents and processes, most of which the PM drafts or coordinates.

AI impact assessments. Before you build or ship, an impact assessment asks what the system is for, who it affects, what could go wrong for them and what you’ll do about it. Microsoft publishes an AI Impact Assessment Template and Guide, ISO/IEC 42001 expects an AI system impact assessment, the EU AI Act requires a FRIA from certain deployers and GDPR requires a DPIA for high-risk processing of personal data. Write one assessment per AI use case that answers the shared questions once and cross-references the DPIA and any FRIA, and let each specialist add their part.

Model cards and system cards. Margaret Mitchell and her co-authors proposed model cards in “Model Cards for Model Reporting” (2019): short documents that accompany a trained model and report its intended uses, the contexts it shouldn’t be used in, how it was evaluated and how it performs across groups, including intersections of groups. Model labs now also publish system cards covering the whole system; Anthropic’s Transparency Hub, for example, summarizes each model’s safety evaluations, prompt-injection robustness tests and external red-teaming, with links to the full cards.

Most B2B PMs don’t train models, so the model card belongs to your model provider. What you own is the system card for your product: which models you use and for what, what data flows where, which tools and permissions the system has, what it can do on its own and what needs approval, how it performs on your evals, what its known limits are, how it’s monitored and what users see. A security reviewer wants it first, and your questionnaire answers and instructions for use are built from it.

Red-teaming. NIST AI 600-1 describes AI red-teaming as “a structured testing exercise used to probe an AI system” for flaws such as inaccurate, harmful or discriminatory outputs. It distinguishes four kinds: general-public red-teaming (ordinary users with lived experience, better in large groups), expert red-teaming (domain specialists such as security researchers), combinations of the two, and human-AI red-teaming, where AI systems lead the attack, which costs less at scale. NIST adds that results need analysis before they drive decisions, and that red teams need people who understand the deployment context.

Two OWASP lists set most of the scope. The Top 10 for LLM Applications 2025 covers risks such as prompt injection, sensitive information disclosure, improper output handling and excessive agency. The Top 10 for Agentic Applications (December 2025) adds agent-specific ones such as goal hijack, tool misuse, identity and privilege abuse, memory and context poisoning, cascading failures and rogue agents. A red-team plan picks the relevant items, writes scenarios for each, defines what counts as a failure and routes every finding into the eval suite.

AI incident response. AI extends your security incident process with new triggers: confident wrong output at scale, an unsafe agent action, a prompt injection that took over an agent, personal data leaking through outputs, a fairness finding, or a model provider change that silently shifts behavior. It adds new controls, such as turning off autonomous actions while keeping suggestions on, or rolling back a prompt or model version. And it adds new clocks: EU Article 73 for high-risk systems once those rules apply, GDPR breach notification when personal data is involved, and whatever your customer contracts promise. NIST AI 600-1 recommends reviewing incident response and disclosure processes specifically for generative AI gaps. The PM owns the severity definitions, the customer communication and turning every incident into an eval case.

Customer AI security questionnaires. Enterprise customers send versions of the same questions. The Cloud Security Alliance’s AI Controls Matrix (AICM), released in July 2025, sets out 243 control objectives across 18 domains, mapped to ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF, NIST AI 600-1 and the EU AI Act, among others; its companion AI-CAIQ questionnaire gives you a standard structure. Build your answer bank on it: one canonical answer per question, with evidence, an owner and a last-reviewed date. One answer then serves the customer who asks in ISO terms and the one who asks in EU AI Act terms.

Responsible AI review inside a product org

Review works when it’s proportional, early and attached to the product’s lifecycle. This book recommends the following pattern, which fits NIST’s Govern function and the ISO/IEC 42001 management-system approach:

  • Triage at the idea stage. Every AI use case gets a quick risk tier. Most land in “light review,” and the team moves on.
  • Gates where decisions happen. Review at PRD sign-off, before beta, before general availability and on any material change: a new model, a new tool permission, a new data source, a new market or a new autonomy level.
  • Clear roles. The PM owns the impact assessment. Applied AI owns evals. Security owns the threat model. Legal and privacy advise. One named approver can block, and that person isn’t the PM.
  • Evidence with the product. Records live in the product’s Product 360, where the next reviewer, auditor or customer can find them.
For product leaders

Make role, tier and transparency duties mandatory fields in every AI PRD across the portfolio, and name one approver per product who can block a launch. The portfolio view of roles and tiers is what counsel and security need to plan their own work.

How to apply it

This method brings one AI product, or one major AI feature, to a defensible state. Run it with your applied AI lead, a security partner and counsel. Plan on four to six weeks for the first pass; after that it’s maintenance.

  1. Inventory the AI use cases. List every place the product uses a model; a summarizer, a classifier and an agent that acts are three use cases with three risk profiles. Note each one’s model and provider, the data it sees and what it can change.
  2. Triage each use case. Record your role, screen against the prohibited list and the Annex III areas, decide whether transparency duties apply and assign an internal risk tier. Where a use case sits near an Annex III area, write down the reasoning and the Article 6(3) conditions you think apply, and let counsel decide.
  3. Write the impact assessment. For anything above the lowest tier, describe intended purpose and prohibited uses, affected people, harms by principle and the twelve NIST generative AI risks, and the mitigations. Ask privacy whether a DPIA is needed, and cross-reference it.
  4. Turn harms into requirements. Each significant harm becomes a product requirement with an acceptance test: an eval slice, an approval gate, a disclosure, a log field, a permission scope. Put them in the AI PRD (Chapter 31) and the backlog, not only in the assessment.
  5. Red-team before beta. Scope the plan from the impact assessment and the OWASP lists, use at least one expert and one AI-driven approach, and convert every confirmed finding into an eval case and a backlog item with a severity.
  6. Write the system card and instructions for use. Draft them from engineering sources and eval results. Have engineering confirm every technical claim. Publish a customer-facing version.
  7. Prepare the incident plan. Define AI-specific severities, the kill switches available for each use case, who decides, who tells customers and what the regulatory and contractual clocks are. Run one tabletop exercise before general availability.
  8. Load the answer bank. Map your system card, assessment and controls to the AI-CAIQ structure. Each answer gets an owner and a review date.
  9. Pass the review gate. Present the evidence to the named approver. Record the decision, the residual risks accepted and who accepted them in the decision log (Chapter 29).
  10. Monitor and re-review. Watch eval results on production samples, user overrides, escalations and incidents. Any material change sends the use case back to step 3. Recheck the regulatory dates and state laws quarterly.
Key ideaThe order matters

Triage before you build, and red-team before beta. An impact assessment written after launch documents decisions nobody can change anymore.

The AI lens

Building AI-powered products

Some questions only arise when the product itself is AI.

  • Agents change the threat model. When an agent reads logs, tickets or web pages, any text it reads can carry instructions. That’s the OWASP agent goal hijack risk, and it means every connector is an attack surface. Scope each tool to the least privilege it needs, treat retrieved content as data rather than instructions and put an approval gate in front of actions that are hard to reverse. Chapter 31’s autonomy dial is a safety control as well as a UX choice.
  • Connectors and MCP servers are suppliers. A third-party MCP server your agent calls is part of your supply chain. List each one in the system card with its maintainer and what it can access.
  • Model changes are material changes. A provider’s model update can shift behavior without any change to your code. Treat it like a release: rerun regression evals, update the system card and re-review if the risk profile moved.
  • Disclosure is a design problem. Article 50 requires people to know they’re interacting with AI unless it’s obvious. In an enterprise tool, the more useful test is whether a responder can tell which parts of a screen the agent wrote and what evidence it used, which is also the core of good trust UX.
  • Your provider’s documents are your inputs. GPAI model providers have had EU obligations since August 2025, and the GPAI Code of Practice includes a Model Documentation Form. Ask your provider for it and for its system cards.

Doing the work with AI

A harness with connectors to your PRDs, eval reports, tickets and policies can draft most of this chapter’s artifacts: an impact assessment from a PRD, a system card from design docs, red-team scenarios from the OWASP lists and questionnaire answers from the answer bank. It also makes a good first-pass reviewer: ask it to find claims in a system card that the eval report doesn’t support.

Three rules keep this safe.

  • The model drafts; a named human signs. Every questionnaire answer, assessment and system card has an owner who checks it against evidence. An invented answer misrepresents your product to a customer, whoever typed it.
  • Never let the model make the legal call. A model can lay out the Article 6(3) conditions and argue both sides. Whether your use case is high-risk is for counsel.
  • Use approved tools for confidential material. Questionnaires, contracts and incident details belong only in the harness your company has approved for that data, with connectors scoped to the task.

Regulatory facts need special care, because the EU dates moved in 2026. A model that says high-risk obligations start in August 2026 is repeating the pre-Omnibus timeline. Give the harness current sources, ask it to cite them and check the citations yourself.

The AI lensThe one-line rule

Use AI to draft the evidence and to attack your own product. Don’t use it to decide what’s compliant or to answer a customer without a human checking the answer.

The Beacon case: getting Beacon Agents ready for an EU bank

Beacon caseWhere Beacon starts

Northlight is preparing to launch Beacon Agents (supervised remediation) at Northlight Live. Meanwhile, Sam’s counterpart at a European bank, one of Beacon’s larger customers, sends a 140-question AI security questionnaire ahead of renewal and asks for Beacon’s EU AI Act documentation. Two months earlier, the wrong-rollback incident (Chapter 31) showed that the team’s controls lagged its ambitions. Beacon has no system card, no impact assessment and no AI incident process beyond the one improvised that week.

Triage. You triage each Beacon capability separately, because their risks differ.

Capability What it does Main risks Internal tier
Correlate Groups alerts into incidents Missed or merged incidents Low
Diagnose Proposes a root cause with cited evidence Confabulation; over-reliance by responders Medium, transparency duties
Act Runs approved runbook steps Wrong action in production; prompt injection; privilege abuse High (internal)
Learn Feeds resolved incidents into retrieval and evals Poisoned or wrong lessons; personal data in logs Medium

Northlight is Beacon’s provider; customers such as the bank and Priya’s company are deployers; the model lab is the GPAI model provider. Beacon doesn’t evaluate people, so the employment and essential-services areas of Annex III don’t apply. The open question is Annex III’s critical infrastructure area, which covers safety components in the management and operation of critical digital infrastructure, and some Beacon customers run exactly that. You write down the reasoning, including which Article 6(3) condition might apply to Diagnose (it prepares a human decision rather than replacing it) and why Act is harder to argue, and send it to counsel. Rather than wait, the team makes Act “high-risk ready”: logs deployers can keep for six months, human oversight controls, instructions for use and accuracy metrics from the eval suite.

One more role question: Leo’s MSP wants to offer Beacon under its own brand to its sixty mid-market customers. Under Article 25(1), putting its name on a high-risk system would make Leo’s firm the provider. That matters only if a use becomes high-risk, but you add it to the partner terms.

Impact assessment and requirements. The assessment for Act names the affected people (the customer’s responders, and the customer’s own users when a production service changes) and the harms, using the NIST list: confabulation, information security, human-AI configuration, and value chain and component integration. Each significant harm becomes a requirement. Rollbacks and restarts need human approval with the evidence shown next to the action, as Chapter 31 decided. Every agent action writes an audit record with the evidence cited, the approver and the model version. Connectors get read-only scopes by default, with write scopes granted per action class. Beacon logs can contain usernames and IP addresses, so privacy confirms Northlight is the processor, Beacon doesn’t train on customer data by default, and the team builds a DPIA support pack for customers who need one.

Red team. Before the Northlight Live beta, the applied AI engineers and an external security firm run a two-week red team: instructions hidden in log lines and tickets (goal hijack), a malicious MCP server (agentic supply chain), a runbook step used to widen access (privilege abuse), one rollback triggering others (cascading failures) and a confident wrong root cause on a multi-region incident. A model generates hundreds of variants of the injection attacks. Two findings are severe: an instruction in a change-ticket description could steer the agent’s proposed action, and the agent could chain two allowlisted steps into an action no customer had allowlisted. Both get fixes and eval cases, and the beta waits a week.

System card and questionnaire. You draft the Beacon Agents system card with the harness from the design docs, eval report and connector inventory; the engineering leads correct four claims, one of them about log retention. The card covers models, connector scopes, the action allowlist and approval gate, autonomy levels, the diagnosis acceptance rate, known limits (multi-region incidents remain weakest), log retention and the disclosure UX. The 140-question questionnaire then takes three days instead of three weeks: the harness drafts answers from the answer bank, which the team has mapped to the AI-CAIQ structure, and each answer’s owner signs it. Eleven questions have no answer in the bank. You write those by hand, and they become new entries.

The incident replay and the review. You replay the wrong-rollback incident through the new plan as a tabletop. Agreeing on severity took 40 minutes during the real incident; with the new definitions it takes five. The kill switch (turning off proposed and autonomous actions while keeping diagnosis on) is now one control per customer instead of an engineering change. At the pre-GA review, the named approver, Northlight’s head of security, signs off Beacon Agents with two residual risks recorded in the decision log: multi-region diagnosis quality, and the open critical infrastructure question awaiting counsel.

What it changed. The bank renews. Sam, the security reviewer persona from Chapter 01, gets what that persona asked for from the start: least-privilege access, audit logs and AI risk documentation. Most of the responsible AI work turned out to be the system card, evals and approval gate the team needed to sell Beacon Agents anyway.

Anti-patterns

Anti-patternPrinciples on a poster

The company publishes six responsible AI principles, and no PRD, eval or launch gate refers to them. Give each principle a product question, an owner and an evidence artifact, and check them at review gates.

Anti-patternCompliance theater

The team produces long assessments after the product ships, written so the answer comes out “low risk.” The documents protect nobody, because they changed no decisions. Assess before you build, and let the assessment add requirements to the backlog.

Anti-patternWaiting for the deadline

“High-risk doesn’t apply until December 2027, so we’ll deal with it then.” Logging, oversight controls and accuracy evidence take quarters to build, and customers are asking for them now. Build them in while the architecture is still moving.

Anti-patternThe PM as lawyer

A PM decides on their own, or with a model’s help, that a use case isn’t high-risk. Write down the reasoning and the facts, and let counsel decide. Then build for the answer you’d least like to hear if the cost is reasonable.

Anti-patternThe one-size review board

Every AI feature goes to the same monthly board, so teams wait weeks for a summarizer and learn to describe agents as “automation” to avoid review. Triage first, run light review for low tiers and save the board’s time for the use cases that can hurt someone.

Anti-patternQuestionnaire answers from memory

Each sales engineer answers AI questionnaires a little differently, and one promises a retention period the product doesn’t support. Keep one answer bank with owners and evidence, and never send an answer, human-drafted or AI-drafted, that its owner hasn’t approved.

Anti-patternThe system card nobody updates

The card was accurate at launch. Since then the model changed twice and a new connector shipped. Tie system card updates to the change triggers in the impact assessment, and log every change.

Checklist

Key takeaways

  • Responsible AI principles help only when each becomes a product question, a requirement with a test and an artifact someone owns.
  • Know three facts for every AI use case: your role, its risk tier and its transparency duties. Classify each use case separately, and let counsel make the legal call.
  • The EU AI Act’s high-risk duties now start in December 2027 and August 2028, but transparency duties already apply, and the logging, oversight and accuracy evidence they require has to be built in, not retrofitted.
  • NIST AI RMF gives you the operating model (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage), its Generative AI Profile gives you a harm list, and ISO/IEC 42001 is becoming the certification customers ask about.
  • For agents, security is the center of responsible AI: least privilege, approval gates, red-teaming against injected instructions and a kill switch you’ve tested.
  • One impact assessment, one system card and one answer bank, kept current and owned, answer most of what regulators and customers will ask.
  • AI can draft all of this evidence and attack your product for you. A named human checks the facts, makes the decisions and signs what goes to customers.
