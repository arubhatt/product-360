<p class="lead">Many companies say they want more innovation and then fund little of it. The money drifts to the core, the bold bets lose their sponsor at the first budget cycle, and the portfolio ends up as a list of features with ambitious names. This chapter gives you the frameworks to decide what kind of innovation your product needs, where it should happen and how much it should get, and a one-page plan, the innovation placemat, that turns those decisions into something an executive will sign and defend.</p>

## Why this matters

Innovation in large companies rarely fails for lack of ideas. What teams lack is a clear link between an idea and a goal the company already cares about, a named executive who will protect the bet when it competes with the core for money, goals that measure learning rather than revenue the bet cannot yet produce, and an honest account of what could go wrong.

The pull toward the core is strong and well documented. George Day, writing in Harvard Business Review in 2007, reported that minor innovations made up about 85% to 90% of the average company's development portfolio, and that the share of major innovations had fallen from 20.4% in 1990 to 11.5% in 2004. Read those numbers as history. The mechanism has not changed: every quarter the core has an escalation, a renewal at risk or a competitor feature to match, and the long bet has a slide. The core wins unless someone has decided in advance how much it is allowed to win.

That decision is the job of an innovation strategy. It answers four questions:

1. **Which corporate goals does innovation serve?** If you cannot name the goal, you cannot defend the spend.
2. **Where will we innovate?** Which customers and markets, which types of innovation, and how far from the core.
3. **How much will we invest in each kind of bet?** A target mix, agreed before the budget fight, so the fight is about the gap and not the principle.
4. **How will we govern, fund and kill bets?** Who sponsors each one, what evidence earns the next tranche of money, and what triggers a stop.

In B2B and platform businesses, the PM who owns a product line is usually best placed to see where the next growth will come from, and is also the person asked to defend the roadmap against it. AI sharpens the question in 2026, because it has made some kinds of innovation much cheaper and others much riskier.

This chapter covers the strategy layer. Chapters 21 to 23 cover the competencies, the toolkit and how to evaluate and scale what works. The innovation placemat built here links them: every later stage reports back against it.

## Core concepts and frameworks

### The innovation placemat

An **innovation placemat** is a one-page innovation plan for a single bet or a small portfolio of related bets. The name comes from the format: everything fits on one landscape page, the size of a placemat, so a sponsor can read it in five minutes and the team can keep it on the table for every review. University courses in innovation strategy use the term for a one-page plan that aligns goals, names an executive champion, sets measurable goals and lays out a risk plan. This book uses its own nine-box format, designed for B2B and platform products.

| Box | What it says | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Strategic intent | The corporate goal this serves, quoted from the company strategy, and a one-sentence innovation thesis | Why should the company care? |
| 2. Where we will play | The cell on the ambition matrix and the innovation types used | How far from the core, and what kind of innovation? |
| 3. Customer and job | Who has the problem, what the problem is, and what evidence already exists | For whom, and how do we know? |
| 4. Bets and hypotheses | The two to four assumptions that must be true, ranked by risk | What has to be true? |
| 5. Goals | One or two outcome goals, leading indicators, a guardrail and a learning goal for this stage | How will we know it is working? |
| 6. Resources and horizon | People, money, time box and the portfolio band it draws from | What does it cost, and from whose budget? |
| 7. Executive champion | Named person, what they own, decision rights and cadence | Who protects it? |
| 8. Risks and mitigations | The top five risks from the risk register, each with an owner and a kill or pivot trigger | What could go wrong, and what will we do? |
| 9. Milestones and stage gates | The evidence needed to earn the next tranche of funding | What earns more money, and when do we stop? |

The placemat is a contract. The team commits to the goals, the evidence milestones and an honest risk section; the sponsor commits to the resources, the air cover and a decision at each gate. If either side cannot sign it, the bet is not ready.

It is short, so it gets read, and specific, so it can be wrong. Revise it at each stage gate and keep the old versions, so the portfolio forum can compare what each bet promised with what it learned.

:::key One bet, one page
If a bet cannot be described on one page with a named sponsor, a corporate goal and a kill trigger, it is still an idea. Ideas are welcome; the placemat is the step that turns one into a funded commitment.
:::

### Ten types of innovation

Most of what makes an innovation hard to copy sits outside the feature list. Larry Keeley and his colleagues at the strategy firm Doblin made this case in *Ten Types of Innovation* (Keeley, Walters, Pikkel and Quinn, 2013), which grouped more than 2,000 successful innovations into ten types across three categories:

| Category | Type | B2B and platform example of a move |
|---|---|---|
| **Configuration** | Profit model | Charge per outcome instead of per seat |
| | Network | Build a partner or connector ecosystem |
| | Structure | Reorganize talent or assets, such as a shared AI platform team |
| | Process | Change how the work is done, such as AI-assisted support triage |
| **Offering** | Product performance | Faster, more accurate, more capable product |
| | Product system | Integrations, extensibility, complementary products |
| **Experience** | Service | Onboarding, managed services, post-incident reviews |
| | Channel | Partners, marketplaces, product-led trials |
| | Brand | What the company stands for, such as trust in AI |
| | Customer engagement | The moments that make customers care, such as a clear answer in under five minutes at 3 a.m. |

The authors' central argument is that most companies over-invest in product performance, the most visible type and the easiest for competitors to copy, and that innovations combining several types are harder to copy. Treat the second claim as the authors' finding from their own database.

Run the ten types as a checklist: for each bet, ask what a move in each type would look like. In enterprise software the strongest moves often sit outside the offering category, in profit model (outcome-based pricing), network (a partner or connector ecosystem) or product system (being the place other tools plug in). An AI model vendor finds these hardest to copy, because they depend on contracts, relationships and installed base rather than model quality.

### Sustaining and disruptive innovation

Clayton Christensen's theory of disruptive innovation began with an HBR article he wrote with Joseph Bower in 1995 and became famous through *The Innovator's Dilemma* (1997). Its popularity means the word "disruptive" is now used for almost any new product, which is not what the theory says.

The Christensen Institute defines two kinds of innovation:

- **Sustaining innovation** makes good products better for an incumbent's most demanding customers. It can be incremental or a breakthrough, and it usually comes at a higher price and margin.
- **Disruptive innovation** starts with simple, cheap offerings at the bottom of a market, or in a market that did not exist, and moves upmarket until it displaces incumbents.

Disruption has two footholds. A **low-end** foothold serves the least profitable customers, the ones incumbents are happy to lose, with a cheaper, good-enough model. A **new-market** foothold serves people who were not customers at all, because the old products were too expensive or too complicated.

The Institute is blunt about the most common misuse: disruptive innovations are not breakthrough technologies that make good products better. A product that is better and more expensive than the incumbent is a sustaining innovation, however exciting it is. Christensen, Michael Raynor and Rory McDonald restated the theory in HBR in 2015 for exactly this reason, using Uber as an example of a company that did not fit the definition, and stressing that disruption is a process that unfolds over time rather than a single event.

The theory has critics. Jill Lepore argued in 2014 that its case selection was weak and that it was being stretched to explain everything. Andrew King and Baljir Baatartogtokh, writing in MIT Sloan Management Review in 2015, asked experts to assess well-known examples against the theory's core elements and found that few cases fit all of them. Use the theory as a lens, not a forecast.

The lens is still valuable because it asks a question incumbents avoid: **which customers are we overserving, and who could serve them more cheaply?** For many specialist B2B tools in 2026, a cloud console or model vendor that bundles a good-enough agent at no extra cost is a low-end entrant for the smallest customers, and a new-market foothold for teams that never bought the category at all. Neither threat looks dangerous measured against your best customers, which is why the theory says incumbents miss it.

### Core, adjacent and transformational: the ambition matrix

Bansi Nagji and Geoff Tuff, then at Monitor Group, published "Managing Your Innovation Portfolio" in HBR in May 2012. Their **innovation ambition matrix** classifies every bet on two axes:

- **Where to play** (horizontal): serve existing customers and markets, expand into adjacent ones, or create new ones.
- **How to win** (vertical): use existing products and assets, add incremental ones, or develop new ones.

The matrix gives three bands:

- **Core**: optimizing existing products for existing customers.
- **Adjacent**: expanding from the existing business into business that is new to the company.
- **Transformational**: developing breakthroughs and new offers for markets that do not yet exist.

The authors reported that companies that outperformed their peers allocated roughly 70% of innovation resources to core initiatives, 20% to adjacent and 10% to transformational, and that the returns ran in roughly the inverse ratio, with transformational bets producing the largest share of long-term return. They also cautioned that the right mix varies by industry, competitive position and stage of development.

Read 70-20-10 as one consulting firm's finding about one set of companies. Its value is as a reference point. Plot every bet into one of the nine cells, sum the spend by band and compare it with a target the sponsor has agreed; the gap is the conversation. An executive can act on "we are at 85% core because of the migration, and our agreed target is 65%." Nobody can act on "we need more innovation."

### Three horizons

The **three horizons** model comes from Mehrdad Baghai, Stephen Coley and David White's *The Alchemy of Growth* (1999), written by McKinsey consultants and popularized through McKinsey's own publications. It sorts a company's businesses by maturity:

- **Horizon 1**: extend and defend the core businesses that produce most of today's profit.
- **Horizon 2**: build emerging businesses that are gaining traction and need investment to scale.
- **Horizon 3**: create options on future businesses through research, pilots, partnerships and small stakes.

The model's lasting value is that each horizon needs different management and metrics: profit and efficiency for Horizon 1, revenue growth and adoption for Horizon 2, learning milestones and option value for Horizon 3. A Horizon 3 bet judged on Horizon 1 metrics will almost always look like a failure.

The model needs three caveats.

- **Horizons describe maturity, not calendar time.** Reading them as "this year, two years, five years" breaks in fast markets, where a Horizon 3 option can mature in a year.
- **The horizons now run at the same time.** Steve Blank argued in HBR in 2019 that the model no longer applies as originally drawn. Its assumption that Horizon 3 ideas have years to mature no longer holds when startups and new technologies can turn them into threats to the core quickly, so companies need a way to run work in all three horizons at once.
- **Horizons and the ambition matrix measure different things.** The matrix measures distance from the core; horizons measure maturity and management mode. Most transformational bets start in Horizon 3, but not all. Use the matrix to set the mix and the horizons to decide how to manage each bet.

### Aligning innovation strategy to corporate strategy

An innovation strategy that is not tied to the corporate strategy gets funded in good years and cut in the first bad one. Write the tie down. This book uses a one-sentence **innovation thesis**:

> Because the company is pursuing **[corporate goal]**, we will innovate in **[ambition band and types]** for **[customers]**, measured by **[outcome]**, funded at **[target mix]**.

Quote the corporate goal from the company's own strategy document. If you cannot find a goal to quote, there are two honest options: find a sponsor who will argue for the bet outside the stated strategy, or stop.

Alignment also has an organizational side. Vijay Govindarajan and Chris Trimble, in HBR in 2010, observed that special teams dedicated to innovation inevitably run into conflict with the rest of the organization, which they call the **performance engine**. Their remedy is a deliberate partnership between the two, with the work divided explicitly, rather than a standalone lab. Charles O'Reilly and Michael Tushman's **ambidextrous organization** (HBR, 2004) makes a related argument: separate exploratory units, integrated at the senior team, so the new business gets protection and the core shares its assets.

For a PM, the lesson is to design the boundary: which assets the innovation team borrows from the core (customers, data, sales capacity, platform services), what the core gets back, and who settles disputes. Settling disputes is the executive champion's job.

### Choosing where to innovate

Five inputs decide where to innovate:

1. **The ambition matrix gap**: which band is underfunded against the agreed mix.
2. **The ten types scan**: which types you have ignored, especially profit model, network and product system.
3. **The disruption lens**: which customers you overserve, and who could serve them more cheaply.
4. **Customer evidence**: jobs, pains and switching stories from discovery (Chapter 01).
5. **Your right to win**: assets you have and a competitor or model vendor cannot easily copy.

George Day's 2007 HBR article offers a screen built on three questions: **Is it real?** (Is there a real market, and can a real product be built?) **Can we win?** (Can the product be competitive, and can this company win with it?) **Is it worth doing?** (Will it be profitable at acceptable risk, and does it fit the strategy?) Run each candidate bet through the three questions before it gets a placemat.

In 2026 the right-to-win question deserves the most weight, because foundation models make generic capability cheap for everyone. Favor bets where proprietary data, installed base, partner distribution, workflow position or ecosystem position give you an advantage that does not come with the model. Chapter 16 covers defensibility in depth.

:::onramp If portfolio language is new to you
Start with one exercise: list every item on your product's roadmap, put each into core, adjacent or transformational, and estimate the share of engineering time in each band. Most teams are surprised by how much of their roadmap is core. That number is the start of every conversation in this chapter.
:::

### Executive sponsorship

Every innovation bet needs an **executive champion**: a senior leader who owns the bet's success at the executive table, secures resources, removes blockers and decides at each gate. Without one, the bet loses its first budget fight with the core.

The most consistent evidence on sponsorship comes from change management practice. Prosci, a change management firm, reports that active and visible sponsorship has ranked as the top contributor to change success in every one of its benchmark studies since 1998, and that projects with extremely effective sponsors met or exceeded their objectives nearly three times as often as projects with very ineffective ones. Prosci also reports that about half of participants felt their sponsors did not adequately understand the role. These are vendor benchmark surveys, not academic studies. Prosci summarizes the role as its ABCs: **active and visible participation**, **building a coalition** of other leaders, and **communicating directly** with the people affected. John Kotter's 1995 HBR article on why transformations fail makes the coalition point too: one executive is rarely enough, and the sponsor's job includes recruiting peers.

Three practical rules for choosing a champion:

- **Pick the executive whose goals the bet serves.** The best champion is the leader whose own metric moves if the bet wins, which is often not your line manager. A pricing innovation might belong with the chief financial officer or chief revenue officer.
- **Avoid sponsors who only lend their name.** The organization reads a sponsor's absence as permission to defund.
- **Agree the kill criteria while everyone is optimistic.** Ending a bet is the hardest conversation. Have it at the start, in writing, so the end is a decision rather than a fight.

The **executive champion brief** in this chapter's artifacts puts the ask on one page. Chapter 36 covers stakeholder management more broadly.

### SMART goals and their limits

Many innovation plans express goals in SMART form. George Doran introduced the idea in *Management Review* in November 1981, with the letters standing for Specific, Measurable, Assignable, Realistic and Time-related. Later versions usually substitute Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound. Doran himself noted that not every objective can be quantified.

SMART forces precision: a named outcome, a number, a date and an owner. It has two limits for innovation.

**Early-stage bets cannot set honest revenue targets.** A transformational bet does not know its market size, price or adoption curve, so a "realistic" revenue target is a guess or a negotiation. Rita McGrath and Ian MacMillan's **discovery-driven planning** (HBR, 1995) argues that new ventures need a different planning tool: plan to learn, list the assumptions that the plan depends on, and convert them into knowledge at checkpoints before committing more money. Chapter 21 covers the experiments that do the converting.

**Goals change behavior, and not always for the better.** Lisa Ordóñez, Maurice Schweitzer, Adam Galinsky and Max Bazerman, in a 2009 Harvard Business School working paper titled "Goals Gone Wild," described systematic side effects of goal setting: a narrow focus that neglects everything outside the goal, more unethical behavior, distorted appetite for risk, a corroded culture and reduced intrinsic motivation. They suggest treating goals like a prescription medication, with careful dosing and close supervision. The research on SMART itself is mixed and depends on context, so avoid strong claims either way.

The fix in this book is to pair every outcome goal with three companions:

| Element | Purpose | Example shape |
|---|---|---|
| **Outcome goal** (SMART) | The result the bet exists to produce | By [date], [segment] will [outcome] from [baseline] to [target], measured by [source] |
| **Leading indicator** | An early signal that moves before the outcome | Weekly active users of the new capability |
| **Guardrail** | A metric that must not get worse, to stop gaming | Customer-reported incidents caused by the feature stay at zero |
| **Learning goal** | The assumption this stage must confirm or refute | Customers will pay per outcome at a price that meets the margin target |

Early-stage bets lead with the learning goal. Later-stage bets lead with the outcome goal. The guardrail stays at every stage, because it is the cheapest protection against the side effects that Ordóñez and her colleagues describe.

### Innovation risk and mitigation

Innovation is risky by design. The aim of risk management is to take the risks you intend, at a size you can afford, and to learn early when one is turning bad.

Group innovation risks into seven categories:

| Category | The question | Typical B2B and AI example |
|---|---|---|
| **Desirability** | Do customers want it? | Users like the demo but will not change their workflow |
| **Viability** | Do the economics work? | Inference cost erodes gross margin |
| **Feasibility** | Can we build it to the needed quality? | Model accuracy is below the trust threshold |
| **Adoption and change** | Will users, partners and channels adopt it? | Partners resist a pricing model that cuts their margin |
| **Organizational** | Will the company sustain it? | The sponsor leaves; the core resents cannibalization |
| **Regulatory and trust** | Will regulators and security reviewers allow it? | Security blocks production access; AI regulation demands documentation |
| **Ecosystem and dependency** | What do we depend on that we do not control? | A model vendor changes price or terms |

The first three are the familiar lenses used throughout this book. In enterprise products, the last four are often what kills the bet.

The common mitigations are staged funding tied to evidence (each tranche earned by a milestone, not a date), reversible pilots with design partners under contract, guardrail metrics, pre-agreed kill triggers, and parallel bets when one assumption is critical and uncertain. For AI products, add evals, human approval gates and an AI risk framework. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework, released in January 2023 with a Generative AI Profile added in July 2024, organizes AI risk work into four functions: **Govern, Map, Measure and Manage**. Use those words for the AI rows of your risk register so your security and compliance reviewers see their own vocabulary. Chapter 33 covers AI regulation, and Chapter 31 covers evals.

The **innovation risk register** in this chapter's artifacts states each risk as "if [cause], then [effect] on [goal]," scores likelihood and impact, names a leading signal to watch, an owner and a kill or pivot trigger. The top rows of the register should match the riskiest assumptions on the placemat. If they do not, one of the two documents is wrong.

:::leader For product leaders
Review innovation as a portfolio. A quarterly forum that sees every placemat on one ambition matrix, with spend by band against the target mix, catches the drift to the core that individual reviews never see. Make the forum the place where bets are killed as well as funded, and celebrate a clean kill backed by evidence as much as a launch.
:::

## How to apply it

This six-step method builds an innovation strategy for your product line and turns its first bet into a signed placemat. Plan on four to six weeks the first time; after that, revise placemats quarterly and the strategy yearly.

**Step 1: Anchor on the corporate strategy (week 1).**

1. Have your AI harness extract every stated corporate goal, with exact wording and source, from the strategy documents, the portfolio plan and your last business review.
2. Pick the one to three goals your product line can move and write the innovation thesis for each. Note each goal's executive owner; it matters when you choose a champion.

**Step 2: Map the current portfolio (week 1).**

3. List every funded initiative on your roadmap. Classify each into one of the nine cells of the ambition matrix and one horizon, and tag the innovation types it uses.
4. Estimate the share of effort in each band from approximate engineering allocation; precision is not the point.
5. Agree a target mix with your manager and the likely champion, starting from your context rather than 70-20-10. A product in a fast-moving AI category may need more outside the core than a mature product with a large installed base.

**Step 3: Generate and screen candidate bets (weeks 2 and 3).**

6. Run the ten types scan, with at least one candidate move per type.
7. Apply the disruption lens: name the customers you overserve and the cheaper alternatives that could serve them, and decide whether to defend, serve them with a lighter offer or let them go.
8. Bring in customer evidence from discovery (Chapter 01) and market sizing (Chapter 13).
9. Screen each candidate with Day's three questions: Is it real? Can we win? Is it worth doing? Keep two to four bets that pass and that close the gap between your current mix and the target.

**Step 4: Secure an executive champion (week 3).**

10. For each surviving bet, identify the executive whose goals it serves and meet them with the executive champion brief.
11. Ask for specific behaviors (attending the quarterly gate, removing one named blocker, recruiting a peer) and agree the kill criteria in the same meeting.

**Step 5: Write the placemat (weeks 3 and 4).**

12. Draft the nine boxes. Write the bets and hypotheses box before the goals box, because the goals should test the hypotheses.
13. Set goals in the four-part form: outcome goal, leading indicator, guardrail and learning goal. For early-stage bets, make the learning goal the headline.
14. Build the innovation risk register. Brainstorm across all seven categories, score each risk and carry the top five onto the placemat with owners and triggers.
15. Define the stage gates: the evidence each gate requires and the funding it releases.

**Step 6: Review, sign and run (weeks 5 and 6, then quarterly).**

16. Ask a skeptical peer, and your AI harness, to red-team the placemat. Fix what they find.
17. Review it with the champion and get a signature, or an explicit list of changes.
18. Add the bet to the portfolio forum's ambition matrix. At each quarterly gate, update the placemat, the risk register and the matrix, and make the decision: continue, pivot, scale or stop.

:::note What good looks like after one quarter
Every bet has a signed placemat. The portfolio's spend by band is visible against the target mix. At least one bet has passed or failed a gate on evidence, and the risk register has changed because something was learned.
:::

## The AI lens

### Building AI-powered products

AI is both an innovation driver and a source of new risk. Five developments shape innovation strategy in 2026.

**AI is everywhere, and the value is concentrated.** Stanford HAI's AI Index 2026 reports organizational AI adoption at 88%, up from 78% for 2024 in the previous year's report. Yet BCG's September 2025 study found that only about 5% of companies were achieving value from AI at scale, while about 60% reported little or none. Most competitors can buy the same models, so "adding AI" is no longer an innovation in itself. Ask which business model, workflow or ecosystem change AI makes possible, using the ten types. Charging per outcome because an agent can now do the work, connecting to the customer's other systems, or running as a service what used to need people are each harder to copy than a model-powered feature.

**The economics keep moving.** The AI Index 2025 reported that the inference cost of a system performing at the level of GPT-3.5 fell more than 280-fold between November 2022 and October 2024. Ideas that failed the viability test in 2023 deserve a second screen. But agentic workloads, which run many model calls per task, can still compress margins, so put inference cost in the viability rows of your risk register and track it as a leading signal, not a quarterly surprise.

**Agents are moving from demo to portfolio line, with real reliability risk.** BCG estimated that agents accounted for about 17% of AI value in 2025 and projected about 29% by 2028. The AI Index 2026 reports the best agent at 66.3% on the OSWorld computer-use benchmark against a human baseline of 72.35%, while agents still fail roughly one structured task in three. Gartner predicted in June 2025 that over 40% of agentic AI projects would be canceled by the end of 2027, an analyst prediction rather than an observed rate. The lesson: classify agentic bets as adjacent or transformational, fund them in stages, and write reliability, cost and trust risks into the register from day one.

**Interoperability lowers the cost of ecosystem innovation.** Anthropic open-sourced the Model Context Protocol (MCP) in November 2024 as an open standard for connecting AI systems with data sources and tools, and in December 2025 MCP became a founding project of the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. For platform PMs, publishing connectors or letting partners publish them is now a cheap network and product-system move. It also means a competitor's agent can plug into the same systems, so the advantage moves to data, workflow position and trust.

**The disruption lens applies to you.** Cloud consoles and model vendors bundling good-enough agents are classic low-end and new-market entrants for specialist B2B tools. Ask which of your customers would settle for a fraction of your product at no extra cost, and decide on purpose what you will do about them.

:::ai Name what AI makes possible
"We will use AI" belongs in no innovation thesis. Name the corporate goal, the customer, the innovation type and the outcome. Then say what AI makes possible that was not possible before, and why a competitor with the same models cannot do it as well.
:::

### Doing the work with AI

A harness with connectors to your documents, CRM, support system and analytics makes an innovation strategy much faster to build, and makes it just as easy to produce a confident, well-formatted strategy nobody has checked. Use it for:

- **Drafting from sources.** Extract the corporate goals from the strategy memo and business reviews, then draft the thesis and first placemat, with a source link on every claim.
- **Classifying the portfolio.** Propose a matrix cell, horizon and innovation types for each roadmap item, with a one-line reason. Your disagreements with it start the strategy conversation.
- **Widening the option set.** Ask for two moves per type in a ten types scan, then discard most of them.
- **Building the risk register from history.** Mine past post-mortems, cancelled projects, win and loss notes and security findings for risks that apply to the new bet.
- **Red-teaming.** Argue against the placemat as a skeptical CFO, a security reviewer and a competitor.

The rule for this chapter: **the AI drafts, the PM and the sponsor decide.** Check every number on the placemat against its source report, and remove anything you cannot trace. A model's estimate of market size or adoption does not belong on a document an executive signs. Placemats often draw on customer data, pricing and unreleased plans, so use only tools your company has approved for confidential information.

## The Beacon case: an innovation placemat for FY2027

You are the Principal PM for Beacon, and Northlight's portfolio review for FY2027 is six weeks away. Beacon is growing fast (about $48M ARR, up 38%, NRR at 118%), but gross margin fell from 81% to 72% after the agentic features launched, because of inference cost. Beacon Agents launches at Northlight Live this year, pricing is moving from per-environment fees toward a per-resolved-incident price, and the Beacon Classic migration is consuming engineering time. The VP of Product asks you to bring an innovation placemat for Beacon's next bet. The corporate goals in the strategy memo, for this chapter's illustration, are to grow the Operations portfolio and to recover gross margin.
:::

The percentages, targets and spend mix in this section are illustrative numbers created for this chapter. They are not part of the Beacon case facts.

**Mapping the portfolio.** The harness classifies every roadmap initiative on the ambition matrix from the roadmap and engineering allocation. After you correct a handful of classifications: roughly 80% of effort is core (diagnosis quality, correlation tuning, the Classic migration, the Observe integration), about 15% adjacent (Beacon Agents, the pricing change) and about 5% transformational. The Classic migration explains most of the core weight. You propose a target of 65/25/10 over the fiscal year, freeing effort as the migration finishes.

**The ten types scan.** Most of what the scan turns up is not a feature:

- *Profit model*: per-resolved-incident pricing, which aligns Beacon's revenue with the North Star metric (incidents resolved with Beacon's help per week).
- *Network*: a multi-tenant edition for MSPs like Leo, and a marketplace where partners publish runbooks as MCP servers.
- *Product system*: deeper use of Northlight Observe data, set against the larger market of staying tool-agnostic.
- *Service*: post-incident reviews offered as a service.
- *Customer engagement*: Dana's need for a clear "what broke and what do I do" in under five minutes.
- *Brand*: trust that the agent will not make things worse, which Priya named as a core need.

**The disruption lens.** Startups building ops agents, and cloud platforms bundling AI into consoles customers already pay for, are low-end entrants for the mid-market that Leo's MSP serves. Beacon risks overserving that segment with enterprise-grade features priced for Marcus, which pushes the MSP edition up the list.

**Choosing the bet.** You screen the candidates with Day's three questions. The partner runbook marketplace is real but fails "can we win" this year, because the connector ecosystem is too thin. The outcome-guaranteed autonomous service fails "is it worth doing" until diagnosis reliability improves; the hallucinated root cause that led a customer to roll back the wrong service is still fresh. The bet that passes all three is a combination: **Beacon Agents with per-resolved-incident pricing**, an adjacent bet that combines product performance, profit model and brand. **Choosing the champion.** The obvious sponsor is your manager, the VP of Product. The better champion is the CFO: margin recovery and the pricing change are on the CFO's agenda, and the bet will cannibalize per-environment fees that someone senior must defend. Your brief asks for approval of a pricing pilot with 10 design-partner customers, funding for two more applied AI engineers, and explicit air cover for the revenue cannibalization. You ask the CFO to attend each quarterly gate and to recruit the chief revenue officer, whose sales team will carry the new price.

**Setting goals with their limits.** Your first draft of the outcome goal is an autonomous resolution rate target. A peer points out the risk Ordóñez and her colleagues describe: a team measured on autonomous resolutions has an incentive to allowlist riskier actions. You rewrite it in four parts:

- *Outcome goal*: by the end of Q2 FY2027, 40 enterprise customers will run Beacon Agents with at least one allowlisted autonomous action, measured in product telemetry.
- *Leading indicator*: weekly responders approving agent-proposed actions.
- *Guardrails*: zero Sev-1 incidents caused by an autonomous action, and no fall in diagnosis acceptance rate.

**The risk register.** The harness proposes 23 risks from past post-mortems, security reviews and win and loss records; you cut them to 9. Each has an owner and a trigger. The first carries a kill trigger: if an autonomous action causes a Sev-1 incident, autonomous mode pauses for all customers until the eval suite is extended and the champion approves a restart.

**Horizons, not calendar years.** Correlate and diagnose is Horizon 1, Beacon Agents with outcome pricing is Horizon 2, and the outcome-guaranteed service and partner marketplace are Horizon 3 options. Beacon Agents launches at Northlight Live this year, so its Horizon 2 metrics (adoption and revenue growth) apply now.

## Anti-patterns

:::warn Innovation theater
A lab, a hackathon and an innovation week, none tied to a corporate goal or a budget. Ideas get applause and die in planning, because nobody with money committed to them. Every bet that leaves the hackathon needs a placemat, a champion and a gate, or it is parked as an idea.
:::

:::warn Copying 70-20-10
The portfolio target is set to 70-20-10 because a slide said so. Nagji and Tuff reported that mix for a particular set of companies and said the right ratio depends on industry and position. Agree a target that fits your product's market and maturity, and explain why.
:::

:::warn The name-only sponsor
An executive agrees to "support" the bet, skips every review and is unavailable when it collides with a core priority. Ask for specific behaviors in writing, and if the sponsor will not commit, find another one.
:::

:::warn Horizon 1 metrics on a Horizon 3 bet
A transformational bet gets a revenue target in its first year, misses it, and is killed. Its job at that stage was to learn whether the riskiest assumption was true. Lead early bets with learning goals and evidence milestones, and move to revenue goals when the bet earns its way to scale.
:::

:::warn Calling everything disruptive
The team describes a better, more expensive product as disruptive, and misses a cheap, good-enough competitor taking its smallest customers. In Christensen's sense, disruption starts at the low end or with non-customers. Use the word precisely, and run the overserved-customer question every year.
:::

:::warn The risk register nobody reads
A long risk list is written for the approval meeting and never opened again, with no owners, signals or triggers. Keep it short, review it monthly, and make its top five match box 8 of the placemat.
:::

- [ ] Every roadmap initiative is classified on the ambition matrix, with a horizon and the innovation types it uses.
- [ ] Current spend by band is compared with a target mix agreed with the champion, and the target is justified for this market rather than copied.
- [ ] Candidate bets came from a ten types scan and a disruption scan, not only from feature requests.
- [ ] Each bet passed Day's three questions: Is it real? Can we win? Is it worth doing?
- [ ] Each bet has a named executive champion whose own goals it serves, three agreed behaviors and kill criteria agreed before the bet started.
- [ ] Goals include an outcome goal, a leading indicator, at least one guardrail and a learning goal, and early-stage bets lead with the learning goal.
- [ ] The risk register covers all seven categories, and its top five risks match box 8 of the placemat.
- [ ] Every risk has an owner, a leading signal and an observable kill or pivot trigger.
- [ ] AI risks are mapped to Govern, Map, Measure and Manage, and inference cost is tracked as a viability signal.
- [ ] Every number on the placemat has been checked against its source report, and AI-drafted sections are disclosed.
- [ ] The placemat is signed by the team and the champion and reviewed at each quarterly gate.

## Key takeaways

- An innovation strategy answers four questions: which corporate goals it serves, where to innovate, how much to invest in each kind of bet, and how bets are funded and stopped.
- The innovation placemat puts one bet on one page: intent, where to play, customer, hypotheses, goals, resources, champion, risks and stage gates. It is a contract between the team and the sponsor.
- Use the ten types to look beyond product performance, the disruption lens to find customers you overserve, and the ambition matrix to compare spend by band with a target you chose for your market, not one copied from 70-20-10.
- Choose the executive whose goals the bet serves as champion, ask for specific behaviors, and agree kill criteria at the start.
- Pair every SMART outcome goal with a leading indicator, a guardrail and a learning goal, and lead early bets with the learning goal.
- In 2026, AI is a capability every competitor has. Innovate in the business model, workflow and ecosystem that AI enables, fund agentic bets in stages, and let the AI draft while the PM and sponsor decide.
