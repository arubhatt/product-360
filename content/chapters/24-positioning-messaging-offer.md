<p class="lead">A buyer meets your product with a question already in their head: what is this, and why should I pick it over what I do today? Positioning is your answer to that question, decided on evidence. Messaging is how you say it to each person in the deal. The offer is what they can actually buy, on what terms, through a quote-to-cash process that works on launch day. This chapter covers all three, plus the discipline that keeps them honest: every claim you make has evidence behind it before anyone says it out loud.</p>

## Why this matters

A good B2B product can still lose because the buyer couldn't work out what it was for, compared it with the wrong alternative, or couldn't buy it without a month of back and forth with legal and finance. Those are positioning, messaging and offer failures, and the PM causes or prevents many of them.

Product marketing writes the words, runs the launch and equips the field (Chapters 25 to 27). But the words rest on product facts only you hold: what the product does, which customers get the most from it, what it does better than the alternatives, how you know and what it can't do yet. The offer rests on the pricing and packaging decisions in Chapters 12 and 15. If those facts are vague, the messaging will be vague too, or confident and wrong.

AI products raise the stakes. Every competitor says "AI-powered," so the label differentiates nothing, and buyers are unsure what any of these products really do. Regulators treat a claim about AI as a product claim like any other: the US Federal Trade Commission's 2024 sweep, Operation AI Comply, and the SEC's "AI washing" cases both turned overstated AI claims into enforcement actions. Positioning that promises more than the product does is now a legal exposure as well as a credibility problem.

This chapter gives you five artifacts, plus a readiness check: a **positioning canvas**, a **positioning statement**, a **messaging framework** with a message house, an **offer definition** and a **claims substantiation log**. They belong to the Go-to-market dimension of your Product 360, fed by the personas in Chapter 01 and feeding the launch plan in Chapter 25.

## Positioning, messaging and the offer

### Three different things

Teams blur these three, and each fails in its own way. Keep them apart.

| | Positioning | Messaging | The offer |
|---|---|---|---|
| **What it is** | The decision about how the product is different and better than the alternatives, for a specific set of customers | The external words that express the positioning, adapted to each persona and channel | The defined, sellable thing: SKUs, editions, bundles, units of charge, terms and eligibility |
| **Audience** | Internal: the product, marketing, sales and leadership teams | External: buyers, users, analysts, partners | Customers, sales, partners, finance and legal |
| **Changes** | Rarely, when the alternatives or your value change | Often, by persona, channel and campaign | At packaging and pricing decisions, with a governed process |
| **Fails when** | It describes features rather than the value only you provide | It promises what the positioning and the evidence don't support | Sales can't quote it, billing can't charge for it or legal hasn't approved the terms |

Pragmatic Institute's framework draws the same line between the first two. Its Positioning box describes the product by its ability to solve market problems and produces internal positioning documents. External messages for each key buyer or persona are developed from those documents. Positioning is a decision you hold steady; messaging is the many ways you express it.

### April Dunford's five components

April Dunford, author of *Obviously Awesome*, defines positioning as how a product is different and better than the alternatives for a particular set of customers. Her method breaks it into five components, worked in order, because each depends on the one before.

1. **Competitive alternatives.** What would customers do if your product didn't exist? Build the list from real deals, not an analyst's market map. In B2B the answer is often a manual process, an internal tool or "do nothing."
2. **Differentiated capabilities.** What do you have that those alternatives lack? Features count, and so do a delivery model, expertise or a data advantage.
3. **Value.** For each capability, ask "so what?" until you reach something the customer cares about in business terms: time, money, risk, revenue. Group the answers into a few value themes, each with its proof.
4. **Best-fit customers.** Who cares most about that value? Describe them precisely enough that sales can spot one quickly and stop chasing the rest.
5. **Market category.** Choose the frame of reference that makes your value obvious to those customers. The category tells the buyer what to compare you with, which sets their assumptions about price, features and competitors before you've said a word.

Teams often start with the category ("we're an AIOps platform") and work backwards. Dunford puts it last because you can't pick the context that makes your strengths obvious until you know what the strengths are and who values them.

Three refinements from Dunford's recent writing matter for an enterprise PM.

- **Decide the scope first.** A multi-product company has to choose whether it's positioning a single product, a suite or platform, the company, or a combination. Teams that skip this argue for weeks because they're positioning different things.
- **Position for the champion.** The target persona is the deal champion: the person who builds the shortlist, brings in the other stakeholders and recommends a choice to the economic buyer. That's often not the person who signs.
- **Before launch, it's a thesis.** Dunford calls pre-launch positioning a positioning thesis. Hold it loosely until the market responds, then revise on evidence from real deals.

Positioning also serves customers, not investors, employees or partners. A narrative built to excite a board rarely helps a buyer choose.

:::key Understand why you win
Dunford argues that understanding why you win matters more than understanding why you lose. When a team says "we have no differentiation," the causes she names include blindness to the value customers already get, weak product knowledge, an inside-out view of competitors, obsessing over lost deals and sloppy segmentation. Study won deals and the competitors that actually reach shortlists. If the product really adds nothing, positioning can't fix it; change the product, pricing, partnerships or services.
:::

### Three styles of market positioning

The category decision has three broad options. Dunford calls them styles of positioning.

- **Head to head.** Claim leadership of an existing category buyers already understand. Fastest when you can win on the category's own terms; hard when an incumbent owns it.
- **Big fish in a small pond.** Lead a subsegment of an existing category, defined by a customer type or use case the leader serves poorly. You borrow the category's familiarity and win where your strengths are decisive.
- **Create a new category.** Define a new frame because no existing one makes your value obvious. Slowest and most expensive, because the market has to learn a new term before anyone can buy.

Most B2B products should start with one of the first two. Dunford observes that startups more often enter an existing category and stretch its boundaries once they have money and momentum, and that category creators often lose to later entrants. Eloqua, which she cites, created a category and dominated it, so it can be done. It's still the expensive option.

### The category design debate

The counterargument comes from *Play Bigger* (2016), by Christopher Lochhead, Dave Peterson, Al Ramadan and Kevin Maney. Their discipline, **category design**, holds that the companies that win define a new problem, name the category that solves it and become its recognized leader. In its current framing, Play Bigger calls category design necessary but not sufficient. It aligns belief inside the company, while a second effort, market creation, carries that belief out to customers, analysts and investors.

Both camps agree that the frame decides how buyers judge you. They disagree on when to build a new one. A test for a PM, drawn from both:

- **Is there a budget line and a shortlist?** If buyers already set money aside and list vendors for something close to what you do, a subsegment of that category usually wins faster.
- **Does every existing frame hide your value?** If each one makes you look like a weaker version of something else, a new frame may be worth its cost.
- **Can you afford to educate?** A new category needs years of investment in analysts, content and events. Will the company fund it after the launch year?
- **Will pragmatic buyers wait?** Geoffrey Moore's work, below, suggests mainstream buyers prefer a known category with a clear leader.

AI products make this debate live: every new capability tempts a team to coin a category.

### Geoffrey Moore: the positioning statement and the beachhead

Geoffrey Moore's *Crossing the Chasm* (first published 1991, third edition 2014) describes the gap between early adopters, who buy technology for its promise, and the early majority, pragmatists who want proven solutions. His advice: focus on one customer group at a time and deliver the **whole product**, everything that group needs to solve one problem, before moving to adjacent groups. That first group is the beachhead, a segment small enough to dominate and the base for the next.

For a platform PM, a beachhead is a segment plus a use case. "Enterprises" isn't one. "SRE teams of 20 to 100 engineers with a heavy incident load, for incident diagnosis" might be.

Moore also popularized the **positioning statement**, a fill-in structure that Chapter 02 borrowed for vision work. In outline:

- **For** [target customers]
- **who** [have this need or problem],
- **[product] is a** [market category]
- **that** [delivers this key benefit].
- **Unlike** [the primary competitive alternative],
- **our product** [has this primary difference].

The statement compresses the Dunford analysis; it can't replace it. Fill it last. The "is a" line is your category decision, and the "Unlike" line must name the real alternative, often the status quo. Written first, the statement tends to fill the "that" line with an aspiration and the "Unlike" line with a competitor nobody shortlists.

### From positioning to pitch

Dunford's *Sales Pitch* turns the five positioning components into an eight-step B2B pitch that replaces the feature walkthrough with a case for choosing you over the alternatives. She opens the pitch with the company's insight about the market rather than with "the problem," and builds discovery into the conversation. The PM supplies much of the raw material: the insight (from discovery, Chapter 01) and the proof behind each value claim (from pilots, customer data and evals, Chapter 31).

Dunford also separates **value** from **objection handling**. Value is what the product does for the customer's business that alternatives can't; it's why someone chooses you. Objection handling covers support, onboarding, integrations, security reviews and certifications: rarely why anyone buys, but missing them disqualifies you. Her short version: "Sell value to your champion. Handle the objections of everyone else." Champions and economic buyers focus on business impact; IT, security, legal and purchasing raise the concerns that kill deals. They belong in different layers of the message.

### Messaging frameworks and the message house

Messaging is the external, persona-specific expression of positioning. James Shaw's messaging framework for the Product Marketing Alliance lists the usual parts: a core value proposition, the customer's pain points, a benefit for each pain, the product details that deliver it and proof points such as case studies. It's tested with product management, applied consistently across web, sales and social, and written per persona.

Many teams organize messaging as a **message house**, a common practitioner tool with no single recognized originator:

- **The roof** is one umbrella message: the positioning, expressed in customer language.
- **Three pillars** carry the supporting messages, usually one per value theme from the positioning.
- **Proof points** sit under each pillar: data, customer evidence, third-party validation.
- **The foundation** holds the facts that make the claims credible: company scale, security and compliance facts and, for AI products, the trust and governance facts.

The mapping from positioning is direct. Value themes become pillars, proof becomes proof points, the objection-handling layer becomes most of the foundation, and the roof expresses the "that" line of the positioning statement. Every proof point needs a row in the claims substantiation log before it appears anywhere external.

Build one house per offer and one variant per persona. The champion's variant leads with value; the security reviewer's leads with the foundation.

### The offer: what you actually sell

The product is what you build; the offer is what customers buy. They're defined and fail separately, and a great product can launch with an offer sales can't quote.

An offer definition specifies:

- **SKUs.** The orderable items in the price book, each with an owner and a unit.
- **Editions and tiers.** Which capabilities are in which edition, and the upgrade path between them.
- **Bundles and add-ons.** What's sold only together, what's sold separately and what's sold both ways. Chapter 12 covers the economics of bundling.
- **Unit of charge.** Per seat, per environment, per usage unit or per outcome, with the exact definition of the unit. For outcome pricing, the definition is a contract term.
- **Terms.** Contract length, minimum commitments, overage, true-up, renewal and price protection.
- **Eligibility.** Who may buy which edition, in which regions, through which channels (direct, reseller, managed service provider) and with what prerequisites.
- **Entitlements and limits.** What the customer gets: quotas, support level, data retention and service levels.

Chapter 15 makes the packaging and pricing decisions; the offer definition turns them into something sales, finance, legal and partners can act on. It also has to match the positioning: the edition built for your best-fit customers should be easy to find and buy.

### Offer readiness: quote to cash

An offer is ready when every stage of the quote-to-cash process can handle it on launch day. Zuora lists the stages as offer configuration, pricing, quoting, contract management, orders, payment and collections, billing, revenue recognition and renewal. Each is a place a new offer can break: a new unit of charge has to exist in the quoting system, be metered accurately enough to bill and make sense to the team that recognizes revenue.

Revenue recognition needs particular attention, because packaging decisions change it. IFRS 15, the international standard issued in May 2014 and effective from 2018 (its US counterpart is ASC 606), recognizes revenue in five steps: identify the contract, identify the separate promises in it (performance obligations), determine the transaction price including variable amounts, allocate that price to each promise and recognize revenue as each promise is satisfied. Two consequences for a PM: a bundle or edition can contain several separate promises, each recognized on its own timing, and usage or outcome pricing creates variable consideration that finance has to estimate. You don't do the accounting, but finance must review the offer before it's announced, not after the first deal is signed.

Legal readiness covers the contract terms, the data processing terms and, for AI products, a set of AI-specific terms: whether customer data is used for training, acceptable use, what the product does autonomously and what needs human approval, and how errors are handled and credited. Chapter 33 covers the regulatory side.

### Claims substantiation

Every message carries claims, and objective claims need evidence. The FTC's 1984 Policy Statement Regarding Advertising Substantiation sets out the principle: an advertiser must have a reasonable basis for objective claims before making them. How much evidence counts as reasonable depends on the type of claim, the product, the consequences of a false claim, the benefit of a truthful one, the cost of developing the evidence and what experts in the field would consider reasonable. A claim that states its own level of support ("tests show," "proven in pilots") needs at least that level of support.

"Cuts diagnosis time in half" is an express, objective, quantified claim. Before it appears in a deck, a web page or a keynote, someone must hold dated evidence for it from a population that matches the customers it addresses. The claims substantiation log holds that evidence. Rules differ by jurisdiction and counsel decides what's sufficient; the log gives them something to review.

### Who owns what: PM and product marketing

Pragmatic Institute's framework lays out 37 activities in seven categories, from market problems and win/loss analysis through positioning, launch and sales enablement, without assigning them to roles. Its guidance for product managers describes the usual split: product management often owns market understanding and strategic direction, while product marketing frequently leads positioning, launch coordination and sales enablement. A workable split:

| Work | PM | Product marketing | Others |
|---|---|---|---|
| Evidence: win/loss, discovery, usage, evals | Owns | Contributes | Sales, customer success |
| Positioning decision | Co-owns | Co-owns, usually facilitates | Sales leader, product leadership |
| Messaging and message house | Reviews for accuracy | Owns | Sales, customer success |
| Offer definition | Owns (from Chapter 15) | Contributes | Finance, sales operations, legal |
| Offer readiness | Accountable for completeness | Contributes | Sales operations, billing, revenue, legal |
| Claims log | Drafts and maintains evidence | Owns claim wording | Legal approves |

The PM owns the product truth: what it does, for whom, proven how and what isn't ready. Product marketing owns the words and the field. Decide positioning together, with sales in the room.

## How to apply it

Here's a method for positioning a new product or a major new offer, from evidence to a sellable, defensible launch. It takes four to six weeks alongside other work, and it should finish before launch planning (Chapter 25) commits to dates and assets.

**Step 1: Prepare the evidence (week 1).**

1. Decide the scope with product marketing (product, suite, company or a combination) and write it down.
2. Name the champion persona from your buying group (Chapter 01). The positioning is for them.
3. Gather the evidence: win/loss notes from the last two to four quarters, discovery snapshots, pilot results, usage data, eval results for AI features and the competitors that actually reached shortlists. Have your AI harness pull and summarize it through its connectors, and check the sources it cites.
4. Pick five to ten customers who get the most from the product. Their patterns are your best-fit signal.

**Step 2: Run the positioning session (week 2).**

5. Bring together product, product marketing, a sales leader who closes deals in the target segment, customer success and a product leader who can decide. Keep it under ten people.
6. Work the five components in order on the positioning canvas. Don't let the group skip to the category.
7. Choose the positioning style and record why the other two were rejected.
8. If the product hasn't launched, mark the canvas as a thesis and set a review after the first 20 or so qualified deals.

**Step 3: Compress and express it (week 3).**

9. Write the positioning statement and test it on three sales reps and two friendly customers. Can they repeat it in their own words? Does the "Unlike" line name what they'd really do instead?
10. With product marketing, build the message house and a variant per buying-group role.
11. Log every proof point and every quantified, comparative or availability claim in the claims substantiation log. Anything without evidence stays out of external material.

**Step 4: Define the offer and make it ready (weeks 3 to 5).**

12. Write the offer definition from the Chapter 15 packaging and pricing decisions.
13. Walk it through each quote-to-cash stage with sales operations, billing, revenue accounting and legal. Give each gap an owner and a date.
14. Label every capability as generally available, preview or roadmap, the same way in messaging and in the offer.

**Step 5: Approve and hand over (weeks 5 to 6).**

15. Get the claims log signed off by legal and product marketing, and the readiness check by finance and sales operations.
16. Hand the package to launch planning (Chapter 25) and sales enablement (Chapter 27), and record the positioning decision in your decision log.
17. At the review date, test the positioning against real deal outcomes. Revise it on evidence, not on the latest competitor announcement.

## The AI lens

### Positioning AI-powered products

April Dunford's 2026 writing on AI starts from a steady premise: the main positioning principles don't change with a big technology shift. What changes is the speed. AI moves the product, the competitors and the market at once, and buyers are confused. Her guidance:

- **Offer a point of view and a path.** Buyers want a credible view of where the market is going and a path from today's needs to it. Future vision helps only when it's tied to present value; otherwise it stalls deals.
- **Position against today's shortlist.** Build the product for tomorrow's competitors, but position against the ones prospects consider now, using customer evidence (advisory boards, win/loss) rather than investor or peer narratives.
- **Don't reposition on every model release.** If a new capability strengthens a value theme you already own, update the proof and keep the positioning. Rebuild only when you can deliver value you couldn't before, or new competitors enter. Value changes more slowly than features.

Four implications follow for AI products.

**Don't make "AI" the category.** "AI-powered" and "agentic" describe how the product works, and every competitor uses them. Position on the job and the outcome. Calling a chatbot or a script an "agent" is the same overstatement as calling a rules engine "AI," and it carries the same substantiation risk.

**Make the autonomy level explicit.** State what the product suggests, what it does with human approval and what it does on its own, under which customer controls. That precision is both a trust message and a substantiation requirement.

**Availability is a claim.** In April 2025, the National Advertising Division of BBB National Programs found that Apple's unqualified "Available Now" heading for Apple Intelligence implied features were available at launch when they weren't, and that its footnotes weren't clear or close enough to the claim. Apple said it would follow the recommendations. Label every capability as available, preview or roadmap at the point of the claim.

**Accuracy and autonomy numbers are claims.** The enforcement record is consistent.

| Case | What happened | Lesson for a PM |
|---|---|---|
| FTC, Operation AI Comply (September 2024) | Five actions announced together. DoNotPay had marketed "the world's first robot lawyer"; the FTC said it couldn't match a human lawyer's expertise. DoNotPay paid $193,000, and the January 2025 final order bars claims that it substitutes for a professional service without evidence. | "Replaces a professional" claims are a known enforcement trigger. |
| FTC, Workado (April 2025) | Workado claimed its AI content detector was 98 percent accurate; independent testing found 53 percent on general-purpose content, and the FTC alleged the model had been trained or fine-tuned only on academic content. The order requires competent and reliable evidence at the time of any effectiveness claim, kept on file. | Evidence must exist when you make the claim and cover the population the claim addresses. |
| SEC, Delphia and Global Predictions (March 2024) | Two investment advisers settled charges of misleading statements about their use of AI, paying $225,000 and $175,000. | For a public company, AI claims in filings, earnings calls and press releases carry securities-law exposure. |
| SEC, Presto Automation (January 2025) | The SEC's order describes misleading statements about an AI drive-thru ordering product, including that it eliminated human order-taking when most orders on that version needed human help, and nondisclosure that a third party owned and operated the speech recognition in deployed units at one point. Presto settled without admitting or denying the findings, and without a penalty. | "Without human intervention" rates are claims: define the denominator, and disclose material reliance on third-party technology. |

The regulatory climate shifts, but not on this point. In December 2025 the FTC set aside its 2024 order against Rytr, a writing tool that could generate fake reviews, citing the administration's AI Action Plan, while saying it would still pursue those who use AI to break the law. Workado shows that unsupported claims about what an AI product does remain in scope. Your own claims about your own product are still the exposure.

:::note This is not legal advice
This chapter teaches the principles a PM needs to work well with counsel. Rules differ by country and by industry, and your legal team decides what evidence is sufficient for a claim. The claims log gives them something concrete to review, early.
:::

**Tie marketing numbers to evals.** The cleanest way to keep AI claims honest is to source every accuracy or autonomy number from the same eval sets and production metrics the product team trusts (Chapter 31). If the number in the keynote can't be traced to an eval run with a date and a scope, it doesn't go in the keynote.

### Doing the work with AI

An AI harness with connectors to your CRM, call recordings, win/loss notes, support tickets and product analytics helps most with the evidence.

- **Mining alternatives and wins.** Ask which alternatives appeared in deals over the last few quarters, how often, and why customers chose you. That replaces the team's memory of "who we usually see" with a list grounded in deals. Insist on citations to specific opportunities and calls.
- **Testing the canvas.** Ask it to argue against your positioning: which capabilities the alternatives share, which value themes lack proof, which best-fit traits don't match won deals.
- **Drafting persona variants.** Models draft variants per persona and channel quickly, and drift from the house just as quickly. Check each against the house and the claims log.
- **Auditing claims.** Give it the launch material and the claims log, and ask it to list every objective claim and match it to a log row. Unmatched claims are your work list.
- **Checking offer readiness.** Ask it to walk the offer through each quote-to-cash stage and list each team's open questions.

The rule from Chapter 01 still applies: AI can summarize what customers said, but it can't tell you what they'd say or decide your positioning. Synthetic buyers can find confusing phrasing; they can't show that a message works. Keep customer data in an approved tool.

:::ai The one-line rule
Position on the job and the outcome, label every capability as available, preview or roadmap, and never let a number reach external material unless it traces to an eval or production metric with a date and a scope.
:::

## The Beacon case: positioning Beacon Agents for Northlight Live

:::example Where Beacon starts
Beacon Agents (supervised remediation) launches at Northlight Live. The first draft of launch messaging from the agency reads "Autonomous operations: the AI SRE that never sleeps." Your discovery work in Chapter 01 found that on-call burnout is the most-cited cost, ahead of MTTR, that Priya cares about keeping her team, and that Marcus cares about MTTR because his CFO does. The pricing work in Chapter 12 set a platform fee plus a per-resolved-incident price for Agents. You and the product marketing manager have five weeks to replace the draft with positioning you can defend.
:::

**Scope and champion.** Northlight would like a suite story across Observe, Desk and Beacon. You and product marketing position Beacon Agents as a product instead, because staying tool-agnostic opens the larger market. The Observe data advantage becomes a differentiated capability for Observe customers, not the frame. The positioning is written for Priya, the SRE lead, who builds the shortlist and recommends to Marcus.

**Competitive alternatives.** The harness pulls every Beacon opportunity from the last four quarters and lists the alternatives named in each. For most prospects the main alternative is humans plus runbooks plus chat. Then come AIOps features bundled into large observability platforms (the two Chapter 01 switchers left for one, on price and procurement simplicity), incident-management tools adding AI and ops-agent startups. The "AI SRE" startups the agency wanted to position against rarely reach shortlists.

**Differentiated capabilities and value.** The team runs the "so what?" exercise on each capability.

| Capability | Alternatives lack | Value theme |
|---|---|---|
| Root cause proposed with cited evidence from logs, metrics, traces, deploys and change tickets | Humans assemble this by hand; bundled features summarize alerts | Faster diagnosis, shorter incidents |
| Supervised remediation: approval gate, autonomy only for allowlisted low-risk actions | Runbook scripts run without context; some agents act without approval | Safe to let it act |
| Per-action audit logs, scoped credentials, kill switch | Rare among ops-agent startups | Safe to let it act |
| Post-incident reviews feed retrieval and evals | Runbooks go stale | Fewer repeat pages over time |
| Connectors, including MCP servers, across tools | Bundled features work best inside one vendor's platform | Works with the stack you have |

Three value themes survive: fewer and shorter 3 a.m. wake-ups for the on-call team; faster diagnosis and lower MTTR, which is Marcus's CFO story; and trust and control that security can approve.

**Best-fit customers.** From the Chapter 01 hypothesis and the won deals: SRE and platform teams of 20 to 100 engineers at enterprises with a heavy incident load and an existing correlation footprint.

**The category argument.** The agency wants to create a category, "autonomous operations." You run the test. Buyers already have budget and shortlists for incident management. Inside incident response, diagnosis with cited evidence stands out, so the existing frame doesn't hide Beacon's value. Northlight hasn't committed to a multi-year category campaign. And "autonomous" overstates a product whose autonomy is limited to allowlisted actions. You choose big fish in a small pond: **AI incident response for SRE teams**, a subsegment of incident management.

**The positioning statement.** *For SRE and platform teams who lose sleep and hours to incident diagnosis, Beacon is an AI incident response agent that finds the likely root cause with cited evidence and runs approved fixes. Unlike runbooks and chat war rooms, Beacon gives the on-call engineer a diagnosis in minutes, and only acts with human approval or within limits the customer sets.*

Three sales reps repeat it back accurately, and a design-partner customer recognizes their own process in the "Unlike" line.

**The message house.** The roof, "Give your on-call team their nights back," leads with the burnout insight, because that's what Priya feels. The second pillar carries Marcus's MTTR story. Sam's requirements (audit logs, scoped credentials, a kill switch, documentation for EU AI Act obligations) sit in the foundation: they won't win the deal, but missing any of them loses it. Dana's variant leads with "what broke and what do I do"; Leo's leads with multi-tenant views and per-customer billing.

**The claims log.** You and the product marketing manager log every claim in the draft deck and web page. Several change.

- "The AI SRE that never sleeps" and "replaces your on-call engineer" are cut. Both imply professional substitution, the pattern in the DoNotPay order, and customers don't want it.
- "Cuts diagnosis time in half" was the Chapter 01 solution hypothesis, not a result. It stays out until the design-partner pilots report, and then it states the population (design partners) and the period.
- "Autonomous resolution" becomes "resolves allowlisted low-risk incidents without waiting for a human," with the rate's denominator written down because of the Presto order.
- "Accurate root cause" gets an eval-backed number with a date and scope, or no number. The claim has to hold up if the customer who rolled back the wrong service after a hallucinated root cause reads it.
- The keynote script labels each capability GA or preview, matching the offer definition.

Because Northlight is public, the log gets a column for investor-relations review of any claim that will also appear in an earnings call or filing.

**The offer definition.** The offer follows the packaging in Chapter 15 and the pricing in Chapter 12: Correlate and Diagnose sit in the platform fee in three tiers, and Agents is the outcome layer, a per-resolved-incident charge with an annual commitment and a monthly minimum. Audit logs, scoped credentials and the kill switch ship in every tier that includes Agents. The EU AI Act documentation pack and the multi-tenant console are add-ons. The Chapter 15 resolved-incident definition becomes a contract term, with its exclusions, dispute window and a credit when Beacon's action makes an incident worse. Only customers with the security controls configured may buy Agents, and the MSP offer gives Leo per-tenant metering and invoicing at a wholesale rate.

**Offer readiness.** The quote-to-cash walk finds four gaps. Quoting has no SKU for a resolved-incident unit. Billing can't yet meter resolved incidents from product telemetry, so engineering adds a billable event carrying the dispute fields finance asked for. Revenue accounting treats the per-incident charge as variable consideration and wants pilot data to estimate it. Legal adds AI terms: no training on customer data without consent, the human-approval commitment and the autonomy allowlist as a customer-controlled setting. MSP partner billing and the EU documentation pack won't be ready in time, so Agents launches direct-only, with the MSP offer labeled "coming" and dated.

**The result.** Northlight Live announces "AI incident response for SRE teams," not "autonomous operations," and every number in the keynote traces to a log row. The positioning is still a thesis: you review it after the first 20 qualified Agents deals, starting with whether the burnout roof works for economic buyers as well as champions.

## Anti-patterns

:::warn Category first
The positioning session opens with an argument about which category to be in and never leaves it. The category follows from the alternatives, capabilities, value and best-fit customers. Decide it last.
:::

:::warn Positioning against the competitor you admire
The deck compares the product to the well-funded startup everyone reads about, while deals are lost to "keep doing what we do now." Position against the alternatives in real deals, often the status quo.
:::

:::warn Coining a category you can't fund
A launch announces a new category name with no budget for the years of education it needs. If buyers already have a budget line and a shortlist nearby, win a subsegment first.
:::

:::warn AI as the positioning
"AI-powered" or "agentic" is the headline, and every competitor's headline says the same. Position on the job and the outcome; the AI is how you deliver it.
:::

:::warn The number nobody can trace
A keynote says "reduces MTTR by 60%" because someone saw it in a pilot readout. Nobody knows which customers, which period or how it was measured. A number without an Approved claims-log row doesn't ship.
:::

:::warn Roadmap sold as available
The launch page says "available now" over a list of capabilities, three of which are in preview. Label every capability as available, preview or roadmap at the point of the claim, and match the offer definition.
:::

:::warn An offer sales can't quote
The product launches on time and the first deal stalls because the new unit of charge isn't in the quoting system and finance hasn't decided how to recognize it. Walk the offer through quote to cash before you announce a date.
:::

## Checklist

- [ ] The positioning scope (product, suite, company) and the champion persona are decided and written down.
- [ ] The competitive alternatives come from real deals, including the status quo, with sources.
- [ ] Each differentiated capability maps to a value theme, and each value theme has proof or a plan to get it.
- [ ] Best-fit customers are described precisely enough for a rep to qualify a deal.
- [ ] The category was chosen last, with the positioning style and rejected alternatives recorded.
- [ ] The positioning statement passes the repeat-back test with sales reps and at least one customer.
- [ ] The message house has a roof, three pillars, proof points and a foundation (where objection handling sits), with a variant for each buying-group role.
- [ ] The offer definition specifies SKUs, editions, bundles, unit of charge, terms, eligibility and entitlements.
- [ ] Usage or outcome units are defined as contract terms, with exclusions, disputes and credits.
- [ ] Every quote-to-cash stage is ready or has a dated fix, and finance has reviewed revenue recognition.
- [ ] Every external claim has an Approved row in the claims log, with evidence matching the claim's population.
- [ ] AI accuracy and autonomy numbers trace to a named eval set or production metric, with the denominator stated.
- [ ] Every capability is labeled available, preview or roadmap, consistently across messaging and the offer.
- [ ] A review date is set for the positioning thesis after launch.

## Key takeaways

- Positioning, messaging and the offer are three separate artifacts. Positioning is an internal decision, messaging expresses it per persona, and the offer is what customers can actually buy.
- Work April Dunford's five components in order: alternatives from real deals, differentiated capabilities, value with proof, best-fit customers, and only then the category.
- Most B2B products should win a known category or a subsegment of one before trying to create a category. Coining a new one is slow and expensive, and AI makes the temptation stronger.
- The PM owns the product truth and the evidence; product marketing owns the words and the field. Decide positioning together, with sales in the room.
- An offer is ready only when every quote-to-cash stage can handle it, including billing for new units and revenue recognition for bundles and variable pricing.
- Claims about AI are product claims. Keep a claims substantiation log, tie accuracy and autonomy numbers to evals, define every denominator and label what's available, in preview or on the roadmap.
