<p class="lead">A product reaches most enterprise customers through other people: a seller who explains it, a partner who deploys it, a customer success manager who gets it adopted. This chapter covers what you owe those people, how you turn customer success and retention into product outcomes you own, and how you run the loop that carries what the field hears back into the roadmap without turning the roadmap into a list of promises.</p>

## Why this matters

In a B2B business, the PM's commercial work starts on launch day. After it, a seller you have never met explains your product to a buyer you will never meet, using whatever words they picked up in a 45-minute training. A partner deploys it for customers you don't see. A customer success manager decides which features to push in onboarding. If any of them gets the product wrong, the customer gets it wrong too, and you find out two quarters later as a lost deal or a contraction at renewal.

The buying group makes this harder. Gong's analysis of 1.8 million opportunities found that 77% of deals involve more than one buyer contact, and that strategic enterprise deals average 17. That is the vendor's own data and it is correlational, but it shows why one pitch for one buyer isn't enough: the seller has to arm a champion, an economic buyer, a security reviewer and procurement, each with a different question (Chapter 01 covers the buying group). Salesforce's 2026 State of Sales survey of 4,050 sales professionals found sellers spend on average 40% of their time actually selling. Whatever you hand the field has to be usable in the slivers of time left over.

Feedback runs the other way too. The field hears more customer problems in a week than a product trio can interview in a quarter, mostly as feature requests attached to deals and filtered through someone with a quota. Ignore it and you lose the signal; obey it and you build a roadmap for the loudest account. The craft is to collect it, dedupe it, link it to problems, decide and tell people what you decided.

This chapter covers the enablement kit the PM owes direct sellers and partners, customer success treated as a product outcome (onboarding, health scores, business reviews, retention), and the feedback loop, including win-loss and a customer advisory board. These sit at the boundary between the Market and Customer dimensions of your Product 360. Positioning (Chapter 24) and launch (Chapter 25) produce the message; this chapter makes sure the message survives contact with the field and that what the field learns comes back.

## What the PM owes the field

### Who owns what

Seismic, a vendor of enablement software, defines sales enablement as the strategic use of people, processes and technology to improve sales productivity and increase revenue. The definition is useful mostly as a reminder that content is one of three parts. A beautiful pitch deck with no training, no certification and no way to find it on a call is not enablement.

The Pragmatic Institute's framework for product management places sales alignment, sales tools, content and channel training in its Programs group, and win-loss analysis in its Market group, but it doesn't assign activities to roles (Chapter 24 covers the PM and product marketing split for positioning). This book recommends a simple split for the field. The PM owns the truth: the problem, the personas, the differentiation, the roadmap and the product's limits. Product marketing owns the packaging and the campaign. Enablement owns delivery and certification. Sales and customer success own the conversation.

| Role | Owns | Owes the PM |
|---|---|---|
| **Product manager** | Problem, persona, differentiation, limits, roadmap, pricing logic, the "do not sell into" list | Nothing is shipped to the field that the PM hasn't checked for accuracy |
| **Product marketing** | Positioning, pitch narrative, battlecards, proof points, launch content | Win-loss and competitive patterns |
| **Enablement** | Training, certification, content findability, cadence | Which content is used and which is ignored |
| **Sales and sales engineering** | Discovery, demo, deal strategy | Objections heard, competitors met, deals lost and why |
| **Customer success** | Onboarding, adoption, renewal, expansion | Health signals, churn reasons, feature requests with context |

In smaller companies the PM also covers much of product marketing's column. The split still tells you which work to hand off when headcount arrives, and which work stays with you.

:::key The PM owns the truth
Product marketing can make the story compelling and enablement can make it stick. Only the PM can say whether it's accurate: what the product does today, what it doesn't, who it's wrong for and what's coming. Every enablement asset should pass through that check before it reaches a seller.
:::

### The sales enablement kit

This book recommends a standard kit for every product or major release. The contents are a synthesis, not any one author's model:

| Component | What it answers for the seller | Primary owner |
|---|---|---|
| One-page positioning | Who is this for, what problem, what do they use today, why we win, proof | PMM with PM |
| Pitch narrative | How do I tell the story in ten minutes? | PMM |
| Discovery guide | What do I ask each persona to find and size the problem? | PM with sales |
| Objection handling guide | What do I say when they push back, and when do I walk away? | PM with PMM |
| Competitive battlecards | How do I win a head-to-head on the shortlist? | PMM |
| Demo script and environment | What do I show, in what order, on what data? | PM with sales engineering |
| Pricing and packaging guidance | What's list, what can I discount, who approves, what can't I promise? | PM with pricing |
| ROI or business value tool | How does the champion prove value to the CFO? | PM |
| Roadmap guidance | What can I say about the future, to whom, under what terms? | PM |
| Known limits and "do not sell into" list | Where will this product fail the customer? | PM |
| FAQ and escalation path | Who do I call when the kit doesn't answer it? | PM |

The PM leads eight of the eleven and owns four outright: the ROI tool, roadmap guidance, known limits and the escalation path. Teams that treat enablement as marketing's problem tend to skip those four, and they're the ones that prevent bad deals.

**Discovery questions.** Neil Rackham's SPIN Selling (1988) came out of a research program at Huthwaite that observed about 35,000 sales calls over 12 years. Rackham grouped successful questioning into four types: **Situation** (facts about the current state), **Problem** (difficulties and dissatisfaction), **Implication** (what the problem causes, which builds urgency) and **Need-payoff** (the value of solving it, stated by the buyer in their own words). Sellers are usually fluent in the first two. The PM should write the last two, because Implication and Need-payoff questions encode the product's value hypothesis. If you can't write a good Implication question for a persona, you probably don't know why that persona would pay.

Matthew Dixon and Brent Adamson's *The Challenger Sale* (2011) reported research that sorted sellers into five profiles and found the one they called the Challenger to be the top performer. For a PM, the useful part is the idea of bringing the buyer an insight about their own business. That insight usually comes from product data, a pattern across hundreds of customers that no single buyer can see, and supplying it is part of your job.

**Objections and battlecards.** An objection handling guide gives, for each common objection, what it usually means, a clarifying question, a response with proof, when to escalate and when the objection is valid. That last field matters most. Some objections are correct, and a seller who talks a customer past a real limit creates a churn risk with a signature on it.

Kimberly Mercer, writing for the competitive intelligence vendor Klue, describes battlecards as tools for the late-stage shortlist rather than for first meetings. Her advice: frame every point from the buyer's need, be specific about the competitor, arm reps against the objections a competitor plants, and pitch a persona's problem against the competitor rather than a feature grid. Crayon's 2026 State of Competitive Intelligence survey found that teams sharing competitive intelligence with sales weekly or faster were much more likely to report revenue impact (79%) than teams sharing monthly or slower (41%). It's a vendor survey and the link is correlational, but it supports a weekly compete feed over a quarterly battlecard refresh.

**The demo.** The Great Demo! method makes two points PMs often miss. Discovery comes before the demo, so each demo can be tailored to the problem the customer has just described. And the best demos run in reverse: show the end result first, then how the product gets there. The PM owes the field a stable demo environment with realistic data, a scripted happy path and, for AI products, a deliberate moment that shows how the product behaves when it's wrong. Chapter 26 covers demo craft in depth.

**Pricing guidance and the ROI tool.** Pricing strategy is Chapter 15. What the field needs is narrower: list price, discount bands and who approves each, what can be bundled, and the things that may never be promised (custom roadmap commitments, uncapped usage, unreleased features at a fixed date). The ROI tool should take the buyer's own inputs, state every assumption with its source, and default to conservative values. An ROI tool that shows a 10x return on invented benchmarks gets a champion embarrassed in front of their CFO, and that champion remembers.

### The enablement cadence

Enablement decays. Products change, competitors ship and sellers forget. Tie the depth of enablement to the launch tier from Chapter 25, and run a standing cadence between launches.

| Trigger | Enablement response |
|---|---|
| Tier 1 launch | Live training, certification for sellers and sales engineers, full kit refresh, demo environment updated |
| Tier 2 launch | Recorded session, kit update, battlecard and FAQ changes |
| Tier 3 launch | Release note to the field channel |
| Every week | Compete and win-loss feed: what changed, what we heard |
| Every month | Field update from the PM: shipped, coming, known issues, top objections |
| Every quarter | Roadmap briefing for sales and CS leaders, with what can be shared externally |
| A competitor ships or win-loss shows a new pattern | Battlecard refresh within days, with a named owner |

### Partner and channel enablement

Many B2B products reach the mid-market through partners, and each kind of partner needs something different. A **managed service provider** (MSP) takes ongoing responsibility for running a client's IT, usually against a service level agreement, with a setup fee followed by a steady monthly fee. A **reseller** sells your product and takes a margin. A **services partner** implements and customizes it. A **technology partner** integrates with it. Large vendors organize their partner programs this way; AWS, for example, runs distinct tracks for resellers, services partners, managed service providers and technology partners. Segment your own partner enablement the same way.

| Partner type | What they need from the product | What they need from enablement |
|---|---|---|
| **Reseller** | Clear packaging, partner pricing, deal registration | Sell kit, pricing guidance, a sales certification |
| **Managed service provider** | Multi-tenant views, per-customer billing, bulk deployment, margin that works at scale | Technical certification, operating runbooks, a sandbox tenant |
| **Services partner** | APIs, configuration depth, migration tools | Implementation certification, reference architectures |
| **Technology partner** | Stable APIs or connectors, a listing, joint support process | Integration guides, co-marketing rules |

The MSP row is where product decisions hide. An MSP's economics depend on running many customers with few people, so features like multi-tenant administration and per-customer billing decide whether the partner can make money. If they can't, no amount of training will make them sell. Every partner segment also needs a feedback channel into the same triage process the direct field uses, or partner customers become invisible to the roadmap.

## Customer success as a product outcome

### Desired Outcome

Lincoln Murphy, a long-time writer and adviser on customer success, defines it as the customer achieving their Desired Outcome through their relationship with the vendor. The payoff for the vendor is that successful customers stay longer, buy more and advocate. In his current version, a Desired Outcome is a **Goal** (an objective, the conditions around it and a time frame) plus an **Appropriate Experience**. In an earlier version he called the first part the Required Outcome, and was explicit that it is not the customer's functional use of the product. Nobody's goal is "use the dashboard." The goal is fewer outages, faster audits or more signups.

The Appropriate Experience is the part PMs tend to skip. It's how the customer needs to reach the goal, and it's appropriate to their segment rather than maximal. A 5,000-person enterprise expects a named contact, a joint plan and a security review. A 30-person customer of an MSP expects never to think about you. Onboarding, in-product guidance and support all belong to it.

### The post-sale lifecycle

Gainsight, the customer success software company, describes four post-sale phases: onboarding and time to value; adoption and value realization; growth and expansion; and renewal and advocacy. Customer success teams run these phases. The product decides how hard each one is.

**Onboarding and time to value.** Time to value is how long it takes a new customer to realize the product's value. Ramli John and Wes Bush, in their work on product-led onboarding, call the first moment of value the "First Strike." Their method maps every onboarding step to it, rates each step for necessity (keep now, delay or remove) and for ease, keeps only the friction that builds the customer's ownership, and produces a straight-line path. Gainsight's onboarding model adds the human side for enterprise accounts: welcome and setup, education, a kickoff with a joint plan, early success in the first 30 to 60 days, then a formal review and handoff, with reviews at 30, 60 and 90 days. It also stresses the sales-to-onboarding handoff, where the goals agreed in the deal should travel with the customer. In enterprise products, that handoff is a common place for time to value to leak.

**Health scores.** Gainsight groups health score inputs into six leading-indicator categories: product usage against entitlement, feature adoption, sentiment, engagement (cadence calls, follow-through on executive reviews), relationships (an executive sponsor, advocacy) and customer value (business outcomes). The product owns the telemetry for the first two outright, and should own a customer-level outcome metric for the sixth. Gainsight's own advice is to benchmark and track the trend, and to revisit the scorecard if churn keeps surprising you. This book goes further: validate the score against what actually happened at renewal. A health score that doesn't predict churn is decoration.

**Business reviews.** Murphy's guidance on the quarterly business review (QBR) with a customer, written for Gainsight, is still a good structure. Executives from both sides attend and the CSM owns the agenda. It reviews the last quarter against the goals set last time, shows value delivered, walks a standard scorecard (support cases, health trend, lifecycle stage, usage and unused features), covers the roadmap and obstacles, collects feedback and sets goals for the next 90 days. It is for the top segment only, and it is not the place for tactical tickets. The PM's role is small: a short roadmap segment and two or three questions you want answered. You're there to listen. (Chapter 29 covers internal business reviews, which are a different meeting.)

**Expansion and renewal.** In Gainsight's last two phases the commercial team signs the paper, and the product decides what there is to sign. Expansion needs a path the customer can see from inside the product: a usage limit they're approaching, an adjacent team that could reuse the same setup, a higher tier whose value they've already had a taste of. Renewal is mostly decided before the renewal call, by whether the customer reached the Desired Outcome they bought for. Ask customer success for a renewal risk view built from the health score at least two quarters ahead, and treat every contraction or churn reason as evidence for the outer loop, not only as a sales loss.

### NRR and GRR are product metrics

Net revenue retention (NRR) and gross revenue retention (GRR) are usually reported by finance and owned by customer success. They're also the cleanest summary of whether the product keeps delivering value after the sale. Gainsight states them this way:

- **NRR** = (starting recurring revenue + expansion − contraction − churn) ÷ starting recurring revenue × 100
- **GRR** = (starting recurring revenue − contraction − churn) ÷ starting recurring revenue × 100, which can never exceed 100%

For context, Gainsight, summarizing a KeyBanc Capital Markets and Sapphire Ventures survey, puts private B2B SaaS medians at about 101% NRR and 90% GRR. Treat that as a rough reference point, not a target.

The two numbers tell you different things. GRR is mostly a value and fit signal: did customers keep what they bought? NRR adds an expansion signal: did the product give customers reasons to buy more? A product with high NRR and weak GRR is growing its best accounts while quietly losing others, and the headline number hides it. Decompose both: churn and contraction reasons by cohort and segment, expansion by trigger (more seats, more environments, a new tier, more usage). Chapter 09 covers survival analysis and lifetime value for the deeper method.

## The feedback loop

### Inner loop and outer loop

The cleanest model of a feedback system comes from Bain & Company's Net Promoter System, which grew out of research led by Fred Reichheld. It has two loops. In the **inner loop**, feedback reaches front-line employees directly and quickly, in the customer's own words, and they follow up with the customer. In the **outer loop**, the company deals with changes the front line can't make alone: products, processes and policies. Bain describes the outer loop as often run by a central office that gathers issues, removes duplicates, triages them and routes each to the team with the resources to fix it. That team finds the root cause, designs a solution and ranks it against its other work.

Map that onto a product organization and the roles are clear. Sellers, CSMs, support and partners close the inner loop with their customers. The PM, usually with product operations (Chapter 28), runs the outer loop. The common failure is an inner loop with no outer one: CSMs promise to "pass it to product," product receives a spreadsheet of requests, and nobody hears back.

### Feature requests are solutions

Teresa Torres's opportunity solution tree (Chapter 01) puts a desired outcome at the root, then opportunities (customer needs, pains and desires), then solutions, then assumption tests. In her framing a feature request is a solution, not an opportunity. The move is to ask why it matters to the customer until you reach the need underneath, and then to check whether there's more than one way to address it. If there's only one way, you probably still have a solution in disguise. Torres also notes that requests, support tickets and sales conversations arrive without much context, so they work best as prompts for customer interviews rather than as direct inputs to the tree.

This rule changes the data model. The unit of record is a **problem**, not a request. When a new request arrives, it's either merged into an existing problem record as additional evidence (with the customer, segment, revenue band and the problem in their words attached), or it creates a new problem record that needs discovery. Counting requests tells you how many people proposed a particular solution. Counting the customers attached to a problem tells you how many people have the problem, which is the number you need.

Three disciplines make the outer loop trustworthy:

- **Dedupe into problems.** Forty requests for the same feature are one problem with forty pieces of evidence, plus whatever differently worded requests belong on the same record.
- **Don't let revenue-weighted counts decide alone.** Weighting by account revenue is a useful input and a bad prioritizer: it favors the largest accounts' preferred solutions over the segment you're trying to grow, and rewards whoever logs the most.
- **Close the loop twice.** Tell the requester and the account team when a decision is made (including "not planned," with the reason) and again when something ships. A field that hears back keeps sending good evidence. A field that hears nothing stops logging and starts escalating.

### Win-loss analysis

Win-loss analysis interviews buyers after deals close to find out what actually drove the decision. Clozd, a win-loss vendor, recommends using a neutral interviewer because buyers rarely give sellers the real reason they lost; interviewing wins as well as losses, soon after close; planning about 25 to 30 interviews per segment before patterns stop changing; tagging every decision driver as positive or negative; and sharing findings continuously rather than in an annual report. Clozd's 2025 survey of win-loss programs found companies more than twice as likely to be satisfied with the depth of feedback when it was gathered within a month of close. It's vendor data without a stated sample size, so treat the number as indicative. Win-loss findings feed both the battlecards and the outer loop.

### Customer advisory boards

A customer advisory board (CAB) is a small group of senior customers who advise on strategy. The Farland Group, a firm that runs CABs, describes five characteristics of good ones: a strategic focus aligned to company goals; executive sponsorship (ideally the CEO attends); senior members from a diverse mix of accounts; momentum, meaning advice becomes tracked actions with outcomes reported back; and adaptability, meaning the charter, format and membership are revisited. Its practical guidance: meet in person once or twice a year for one to one and a half days, with optional virtual sessions of 60 to 90 minutes between; recruit senior executives from strategic accounts who can think beyond their own account; avoid prospects; keep sessions pitch-free and confidential; and let sales nominate members but not attend.

A CAB is worth its cost only for questions a feature request can't answer: where the category is going, how much autonomy customers will grant an AI system, how they'd want to buy. A roadmap walkthrough followed by feature voting turns it into an expensive request queue.

## How to apply it

Here's a method for setting up enablement, customer success and the feedback loop for a product or a major release. Steps 1 to 6 happen before and around launch; steps 7 to 12 are the standing machinery you keep running.

**Before launch: arm the field.**

1. **Agree the ownership split.** Fill in the who-owns-what table with product marketing, enablement, and sales and CS leadership. Settle it in writing, so nobody discovers the gap on launch day.
2. **Write the truth first.** Before anyone drafts a slide, write the one-page positioning, the known limits and "do not sell into" list, and the roadmap guidance (what can be said, to whom, under what terms). Everything else in the kit is derived from these three.
3. **Write the discovery guide per persona.** For each persona in the Chapter 01 buying group, write a few Situation and Problem questions and, more carefully, the Implication and Need-payoff questions that encode why they'd pay. Test them on two friendly sellers first.
4. **Build the objection handling guide and battlecards from evidence.** Have your harness cluster objections from recent calls, win-loss interviews and lost-deal notes, then write the responses yourself. Mark the valid objections, and say what the seller should do instead of arguing.
5. **Prepare the demo, the pricing guidance and the ROI tool.** Lock a demo environment with realistic data and a reverse-chronology script. Write discount bands and approvals with your pricing lead. Build the ROI tool on the buyer's inputs with conservative, sourced defaults.
6. **Enable partners by segment.** For each partner type you sell through, decide what they must know, sell, deploy and support, and what certification proves it. Give every partner segment a feedback channel into your triage process.

**After launch: keep customers and listen.**

7. **Define each customer's Desired Outcome at the handoff.** Make the sales-to-CS handoff carry the customer's goal in Murphy's form (objective, conditions, time frame) and the agreed first-value milestone. If the deal closed without one, CS and the PM write it with the customer in the kickoff.
8. **Straighten the onboarding path.** Map every onboarding step to the first moment of value. Remove or delay steps that don't lead there. Track time to first value per cohort and review cohorts at 30, 60 and 90 days.
9. **Co-own the health score.** Give customer success the telemetry they need: usage against entitlement, adoption of the features that predict retention and the customer's outcome metric. Each quarter, check whether last quarter's scores predicted renewals and contractions, and change the score if they didn't.
10. **Decompose retention every quarter.** Ask for GRR and NRR by segment and cohort, churn and contraction reasons, and expansion by trigger. Bring the decomposition to your product review (Chapter 29) as a product finding, with the problems it points to.
11. **Run the outer loop weekly.** Hold a 30-minute triage with product ops: dedupe requests into problem records, link them to opportunities, send the unclear ones to the trio's interview list, decide what you can and close the loop. Publish a monthly field update.
12. **Run win-loss continuously and convene the advisory board.** Interview wins and losses within a month of close. Hold the CAB once or twice a year on strategic questions, report back on what you did with its advice, and refresh membership yearly.

:::onramp If you're new to working with the field
Start with two habits. Join two sales calls and one customer success call a week, and listen more than you speak. And answer every field question within one business day, even if the answer is "I don't know yet, I'll find out by Friday." Credibility with the field is built from small, reliable answers long before it's built from big launches.
:::

## The AI lens

### Enabling and supporting AI-powered products

AI products change what the field has to explain, what customer success has to measure and what "resolved" means.

- **Sell the failure modes, not just the capability.** A seller who implies an AI feature is always right creates a renewal problem the first time it's wrong. The kit needs a plain-language section on how the product behaves when it's wrong, what controls the customer has and what evidence it shows for its outputs. Zendesk's CX Trends 2026 research reports that 95% of consumers expect an explanation for decisions made by AI, while 37% of organizations provide one. Those are consumer figures from a vendor, with no stated sample size, but enterprise security reviewers ask the same question in writing.
- **The objection guide grows a trust section.** Expect objections about data use, model training, autonomy, audit and regulation. Each needs a response written with the PM and, for regulatory questions, reviewed by legal (Chapter 33).
- **Define "resolved" before you celebrate it.** Some AI support and operations products are now priced and measured on outcomes, Beacon Agents among them. Intercom prices its Fin AI agent at $0.99 per resolution and counts two kinds: confirmed (the customer says it helped) and assumed (the customer leaves without asking for more help). A follow-up question or a request for a human doesn't count, and a resolution is deducted if the customer comes back. Intercom also claims an average resolution rate of 76% across its customers. Klarna said in February 2024 that its AI assistant handled two-thirds of its customer service chats in its first month, the work of 700 full-time agents. Both are company claims. The lesson for a PM is about definitions: an "assumed" resolution may be a customer who gave up. Whatever your product's outcome metric is, write its definition into the kit, the health score and the contract, and make sure CS can explain it to a customer.
- **Health scores need AI-specific signals.** Usage alone hides whether customers trust the output. For an AI feature, add a signal for acceptance (how often users act on the AI's output) and for override or reversal. A customer whose usage is flat but whose acceptance rate is falling is losing trust, and that shows up at renewal.

### Doing the work with AI

The field already works with AI. Salesforce's 2026 State of Sales survey found that 54% of sellers had used AI agents, nearly nine in ten planned to by 2027, and 51% of sales leaders using AI said disconnected systems were slowing them down. Crayon's 2026 survey found 80% of competitive intelligence teams using AI to generate compete content. Both are surveys run by companies that sell into these teams, so read them as direction rather than precise measures. The direction has three consequences for the PM.

**The seller's AI assistant is now a reader of your kit.** If the objection guide lives in a slide deck and the pricing rules in an email thread, an AI assistant briefing a seller before a call will either miss them or get them wrong. Write enablement content as structured, dated, owned documents in a place the field's tools can reach, with the known limits stated explicitly. A limit that isn't written down won't be in the assistant's briefing.

**Call and ticket analysis replaces anecdote.** With connectors (often MCP servers; the Model Context Protocol is an open standard for connecting AI applications to data and tools) to the CRM, call recordings, support and product telemetry, your harness can answer questions that used to take a research project: which objections came up in the last 90 days, in which segment, and how often the deal was lost after them; which competitors were mentioned by name; which problems appear in both support tickets and lost deals. Chapter 19 covers how to permission and check this kind of analysis. The PM's job is to ask the question precisely, demand the call excerpts as evidence and read a sample of them before believing the cluster.

**Draft with AI, own with a human.** AI can draft battlecards from win-loss and call transcripts, draft the monthly field update from the release notes and triage log, propose problem-record merges in the outer loop and code win-loss interviews by decision driver. Each of those needs a named human owner who checks it. Battlecards drafted from transcripts will repeat whatever a competitor's sellers said about you, including things that are false. Merges proposed by a model will sometimes combine two different problems that share vocabulary.

:::ai Keep the evidence attached
Every AI-generated finding about the field (a top objection, a churn reason, a merged problem record) should carry links to the calls, tickets or interviews behind it. If a pattern can't be traced to at least a handful of real conversations, treat it as a hypothesis. Use only tools approved for customer data, and strip personal details before analysis where you can.
:::

## The Beacon case: arming the field for Beacon Agents

:::example Where Beacon starts
Beacon Agents (supervised remediation) launches at Northlight Live. Northlight sells Beacon direct to large enterprises and through MSPs and resellers to the mid-market. The existing field kit was written for the correlation product in 2024. NRR is 118%, but nobody on the product team can say how much of that is expansion and how much churn is hiding underneath. Customer success forwards feature requests in a shared spreadsheet that the PMs read when they have time. You have six weeks before launch.
:::

**The ownership split.** Filling in the table with product marketing, enablement and the sales and CS leaders reveals one gap: nobody owns the known-limits list or roadmap guidance, and sellers have been improvising both. You take them.

**The truth first.** Your known-limits list has three entries the field had been glossing over. Beacon Agents acts only with human approval except for low-risk actions the customer allowlists. Diagnose depends on the connectors a customer has enabled, so a customer with no deploy or change-ticket connector gets weaker root causes. And the per-resolved-incident price applies only to Beacon Agents; the platform fee per environment remains.

**Discovery questions per persona.** You write Implication and Need-payoff questions for each persona in the buying group:

| Persona | Implication question | Need-payoff question |
|---|---|---|
| Marcus (VP of IT Operations) | "When a P1 runs long, what does it cost you in SLA credits, escalations and your team's next week?" | "If diagnosis took minutes instead of most of the incident, where would you put the hours you got back?" |
| Priya (SRE lead) | "How many of your engineers have been paged at night more than once this month? What has that done to retention?" | "What would it mean for your team if the agent handled the low-risk restarts you've already approved?" |
| Sam (security and compliance) | "What happens in your review when a tool asks for production write access?" | "What would you need to see to approve scoped, logged actions with a kill switch?" |
| Leo (MSP operations manager) | "How many customer environments does one of your engineers cover overnight?" | "What would per-customer billing for resolved incidents do to your margin?" |

**The objection guide.** The top objection in the last quarter's call recordings is "your agent will make things worse in production." You write the response around the controls (approval gate, allowlisted autonomy, per-action audit logs) and you tell sellers to bring up the hallucinated root cause that led a customer to roll back the wrong service before the buyer does, then explain what changed in evals and in the approval flow (Chapter 31). Hiding it would fail the first reference call. Two other objections get entries: "our observability vendor already has AIOps," answered with Beacon's tool-agnostic connectors and cited evidence, and "per-resolved-incident pricing is unpredictable," answered with the caps and bands in the pricing guidance. A fourth, "where is your EU AI Act documentation?", is routed to Sam's pack, owned by legal.

**The battlecards.** The first is against the main alternative, humans plus runbooks plus chat. The second is against AIOps features bundled into large observability platforms, written from the buyer's need (tool-agnostic diagnosis across every system they run).

**The ROI tool.** Marcus's ROI tool takes his own incident count, current MTTR and on-call cost, and shows Beacon's effect as a range the customer sets, labeled as an assumption. It contains no benchmark gains. You had a draft with a "typical 40% MTTR reduction" line in it from a marketing slide; you deleted it because nobody could source it.

**The MSP plan.** For Leo's segment, the partner enablement plan adds a technical certification for deploying connectors across many customer tenants, a sandbox tenant, and margin math under per-resolved-incident pricing. Two product gaps surface: the multi-tenant view doesn't roll up resolved-incident counts per customer, and partners can't set per-customer allowlists in bulk. Both go into triage as problem records with partner evidence attached.

**Onboarding and health.** You rewrite the onboarding plan around a Desired Outcome stated in Murphy's form, for example: "Reduce P1 MTTR by an agreed amount within 90 days, while keeping change approval with the on-call lead." The First Strike is the first incident in which the on-call responder accepts Beacon's root cause. Sam's security review becomes a prerequisite scheduled in week one rather than a surprise in week four. With customer success, you add diagnosis acceptance rate, weekly active responders and incidents resolved with Beacon's help per account (the North Star at account level) to the health score, and agree to check the score against the next two quarters of renewals.

**Retention.** You ask for GRR alongside the 118% NRR, by segment. Two mechanisms go on the watch list: the Beacon Classic sunset (Chapter 30) is a GRR risk if migrations stall, and per-resolved-incident pricing moves NRR only as far as Beacon actually resolves incidents.

**The feedback loop.** You replace the spreadsheet with a weekly outer loop. The first triage finds about 40 requests across accounts for "auto-rollback without approval." Asking why, account by account, collapses them into one problem record: slow approvals at 3 a.m. extend incidents (Dana's problem). There are at least three ways to address it: approvals from the on-call engineer's phone, a broader default allowlist, and time-boxed auto-approval for specific actions. The trio adds the problem to the opportunity solution tree and three requesting customers to the interview list. Every requester and account team hears within a week what's being explored and when they'll get a decision.

**The advisory board.** You draft a CAB charter for ten to twelve senior operations leaders, meeting twice a year with virtual sessions between, with one seat for an MSP. The first agenda has two questions: how far customers will let an agent act without a human, and how they want to buy autonomy. There's no roadmap walkthrough.

## Anti-patterns

:::warn The kit nobody checked
Product marketing ships a beautiful deck with a capability that only exists in a beta, and a seller sells it. The PM owns the truth of every enablement asset. If you didn't check it, it isn't ready.
:::

:::warn Enablement as a launch event
One big training on launch day, then nothing for a year while the product, the competition and the price all change. Enablement decays. Run the weekly compete feed, the monthly field update and the quarterly roadmap briefing.
:::

:::warn The feature request vote
Requests are counted, weighted by revenue and ranked, and the top of the list becomes the roadmap. The result is the largest customers' preferred solutions, not the most important problems. Dedupe into problems, link them to opportunities, and treat revenue weight as one input.
:::

:::warn The black hole
CSMs log requests and never hear back, so they stop logging and start escalating to executives. Close the loop at decision and at ship, including "not planned" with a reason.
:::

:::warn Promises in deals
A seller commits a roadmap date to close a quarter, and the PM finds out at renewal. Write roadmap guidance, require PM sign-off for any commitment, and record it on the problem record so it's visible.
:::

:::warn The decorative health score
A health score built from whatever data was available, never checked against renewals. Customers rated green churn and nobody revisits the model. Validate the score every quarter against what actually happened.
:::

:::warn The advisory board as a focus group
The CAB spends a day on a roadmap walkthrough and feature voting. Senior customers stop coming. Use the board for strategic questions you can't answer any other way, and report back on what you did with its advice.
:::

## Checklist

- [ ] The ownership split between PM, product marketing, enablement, sales and CS is written down, and the PM owns the truth of every asset.
- [ ] The sales enablement kit has all eleven components, each with an owner and a last-checked date.
- [ ] Every persona in the buying group has Implication and Need-payoff discovery questions.
- [ ] The objection guide is built from real calls and marks valid objections; battlecards cite sources and refresh on a trigger.
- [ ] The ROI tool uses the buyer's inputs with sourced, conservative assumptions, and roadmap guidance carries "not a commitment."
- [ ] Each partner segment has a plan covering know, sell, deploy and support, plus a feedback channel into triage.
- [ ] Every new customer has a Desired Outcome and a first-value milestone, with time to first value reviewed by cohort at 30, 60 and 90 days.
- [ ] The health score includes product telemetry and an outcome metric, and has been checked against renewals.
- [ ] GRR and NRR are decomposed by segment, cohort, reason and trigger every quarter, and the findings reach the product review.
- [ ] Feature requests are deduped into problem records linked to opportunities, triaged weekly, with the loop closed at decision and at ship.
- [ ] Win-loss interviews run continuously, soon after close, for wins and losses.
- [ ] The customer advisory board has a charter, a strategic agenda and a report-back for every meeting.
- [ ] For AI products, the kit explains failure modes and controls, and the outcome metric ("resolved," "accepted") is defined the same way in the kit, the health score and the contract.
- [ ] AI-generated field analysis links to its source calls and tickets, and runs only in approved tools.

## Key takeaways

- The PM owns the truth of what the field says: the problem, the personas, the limits and the roadmap. Product marketing packages it and enablement delivers it; neither can check it for you.
- The PM-owned parts of the kit are the ones most often skipped: known limits, roadmap guidance, the ROI tool, and the discovery and objection content that says who the product is wrong for.
- Partners need different things by segment, and MSPs need product features (multi-tenant views, per-customer billing, margin that works) as much as training.
- Customer success starts with the customer's Desired Outcome and a first-value milestone carried over from the sale. Time to value, health scores that predict renewals and decomposed GRR and NRR make retention a product outcome.
- The feedback loop records problems, not requests. Dedupe into problem records, link them to opportunities, decide weekly and tell the requester at decision and at ship.
- AI makes the field's evidence searchable and lets you draft battlecards, updates and triage quickly. Keep a named human owner on every asset and the source calls attached to every finding.
