<p class="lead">A presentation is a tool for getting a specific group of people to know, believe or decide something they didn't before the meeting. This chapter treats it that way: plan for one audience and one idea, put the answer first, show data that carries its own uncertainty, deliver it with habits that have evidence behind them, and use AI to build and rehearse without letting it write your judgment or your numbers.</p>

## Why this matters

As a Senior or Principal PM, much of your influence travels through a few high-stakes rooms: a portfolio review where funding gets decided, a launch go or no-go, a customer business review before a renewal. You may spend weeks on the analysis and twenty minutes on the moment that decides whether the analysis matters.

Executive meetings fail in predictable ways. The PM builds the deck in the order they did the work (context, method, findings, recommendation), and the executive interrupts on slide three because they wanted the recommendation on slide one. The data is right but the chart title says "MTTR by month" and leaves the room to find the point. A claim sounds certain when the evidence is a range. Someone asks a hard question about margin, and the PM either bluffs or freezes. Each is a planning or structuring mistake you can fix in advance.

The cost compounds. A decision that slips a quarter because the ask was buried is a quarter of roadmap delay, and a PM who overstates an AI result once loses credibility on every result after it.

Writing gets its own treatment in Chapter 34, and keynotes and live demos at events get theirs in Chapter 26. This chapter is about the meetings in between, where most product decisions get made.

## Planning: audience, objective, one idea

### The audience is the hero

Nancy Duarte's *Resonate* (2010) makes one reframing that changes how you plan every presentation: the audience is the hero of the story and the presenter is the mentor. The hero has to change (adopt a belief, make a decision, take an action), and the mentor's job is to give them what they need to do it. Her earlier *slide:ology* (2008) covers the visual design of slides, and Duarte's "glance test" from that line of work asks whether the audience can grasp a slide's main point in about three seconds; if they can't, they read instead of listening. *Resonate* covers the story underneath, including the movement between "what is" and "what could be" that Chapter 26 applies to keynotes. For internal and customer meetings, the hero idea matters more than the shape.

In B2B product work, the hero is almost never your product. In a quarterly business review the hero is the customer's operations team; in a portfolio review, the executive who must make a funding call and defend it upward; in a sales kickoff, the account executive who has to win next month's deal. Write down who the hero is, what they need to do differently, and what stands in their way. If the honest answer to "who is the hero?" is "my team," you're preparing a status update, and it probably belongs in writing.

### Audience analysis

Most PMs skip audience analysis because they think they know the room. Do it in writing anyway. For each key person, answer:

- **Role in the decision.** Who decides, who advises, who can block, and who will carry the outcome into their own organization?
- **What they know.** Have they read the pre-read? Do they know the product's metrics, or will "diagnosis acceptance rate" need a sentence of explanation?
- **What they believe.** What do they think is true today about this product, this market or you? Where does your message contradict it?
- **What they fear.** Margin, risk, reputation, headcount, a regulator, a customer escalation. The fear usually shapes the questions.
- **What they need to leave with.** A decision, a number they can repeat upward, a reassurance, a task.

The *curse of knowledge* is why this step matters. Elizabeth Newton's 1990 Stanford study asked people to tap out well-known songs and predict how often listeners would recognize them; tappers badly overestimated their listeners' success, because they could hear the melody in their heads and the listeners couldn't. Chip and Dan Heath popularized the lesson for business: experts overestimate how clear their message is. A PM six months inside a launch is the tapper, so test your opening on someone outside the team and ask them to say back your ask.

### The objective: know, feel, do

State the objective as what the audience will **know**, **feel** and **do** at the end. "Inform the leadership team about Q3" is a topic. "The CFO knows inference cost is now the main drag on margin, feels confident we have a plan, and approves the pricing pilot" is an objective. The *do* is the part that matters most for a PM, and it should be specific enough that you can tell afterward whether it happened.

### The Big Idea

Duarte and Cole Nussbaumer Knaflic (in *Storytelling with Data*, 2015) both insist on reducing the presentation to one sentence before building anything, and both call it the Big Idea: a single sentence stating your point of view and what's at stake for the audience. A useful paraphrase of the form:

> **[Audience]** should **[act or believe]** because **[your point of view]**, and if they don't, **[what's at stake]**.

The Big Idea forces a position, an audience and a consequence. "Beacon Agents update" has none of them. "The exec team should approve launching Beacon Agents for allowlisted actions only, because that is where our evidence is strong, and waiting a year hands the category to competitors" passes, and it can be argued with, which is the point.

Knaflic adds the **3-minute story**: if you had only three minutes, what would you say? It's the Big Idea plus the two or three supporting points that matter most. Write it and time it aloud; it's your plan for when the slot gets cut.

### Choosing the mode before the deck

Not every communication needs slides, and the mode is a planning decision. Jeff Bezos's 2017 letter to shareholders described Amazon's practice of replacing slide presentations with narratively structured six-page memos, read silently at the start of the meeting; he wrote that a great memo goes through drafts, review and rewriting and can't be done in a day or two. Edward Tufte's essay *The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint* argues that slide templates fragment reasoning and corrupt statistical analysis; he reports that the board investigating the Columbia shuttle accident found slides inappropriate for engineering reports, and recommends dense written documents with slides reserved for visuals.

You don't have to adopt either position whole. Choose by purpose:

| Purpose | Best mode | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Decision with real trade-offs | Pre-read memo (two to six pages) plus a short verbal frame and discussion | Complex reasoning survives in prose; the meeting is spent on the decision |
| Alignment or inspiration | Talk with visuals | Emotion, energy and a shared moment matter |
| Teaching a concept or a workflow | Talk with visuals or a recorded walkthrough | Words and pictures together help people learn |
| Status | Written update, no meeting | Nobody needs a room to read numbers |
| Customer business review | Short talk on a few charts, with a leave-behind | The buyer needs a story they can repeat internally |

Richard Mayer's research on multimedia learning (summarized in *Multimedia Learning*, 2001) supports the middle rows: people learn better from words and pictures together than from words alone. It isn't an argument for slides full of text that you then read aloud.

:::key Plan on one page before you open a slide tool
Audience and decision maker, objective (know, feel, do), the Big Idea in one sentence, the mode, the 3-minute version and the ask. If you can't fill that page, more slides won't help. The presentation plan template later in this chapter is that page.
:::

### Storyboard before software

Knaflic recommends storyboarding low-tech, on sticky notes, before opening presentation software, because software pulls you into formatting before the argument is settled. The 2026 version of the trap is asking an AI tool for a deck before you know what it should say, and getting twenty polished slides that commit to nothing. Write each slide title as a full sentence on its own note, and check that the titles alone tell the story.

## Structuring for executives

### Answer first

Barbara Minto's *Pyramid Principle*, developed from her work at McKinsey, organizes ideas as a pyramid under one governing point. Her introduction pattern, **Situation, Complication, Question**, sets up the question that is already in the reader's mind so that the top of the pyramid can answer it:

- **Situation**: what the audience already knows and agrees with.
- **Complication**: what changed, or what makes the situation a problem.
- **Question**: the question the complication raises (usually "what should we do?").
- **Answer**: your recommendation, which sits at the top of the pyramid, with grouped supporting arguments beneath it.

The military writing convention of **BLUF** (bottom line up front), which Kabir Sehgal described for business email in HBR in 2016, makes the same demand more bluntly. For a PM, the translation is simple: the first slide or first paragraph states the decision you're asking for, your recommendation and the cost of not deciding. Context comes after.

Duarte's advice for presenting to senior executives adds practical rules. Plan for your slot shrinking to five minutes. Lead with findings, recommendation and call to action, and tell the room the summary comes first and discussion follows. Put everything else in an appendix; her "10% rule" suggests about five summary slides for a fifty-slide appendix. Let the discussion drive, pull appendix slides on request, and if the executive asked a specific question, answer it first.

### Pre-reads

For decisions with real trade-offs, send a pre-read and treat the meeting as the discussion of it. A good pre-read for a product decision has six parts: a one-paragraph ask; the context in Situation, Complication, Question form; the options with their trade-offs; your recommendation; the risks and what evidence would change your mind; and an appendix or FAQ. Chapter 34 covers how to write it.

Two rules make pre-reads work. Don't present the pre-read: walking the room through it rewards those who didn't read it. Restate the ask in two sentences and ask what questions the reading raised. And if your culture doesn't read in advance, borrow the Amazon pattern and start with ten minutes of silent reading.

### Handling interruptions

An executive's interruption is usually a signal: they have what they need from this part, or a concern is blocking the rest. Matt Abrahams, on Stanford Graduate School of Business's *Think Fast, Talk Smart* podcast, suggests three habits: ask for a set amount of time up front ("give me two minutes for the overall picture, then let's discuss"), signpost how many points are coming, and when someone interrupts, paraphrase their point, respond, then resume.

This book adds a rule for product reviews: answer an interrupting question in one sentence, then jump to the slide that addresses it or offer the appendix slide. Don't say "I'll get to that," because their concern now matters more than your sequence. If an interruption shows the room already accepts your argument, skip ahead to the ask.

:::leader For product leaders
Set the norm for your own reviews: pre-reads go out at least a day before, the first slide is the ask, and presenters are told in advance which decision the meeting exists to make. You'll get better presentations from your PMs by changing the meeting format than by coaching individuals.
:::

## Data storytelling

### Explore, then explain

Knaflic's guiding principles for an effective graph start with intent: are you exploring data to find the story, or explaining the story you found? Exploratory charts are for you and your harness. Explanatory charts are for the room, and follow her other principles: create an "aha" moment, don't overcomplicate, remove what isn't essential, make it obvious where to look, use words to make the graph accessible (an action title, axis titles, a source note), and remember that the audience trumps everything else.

The single most useful habit is the **action title**. Every chart's title is a full sentence stating the takeaway. "MTTR by month" names a metric. "MTTR fell after agent-assisted diagnosis rolled out" makes a claim, and the chart then has to support it. If you can't write the action title, you don't know why the chart is there.

### One chart that proves it

For each Big Idea, find the one chart that proves it and design around it, with at most one context chart for the baseline. Highlight the data that carries the point, gray out the rest, and annotate the moment that matters (a launch, a pricing change, an incident) on the chart itself. Chapter 18 covers which product reports matter.

### Findings are separate from recommendations

Knaflic's advice on presenting unwelcome findings is especially useful for PMs. Understand the stakeholders' constraints before you present. Name what the audience expected before you show what you found. And separate the findings from the recommendation, because people can accept the data and still reject the action you propose. When the topic is contentious, offer options and ask for input rather than presenting a single recommendation as the only reasonable reading of the data. Doing so keeps the argument on the decision instead of on whether your chart is right.

### Words of estimative probability

Uncertainty words mean different things to different people. Sherman Kent, writing for the CIA's *Studies in Intelligence* in 1964, described a 1951 intelligence estimate that called a Soviet bloc attack on Yugoslavia a "serious possibility." Kent had meant odds of about 65 to 35 in favor; a policy reader took it as much lower odds; and fellow Board members who had agreed to the wording turned out to have meant odds anywhere from 20 to 80 up to 80 to 20. His proposed fix was to tie each phrase to a numeric range:

| Phrase | Kent's approximate probability |
|---|---|
| Almost certain | About 93% (give or take about 6%) |
| Probable | About 75% (give or take about 12%) |
| Chances about even | About 50% (give or take about 10%) |
| Probably not | About 30% (give or take about 10%) |
| Almost certainly not | About 7% (give or take about 5%) |

For a product deck, any word like "likely" or "strong chance" carries a number or range, or the slide states its scale. Executives will repeat your word, not your caveat, so pick the word you'd be comfortable hearing back in a board meeting.

## Delivery and nonverbal communication

### What Mehrabian actually found

A widely repeated claim in presentation training is that communication is 7% words, 38% tone of voice and 55% facial expression. It comes from Albert Mehrabian's experiments on how people communicate liking or disliking, summarized in his book *Silent Messages* (1981), and it's routinely misapplied. Mehrabian's own account says the split applies only when a speaker is communicating feelings or attitudes. It doesn't mean words carry 7% of a presentation's meaning. If it did, you could understand a talk in a language you don't speak.

What the research does support is narrower: when words and tone conflict about a feeling, listeners tend to trust the tone and face. If you say "I'm confident in this plan" in a flat voice while looking at your notes, the room will believe your delivery and not your sentence. The fix is to only say you're confident when you are, and to say exactly what you're uncertain about when you aren't.

### Power poses and nerves

The second piece of popular advice to drop is the "power pose." A widely publicized 2010 study by Dana Carney, Amy Cuddy and Andy Yap reported that expansive "power poses" affected hormones and behavior. In 2016 Carney, its first author, stated that she doesn't believe power pose effects are real, citing the small sample, flexible analysis and failed replications, including a 2015 replication by Ranehill and colleagues and a p-curve analysis by Simmons and Simonsohn. Cuddy and colleagues have argued separately for effects on how powerful people feel; that claim remains contested. Don't use poses as a hormone or performance hack.

A better-supported technique for nerves is reappraisal. Alison Wood Brooks, in a 2014 paper in the *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, found across singing, public-speaking and math tasks that people who reframed their anxiety as excitement (for example by saying "I am excited") felt and performed better than people who tried to calm down. It's a single paper whose replication record this chapter doesn't assess, but the advice costs nothing: call the racing heart before a big review readiness instead of fighting it.

### Craft habits

Beyond the research, a set of habits is worth teaching as practitioner advice rather than science:

- **Pause after the key sentence.** Say the ask, then stop. Silence gives the decision maker room to respond.
- **Slow down on numbers.** State the number, the unit and the comparison, then pause before moving on.
- **Look at the decision maker when you state the ask.** Look at the questioner when you answer a question.
- **Keep still while listening to pushback.** Nodding along while someone attacks your plan reads as agreement.
- **Don't read your slides.** The room reads faster than you speak.

## Virtual and recorded presentations

### Video calls

Many product reviews are now hybrid or virtual, and video is a different medium. Jeremy Bailenson's 2021 paper "Nonverbal overload," in *Technology, Mind, and Behavior*, argues that video calls tire people because they overload the nonverbal channel and impose unusual patterns of communication. Design around that. Keep segments short and change mode often. Put the ask in writing in the chat or on the slide. Hide your self-view so you watch the audience. Let listeners keep cameras off. Name the person you want a response from, because silence on a call is ambiguous. Test camera, audio, screen share and captions before the meeting starts.

### Recorded and async video

Async updates and recorded walkthroughs are routine for PMs: a weekly update to engineering, a recorded demo for a customer who missed the call. These norms are practitioner habits rather than findings, and they follow from how people watch recordings: skimming, sped up, often muted.

- Put the ask or the headline in the first 30 seconds.
- Keep it short. TED's guidance to its speakers notes that strong talks can run 10 to 12 minutes and that many are only 6 to 8, under strict time limits and with coaching beforehand. Your product update has less to say than a TED talk.
- Add chapter markers and captions.
- Post a written summary under the video, with the ask, the key numbers and links to sources, so an executive can get the point without pressing play.
- Look into the camera lens, not at the faces on screen, when you state the ask, and frame yourself at eye level with light in front of you.
- Re-record a rambling first take. A recording is optional viewing, and a slow start is where people stop watching.

## Q&A, pushback and executive presence

### Prepare for the hardest questions

The decision is often made in the Q&A. List the ten hardest questions you could be asked, and for each write the likely asker, a one-sentence answer, the evidence behind it, the appendix slide that shows it, and who will follow up if you don't have the answer. Rehearse them aloud with someone playing the skeptic. The harness makes a good murder board: ask it to play the CFO, the security reviewer and the peer PM who disagrees with you.

This book suggests a simple answer pattern for pushback, as a heuristic rather than a sourced framework:

1. **Acknowledge**: restate the concern in the asker's terms, so they know you heard it.
2. **Answer**: one sentence.
3. **Anchor**: one piece of evidence, or the appendix slide.
4. **Advance**: return to the ask, or state what would change your recommendation.

### Saying "I don't know"

Lloyd Minor, talking about communicating uncertain science with Matt Abrahams on Stanford's *Think Fast, Talk Smart* in 2022, made the case plainly: when you don't know, say so; admitting a gap does less damage than reversing a confident claim later; follow up with the answer; and be open when your view changes. For a PM in an executive room, "I don't know; I'll have it by Thursday" is a strong answer when it's followed by the answer on Thursday. A guess presented as a fact is the weakest answer, because the next time you're right, nobody will be sure.

### Executive presence

Executive presence is the vague quality managers cite when they say someone isn't ready for the next level. The Center for Talent Innovation (now Coqual), in the research behind Sylvia Ann Hewlett's book *Executive Presence*, surveyed 268 senior executives and nearly 4,000 college-graduate professionals in 2012. Respondents described executive presence as gravitas, communication and appearance: 67% of senior executives named gravitas as the core, 28% communication and 5% appearance, and they said executive presence accounted for 26% of what it takes to get the next promotion. Gravitas meant behaviors such as grace under fire, decisiveness, integrity and speaking truth to power; communication meant speaking well, commanding a room and reading an audience.

The research also found a problem. People of color were more likely than white respondents to feel held to a stricter code of executive presence (56% against 31%), and the report notes that feedback on presence is often contradictory, with 81% saying they were unclear how to act on it. "Presence" can be a cultural filter as much as a skill. Teach and look for the substantive parts, and be suspicious of feedback that amounts to "be more like the people already in the room."

For a PM, the substantive parts are concrete: know your numbers without looking, own your recommendation and its risks, stay specific when challenged, say what you don't know and when you'll know it, and change your mind in public when the evidence warrants it.

:::onramp If presenting to executives is new to you
Before your first big review, do three things. Ask your manager what decision the meeting exists to make and who makes it. Rewrite your first slide so it states that decision and your recommendation. And rehearse your answer to the one question you most hope nobody asks.
:::

## How to apply it

Use this method when a decision, a renewal or a launch depends on the outcome. For a routine meeting, steps 1, 3 and 9 are enough.

**Plan (one to two weeks out)**

1. **Name the decision and the decision maker.** If nobody can say what the meeting exists to decide, propose it.
2. **Write the presentation plan**: audience analysis, objective, Big Idea, mode, slot and ask.
3. **Write the 3-minute story and the 5-minute cut**, and time both aloud.
4. **Gather the evidence through the harness**, pulling reports, evals and incident reviews through connectors, with a source for every number.

**Build (one week out)**

5. **Storyboard the titles** as full-sentence action titles, and check that the titles alone tell the story.
6. **Structure answer first**: ask and recommendation, Situation, Complication, Question, three arguments, risks and options. Everything else goes to the appendix.
7. **Design the one chart** for each argument, with an action title, highlighting, annotation and its uncertainty (range, sample size, method).
8. **Write the pre-read** if the decision has real trade-offs, and send it at least a day ahead.
9. **Audit every number.** Compare each figure on every slide with its source, and put the source in the speaker notes.

**Rehearse (two to three days out)**

10. **Prepare the Q&A sheet.** Ten hardest questions, each with asker, one-sentence answer, evidence, appendix slide and follow-up owner.
11. **Run the murder board.** Rehearse with a colleague or the harness playing the hardest people in the room.
12. **Rehearse the delivery**: a timed full run, a 5-minute run, and a recorded run reviewed for pace, filler words and slide reading. Learn the first and last 60 seconds by heart.
13. **Check the setup**, including a recorded fallback for any live demo.

**Deliver and follow up**

14. **Open with the ask**, and say the summary comes first and discussion follows.
15. **Let the room steer**: answer interruptions in one sentence, jump to the slide, return to the ask.
16. **Close on the decision**: what was decided, who owns the next steps, by when.
17. **Send the follow-up within a day**, including the answers you promised.

## The AI lens

### Presenting AI-powered products honestly

AI products are right some of the time, so the honest description of their quality is a distribution. The temptation is to show the best number and the best demo, which earns a launch and loses trust at the first failure nobody was warned about. Chapter 31 covers how to measure AI quality; these are the rules for presenting it.

- **Show the range and the sample size.** Anthropic's November 2024 research on model evaluation recommends reporting the standard error and a 95% confidence interval for eval scores, and comparing models with paired differences. The executive translation: never show a single accuracy number without its range and how many cases it came from, and never claim an improvement smaller than the noise.
- **Show the denominator.** "The agent resolved hundreds of incidents" means nothing without how many it attempted, and how many it got wrong.
- **Show failures alongside wins.** Curated good outputs mislead even when every example is real. Show representative failures, and what the product does when it's wrong.
- **Separate offline evals from production outcomes.** An eval score and a customer's MTTR are different evidence. Label which is which.
- **Use a model card for executives.** Margaret Mitchell and colleagues' *Model Cards for Model Reporting* (2019) proposed documenting a model's intended use, its performance across relevant groups and its evaluation procedures. One slide can do the same job for an exec audience: what the product is for, what it isn't for, how it was tested, how it performs by segment and its known failure modes.
- **Use estimative language with numbers.** "The agent is usually right" needs Kent's discipline: say the rate and the range.
- **Don't anthropomorphize.** Avoid "the AI understands" or "the agent knows." Say what the system does: retrieves, ranks, proposes, acts after approval.
- **Label demos.** Say when a demo is scripted or uses prepared data, and keep a recorded fallback. Chapter 26 covers demos in depth.

### Doing the work with AI

Slide production is now cheap. Anthropic added file creation, including PowerPoint decks, to Claude in September 2025 (with a warning in the announcement that the feature's internet access may put data at risk), and Claude for Microsoft 365 builds slides in an organization's own templates and masters. Microsoft's Copilot in PowerPoint creates decks from a prompt or a document and applies an organization's brand kit, and Google's Gemini in Slides generates slides in the current theme from prompts and referenced files. Features change fast, so learn the workflow rather than the menus.

Polish no longer signals effort. Executives will see more decks that look finished and say nothing, and the scarce skills become choosing the Big Idea, making the cut and guaranteeing every number. A workflow that keeps those with you:

1. The harness drafts a presentation plan from your brief and the evidence it pulls through connectors.
2. You write the Big Idea and the ask yourself, and you choose between options if you asked for several.
3. The harness drafts the storyline as action titles. You cut.
4. The harness builds slides in the corporate template.
5. You verify every number against its source; the harness can list mismatches, but you check them.
6. You rehearse with AI. Tools such as PowerPoint's Speaker Coach report on pace, pitch, filler words, wordiness and reading slide text, and a harness can play personas for Q&A.
7. The harness drafts the follow-up from your notes, and you correct it before it goes out.

Three risks need active management. Narratives come out fluent and generic, so ask whether a competitor's PM could have written your AI-drafted Big Idea. Numbers get invented and charts get mis-scaled, so the audit is mandatory. And confidential roadmaps, financials and customer data go only into tools your company has approved for them.

:::ai The one-line rule
Let AI build the slides and play the skeptic. Keep the Big Idea, the cut and the numbers as your own work.
:::

## The Beacon case: the Beacon Agents launch decision

:::example The situation
Northlight's executive team will decide in two weeks whether to launch Beacon Agents (supervised remediation) at Northlight Live. You have a 30-minute slot in the portfolio review. The context: Beacon has about $48M in ARR, growing 38% a year, with net revenue retention of 118%. Gross margin fell from 81% to 72% after agentic features launched, because of inference cost. And a customer recently rolled back the wrong service after Beacon proposed a hallucinated root cause.
:::

**The plan.** You start with the presentation plan. The CEO decides; the CFO and the head of security can each block. Your audience analysis finds three fears: margin for the CFO, a repeat of the hallucinated root cause for security, and, for the CEO, losing the category while Northlight waits. Your Big Idea: *The exec team should approve launching Beacon Agents at Northlight Live for allowlisted, low-risk actions only, because that's where our evidence and guardrails are strong, and waiting a year cedes the category while the margin problem stays unsolved either way.*

**The structure.** Slide one is the ask: approve a scoped launch, with broader autonomy deferred to the first half of next year. Then the Situation (customers grow and stay, with NRR at 118%), the Complication (margin pressure from inference cost and the hallucinated root cause) and the Question (launch now or wait). Three arguments follow, each with one chart: diagnosis acceptance rate, with its interval and sample size; the guardrail story (new evals and the approval gate since the hallucinated root cause); and the margin plan, tied to the move from per-environment to per-resolved-incident pricing (Chapter 15). The competitive analysis and eval detail go to the appendix. A four-page pre-read goes out two days ahead.

**Presenting the failure.** The hallucinated root cause gets its own slide in the main flow: what happened, the blast radius, why the guardrail missed it, what changed, and the acceptance rate with its confidence interval before and after the fix. The head of security already knows the story, and burying it in the appendix would cost you the room.

**Rehearsal.** You ask the harness, connected to the eval dashboard and the incident review, to play the CFO, the head of security and the Observe group PM, who wants Beacon built on Observe data. The CFO persona asks whether per-resolved-incident pricing recovers margin at current inference cost. You don't know, so the Q&A sheet records the honest answer: the pricing pilot is designed to find out, with results at the next quarterly review. The number audit catches two figures that don't match the eval report, one rounded wrongly and one from an earlier run.

**The meeting.** Your slot is cut to fifteen minutes. On slide two, the CEO interrupts: "Is this safe?" You answer in one sentence ("Only allowlisted low-risk actions qualify, every step needs human approval, and every action is logged"), jump to the guardrail slide, take two questions from security, and return to the ask. The decision is a conditional yes: launch with the allowlist, and come back with pricing-pilot results before any expansion of autonomy. Your follow-up goes out that afternoon.

**The customer version.** At a quarterly business review with Marcus, the VP of IT Operations, the hero is his organization. The Big Idea is about his team's MTTR and on-call load, from his own data, shown as ranges with incident counts. The guardrail slide stays; the margin slide goes, because margin is Northlight's problem.


## Anti-patterns

:::warn The work-order deck
Context, method, data, findings and, on slide fourteen, the recommendation. The deck follows the order in which you did the work, and the executive asks for the answer on slide three. Put the ask and recommendation first; move the method to the appendix.
:::

:::warn The metric-name title
"Weekly active responders, Q1 to Q3." The chart is correct and the room has to guess why it's there. Every chart title is a full-sentence takeaway that the chart supports.
:::

:::warn The naked accuracy number
"The agent is 92% accurate." On what, how many cases, with what range, measured offline or in production? A single AI-quality number without its interval and sample size is a claim nobody can check, and it will be repeated upward without its caveats.
:::

:::warn The cherry-picked demo
Five perfect outputs, shown live, with no mention of how they were chosen. When a customer hits the first failure, every claim from the demo is discounted. Show representative failures and what the product does when it's wrong.
:::

:::warn The 7-38-55 lecture
Delivery training that teaches words carry 7% of meaning, plus a power pose before you walk in. Neither holds up; teach what the evidence supports instead.
:::

:::warn The polished, empty AI deck
Twenty well-designed slides generated from a prompt, with no Big Idea, no ask and two numbers nobody can trace. Write the Big Idea and the ask yourself, and audit every number before the deck leaves your hands.
:::

## Checklist

- [ ] The decision and decision maker are named, and a presentation plan has audience analysis and a know, feel and do objective.
- [ ] The Big Idea is one sentence with a position, an audience and a stake, and the mode was chosen on purpose.
- [ ] The 3-minute story and the 5-minute cut are written and rehearsed.
- [ ] The first slide or paragraph states the ask, the recommendation and the cost of not deciding.
- [ ] Every chart has a full-sentence action title that its data supports.
- [ ] Every number has a source in the speaker notes, and the deck has been audited against sources.
- [ ] Probability words carry numbers or ranges; AI results show range, sample size and failure modes.
- [ ] Findings are separated from recommendations, and options are ready if the room accepts the data but rejects the action.
- [ ] The Q&A prep sheet covers ten hard questions, including the one you most hope nobody asks.
- [ ] A murder board (human or AI) has been run against the deck.
- [ ] Virtual setup, demo fallback and captions are tested.
- [ ] Confidential material went only into approved AI tools.
- [ ] A follow-up with decisions, owners and dates goes out within a day.

## Key takeaways

- Name the decision and decision maker, analyze the audience and write the Big Idea in one sentence before opening any slide tool.
- Lead with the ask and recommendation, support it with Situation, Complication, Question and three arguments, plan for a five-minute cut, and use pre-reads for real trade-offs.
- Give every chart an action title, and every number its source, range and sample size. Words like "likely" need numbers.
- The 7-38-55 split applies only to conveying feelings, and power poses don't hold up. Reframing nerves as excitement and keeping your tone consistent with your words are better bets.
- Prepare for Q&A as carefully as for the talk, and treat "I don't know; I'll have it by Thursday" as a strong answer when Thursday's answer arrives.
- Present AI products with their ranges, denominators and failure modes alongside the wins, and label demos honestly.
- AI can build the slides, audit the numbers and play the skeptic in rehearsal. The Big Idea, the cut and the integrity of every number stay with you.
